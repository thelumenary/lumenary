# Viewpoints: many ways of seeing one mathematics

Mathematics is one subject, but mathematicians see it differently. Given the same object — a group, a function, a space, a proof — a topologist asks what survives continuous deformation, a geometer asks about distance, curvature and symmetry, an analyst asks about limits and estimates, an algebraist looks for the operations and their structure, a number theorist for integrality and primes, a combinatorialist for what can be counted or forced, a probabilist for typical behaviour, a logician for what can be defined, proved or decided, a computer scientist for algorithms, and a physicist for where the idea lives in nature. Set theory and category theory are powerful common languages for all of these, and Lumenary Math teaches both in depth — but a curriculum that saw higher mathematics only through those two languages would miss much of what makes it alive.

## What the curriculum does

- **Every lesson from early undergraduate onwards has a `viewpoints` section** of `<Viewpoint lens="…">` blocks. The lesson checks enforce at least two lenses at UG 1 and at least three at UG 2, graduate and research level, of which at least one (UG 1) or two (higher) must come from outside set theory, category theory and logic. Lenses: topology, geometry, analysis, algebra, number theory, combinatorics, probability, logic, computation, physics, sets, categories.
- **Geometry and topology are a traced thread** (`geometry-topology-progression.md`), with their own topics at every level from K–2 to research and the specialisation pathway `geometry-topology`.
- **Specialisation pathways for different mathematical temperaments**: `geometry-topology`, `analysis-dynamics`, `algebra-number-theory` and `mathematical-physics`, alongside the general pathways (`math-major`, `graduate-prep`, `research-prep`, …).
- **The research column represents many fields**: low-dimensional topology, geometric group theory, symplectic and Kähler geometry, stable homotopy theory, geometric analysis, arithmetic geometry and the Langlands programme, random geometry, gauge theory, extremal and additive combinatorics — as well as higher and derived categories, homotopy type theory and large cardinals.

## How a viewpoint is written

A viewpoint is mathematics, not a slogan. It names a precise statement, example or theorem (with its hypotheses), explains what that lens notices that others miss, and links the relevant topics. Analogies are labelled as analogies. A few illustrations of the standard we aim for:

| Topic | Lens | A viewpoint worth writing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linear algebra | Topology | GL(n, ℝ) has two path components, distinguished by the sign of the determinant: orientation is topological. |
| Groups | Geometry | Every group is the symmetry group of something (a Cayley graph); finitely generated groups have a large-scale geometry. |
| Galois theory | Topology | The Galois correspondence has a twin: connected covering spaces of a nice space correspond to subgroups of its fundamental group. |
| The Yoneda lemma | Geometry | In algebraic geometry a scheme is determined by its functor of points — Yoneda made geometric. |
| Measure theory | Probability | A probability space is a measure space of total mass 1; independence becomes product measure. |
| Real analysis | Topology | Continuity is about open sets; compactness turns local information into global bounds. |
| Fourier analysis | Algebra | Characters of the circle group; Pontryagin duality for locally compact abelian groups. |
| Incompleteness | Computation | No algorithm decides all arithmetic truths; incompleteness and the halting problem are two faces of diagonalisation. |
| Euler characteristic | Analysis | Gauss–Bonnet: ∫K dA = 2πχ — an integral of curvature computes a topological invariant. |

## Why this matters to the Lumenary's values

Intellectual humility includes recognising that one's own preferred language is not the only way to see the truth. Giving learners many viewpoints respects different mathematical temperaments and helps them recognise structure across fields — the habit behind many of the deepest discoveries in mathematics.
