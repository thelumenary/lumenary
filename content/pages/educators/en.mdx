## Assessment suggestions

The Lumenary deliberately avoids scores, streaks and rankings for learners. For classroom assessment we suggest **formative**, conversation-rich approaches:

- **Explain-it-back.** Ask a student to explain *why* a result holds — for example why $(-a)(-b) = ab$ — rather than to recite it. Listen for the use of definitions and properties.
- **Example, non-example, counterexample.** For any definition (function, injective, subset), ask students to produce one example, one non-example, and — for a false general claim — a counterexample.
- **Evidence or proof?** Show a computation (a table of $n^2 + n + 41$, a check of all subsets of a small set) and ask what it does and does not establish.
- **Misconception probes.** Each lesson lists common misconceptions; turn each into a true-or-false question and ask students to justify their answer.
- **Hint-aware practice.** The online exercises reveal hints one at a time and allow unlimited attempts. Encourage students to note which hint unlocked their thinking.

## Classroom activities

- **Market sort (ages 5–8).** Bring a basket of real or paper fruit. Groups invent a sorting rule, sort the basket, then swap baskets and guess each other's rule. Include an empty box and discuss whether it is a collection.
- **Human Venn diagram (ages 8–12).** Lay two hoops on the floor. Students stand in the region matching two properties (plays an instrument / has a younger sibling). Name each region with set notation.
- **Number-line walk (ages 11–14).** Tape a number line on the floor. Students act out $a + b$ and "take the opposite". Then ask: what does multiplying by $-1$ do to every position at once?
- **Arrow-diagram relay (ages 12–15).** Teams draw arrow diagrams on the board and challenge each other: is it a function? injective? surjective? Introduce the pigeonhole principle with four students and three chairs.
- **Function machines (ages 14–18).** Pairs secretly choose two machines ("add 3", "double"). The class feeds in numbers and must determine both machines — and their order.
- **Clock arithmetic (any age).** Use a paper clock with a removable face to explore 5-, 7- and 12-hour clocks, and hunt for multiplicative partners.

## Using the lessons

Each lesson follows a common architecture — motivation, intuition, definitions, worked examples, proofs, formal and computational sections, exercises, misconceptions, Wonder Trails, connections and history — adapted to the learner's age. The **lens** control at the top of every lesson highlights one mode of reasoning at a time, which can help when teaching a particular skill.

Wonder Trails are optional by design. They work well as extension tasks for curious students — but should never be required or graded.
