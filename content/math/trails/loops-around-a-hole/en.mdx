The number of times a closed path goes around a point is its **winding number**. If the point is $a$ and the path is traced by a complex number $z(t)$, the winding number is the total change in the argument of $z - a$, divided by $2\pi$. It is always a whole number, because the path ends where it began.

Here is the key fact: if you deform a loop continuously without ever passing through $a$, its winding number cannot jump — a whole number that changes continuously cannot change at all. So loops in the plane with a hole are sorted by their winding number, and two loops can be deformed into each other exactly when their winding numbers agree. In the language of topology, the **fundamental group** of the plane with one point removed is $\mathbb Z$.

Complex analysis computes the same number with an integral:

$$
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint \frac{dz}{z - a} = \text{winding number}.
$$

Winding numbers even give a proof sketch of the fundamental theorem of algebra. A polynomial $z^n + \dots$ maps a very large circle to a loop that winds $n$ times around $0$, while a tiny circle near the origin maps to a loop that winds $0$ times around $0$ unless $p(0) = 0$. Shrinking the circle changes the winding number from $n$ to $0$, which can only happen if the image loop passes through $0$ somewhere — that is, if the polynomial has a root. (Turning this sketch into a proof takes care; see the fundamental-group lesson.)

With **two** holes the story changes: loops are no longer classified by two winding numbers, because going around one hole and then the other is different from the reverse order. The fundamental group becomes non-commutative.
