Addition and multiplication of integers look alike: each combines two integers into a third, each is associative, and each has a "do nothing" number — $0$ for addition, $1$ for multiplication. Mathematicians call such a number an **identity**.

The difference is **inverses**. For addition, every integer $a$ has a partner $-a$ with $a + (-a) = 0$. For multiplication, a partner $b$ with $ab = 1$ exists only for $a = 1$ and $a = -1$: if $|a| \geq 2$ and $b \neq 0$, then $|ab| \geq 2$.

Mathematicians give names to these patterns:

- A set with an associative operation and an identity is a **monoid**. The integers under multiplication form a monoid.
- A monoid in which **every** element has an inverse is a **group**. The integers under addition form a group; under multiplication they do not.
- The integers with both operations, linked by distributivity, form a **ring**.

When we move to the rational numbers, every *non-zero* number gains a multiplicative inverse ($\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{3}{2} = 1$), and the non-zero rationals form a group under multiplication. Zero never has a multiplicative inverse, in any ring with $0 \neq 1$: $0 \cdot b = 0$ for every $b$.
