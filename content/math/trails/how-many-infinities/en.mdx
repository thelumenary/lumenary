Two sets have the **same size** — the same **cardinality** — when their elements can be paired off one-to-one, by a bijection. For finite sets this is just counting. For infinite sets it gives surprises: the even numbers $0, 2, 4, \dots$ can be paired with all counting numbers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ by $n \mapsto 2n$, so there are "as many" even numbers as counting numbers. A set that can be listed as $s_1, s_2, s_3, \dots$ is called **countable**.

Are all infinite sets countable? In 1891 Georg Cantor gave a famous argument that the answer is **no**.

Suppose someone hands us a list $s_1, s_2, s_3, \dots$ of infinite 0–1 sequences. Build a new sequence $d$ by flipping the diagonal: let the $n$-th digit of $d$ be the opposite of the $n$-th digit of $s_n$. Then $d$ differs from $s_1$ in position $1$, from $s_2$ in position $2$, and in general from $s_n$ in position $n$. So $d$ is **not anywhere on the list**.

Since *every* list misses some sequence, the set of all infinite 0–1 sequences is **uncountable**: it is a strictly bigger infinity than the counting numbers.

<Note>

The experiment shows the mechanism on a finite part of a list. It illustrates the idea; the argument above — which works for any list whatsoever — is the proof.

</Note>

The same diagonal idea reappears across mathematics and computer science: in showing that the real numbers are uncountable, in Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and in Turing's proof that no program can decide whether every program halts.
