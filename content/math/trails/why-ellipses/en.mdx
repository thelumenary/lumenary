Johannes Kepler found the ellipses in 1609, by fitting Tycho Brahe's careful observations of Mars. He did not know *why*. Isaac Newton explained it in the *Principia* (1687): if the force pulling a planet towards the Sun has strength proportional to $1/r^2$, then the planet's path is a conic section with the Sun at a focus. In polar coordinates centred on the Sun,

$$
r = \frac{\ell}{1 + e\cos\theta},
$$

where $e$ is the **eccentricity**: $e = 0$ gives a circle, $0 < e < 1$ an ellipse, $e = 1$ a parabola and $e > 1$ a hyperbola. Which one occurs depends on the planet's energy. Comets on long ellipses return; an object with too much energy passes once along a hyperbola and leaves — as the interstellar visitor 'Oumuamua did in 2017.

Ellipses are special. In 1873 Joseph Bertrand proved that among forces that depend only on distance, only two make *every* bounded orbit close up: the inverse-square law and a spring-like force proportional to $r$. With any other law, orbits trace rosettes.

Real orbits are not perfect ellipses: the other planets tug, and the ellipse of Mercury slowly turns. Almost all of that turning is explained by Newton's law, but an extra $43$ arcseconds per century was explained only in 1915, by Einstein's general relativity.
