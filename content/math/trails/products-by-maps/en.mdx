The product $A \times B$ comes with two **projections**, $\pi_1(a, b) = a$ and $\pi_2(a, b) = b$. Given any set $Z$ and any functions $f : Z \to A$ and $g : Z \to B$, there is a function

$$
\langle f, g \rangle : Z \to A \times B, \qquad z \mapsto \big(f(z), g(z)\big),
$$

with $\pi_1 \circ \langle f, g \rangle = f$ and $\pi_2 \circ \langle f, g \rangle = g$. The experiment shows more: it is the **only** such function. Any $h$ with $\pi_1 \circ h = f$ and $\pi_2 \circ h = g$ must send $z$ to the pair with first coordinate $f(z)$ and second coordinate $g(z)$ — that is, $h = \langle f, g \rangle$.

This "exists and is unique" statement is called the **universal property of the product**. It describes $A \times B$ entirely through its relationships with all other sets, without mentioning ordered pairs at all. In any category, an object with two arrows satisfying this property is called a **product** of $A$ and $B$.

The universal property pins the product down **up to isomorphism**: any two products of $A$ and $B$ are related by a unique isomorphism compatible with the projections — though they need not be literally equal. In the category of subsets of a set ordered by $\subseteq$, the product of $A$ and $B$ turns out to be $A \cap B$; in other categories products look different again. Universal properties like this one are the starting point for **limits**, **adjunctions** and the **Yoneda lemma**.
