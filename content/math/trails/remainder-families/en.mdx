Dividing by $k$ splits the integers into exactly $k$ **families** — the integers with remainder $0$, with remainder $1$, …, with remainder $k - 1$. Every integer belongs to exactly one family. A splitting like this, into non-overlapping pieces that together cover everything, is called a **partition**.

The rule "same remainder" has three properties:

- every integer is related to itself (**reflexive**);
- if $a$ is related to $b$, then $b$ is related to $a$ (**symmetric**);
- if $a$ is related to $b$ and $b$ to $c$, then $a$ is related to $c$ (**transitive**).

A relation with these properties is an **equivalence relation**, and its families are called **equivalence classes**. Every equivalence relation gives a partition, and every partition comes from one.

Now the surprise from the experiment. If $a \equiv a'$ and $b \equiv b' \pmod{k}$, then $a + b \equiv a' + b'$. *Why?* Because $a - a'$ and $b - b'$ are multiples of $k$, so their sum $(a + b) - (a' + b')$ is too. The family of a sum depends only on the families. The same is true for products.

That means we can add and multiply **the families themselves**. The families become the elements of a new number system, written $\Z/k\Z$ — a **quotient** of $\Z$. Building new structures by "gluing together" equivalent elements, and checking that the operations are **well defined**, is one of the most-used constructions in all of algebra.
