On a clock with $k$ hours, counting **wraps around**: after $k - 1$ comes $0$ again. Two numbers land on the same position exactly when they have the same remainder after division by $k$. Mathematicians write

$$
a \equiv b \pmod{k}
$$

("$a$ is **congruent** to $b$ modulo $k$") when $a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder. So $9 + 5 = 14 \equiv 2 \pmod{12}$, and $9 + 29 = 38 \equiv 2 \pmod{12}$ as well.

<Lean decl="Lumenary.Modular.clock_wraps" />

Clock arithmetic has its own multiplication too, and something remarkable happens. On a 7-hour clock, $3 \cdot 5 = 15 \equiv 1$, so $5$ acts like "one third"! On a 4-hour clock, though, $2$ has no partner at all: $2 \cdot x$ is always $0$ or $2$.

The pattern you can see in the experiment is a theorem: **$a$ has a multiplicative partner modulo $k$ exactly when $a$ and $k$ have no common factor except $1$.** The table is evidence for small $k$; the proof, which uses Bézout's identity, comes in number theory. When $k$ is prime, every non-zero number has a partner — so arithmetic on a prime clock behaves like arithmetic with fractions. These number systems are called $\Z/k\Z$, and for prime $k$ they are examples of **finite fields**.

<Lean decl="Lumenary.Modular.inverses_mod_five" />

Modular arithmetic is not only a curiosity. It underlies calendars and check digits on identity numbers, and it is the foundation of much of modern cryptography.
