Every path of arrows in the experiment composed to a single arrow, the identity arrows changed nothing, and the two ways of bracketing $h \circ g \circ f$ always agreed. These are exactly the laws that define a **category**:

- a collection of **objects**;
- for any two objects $X, Y$, a collection of **arrows** (or **morphisms**) $X \to Y$;
- a **composition** of arrows $X \to Y$ and $Y \to Z$ into an arrow $X \to Z$;
- an **identity** arrow on every object;

such that composition is **associative** and identities are **neutral**.

Here is that definition in Lean. Our version writes composition in "diagrammatic" order — first the left arrow, then the right — the same convention Mathlib uses for its `≫` notation:

Sets with functions form a category. So do groups with group homomorphisms, vector spaces with linear maps, and topological spaces with continuous maps. Even a single monoid is a category, with **one** object whose arrows are the monoid's elements:

<Lean decl="Lumenary.Category.monoidAsCategory" />

Once many areas of mathematics are seen as categories, we can compare *them* with arrows too. A structure-preserving map between categories is called a **functor**. Category theory, introduced by Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane in 1945, is the study of these patterns — and it has become a common language for algebra, topology, logic and the theory of programming languages.
