A square has exactly **eight** symmetries: turns through $0^\circ$, $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$ and $270^\circ$, and flips in its two diagonals and its two mid-lines. These moves have four properties:

- doing one symmetry and then another always gives a symmetry;
- "do nothing" is a symmetry;
- every symmetry can be undone by another symmetry;
- for three moves, it does not matter how you group them: $(a \text{ then } b) \text{ then } c$ equals $a \text{ then } (b \text{ then } c)$.

Any collection of moves with these four properties is called a **group**. The symmetries of the square form the dihedral group of order $8$. Order matters: a quarter turn followed by a flip is usually not the same as the flip followed by the quarter turn, so this group is **not commutative**. A regular polygon with $n$ sides has $2n$ symmetries; a circle has infinitely many, one for every angle of turning.

Groups are the mathematics of symmetry everywhere. There are exactly $230$ space groups describing the possible symmetries of crystals; Évariste Galois used groups to explain why there is no formula for the roots of a general polynomial of degree five; and physicists classify elementary particles by the symmetry groups of the laws they obey. Groups of symmetries that vary continuously, like turning by any angle, are **Lie groups**, named after Sophus Lie.
