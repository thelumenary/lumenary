<Section id="ideas" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="collection">

A **collection** is a group of things that we think about together. The things in a collection are called its **members**.

</Definition>

When something is in a collection, we say it **belongs** to the collection. The banana belongs to the collection of yellow things. The apple does not.

<Definition term="the same collection">

Two collections are **the same** when they have exactly the same members. It does not matter what order the members are in.

</Definition>

<Definition term="the empty collection">

A collection with **no members at all** is called the **empty collection**. The box of blue things is empty — but it is still a collection!

</Definition>

<Narrator who="gideon">

I'm Gideon. I like to be *precise*. A thing either belongs to a collection, or it does not. There is no "a little bit in". Ask the question, and the answer is yes or no.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="counting" kind="human" title="Why counting always gives the same answer">

When we count, we match each thing with exactly one counting word: *one, two, three, four*. Each thing gets one word, and no word is used twice.

If you start somewhere else, you make a different matching — but you still use up the same counting words, because there are just as many things as before. Nothing was added and nothing was taken away.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So the number of members belongs to the *collection*, not to the way we counted it. That's why everybody who counts carefully gets the same answer.

</Narrator>

<ForGrownUps title="what this idea becomes">

The idea that "counting is matching" is the seed of a deep idea in mathematics: two collections have the same number of members exactly when their members can be paired off one-to-one. Much later, this pairing idea lets mathematicians compare the sizes of *infinite* collections.

</ForGrownUps>

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's yes-or-no game">

Gideon likes statements that are clearly **true** or clearly **false**. Look at the collection of yellow things: 🍌 🍋. Is each statement true or false?

1. "The banana belongs to the yellow collection." — **True.**
2. "Every member of the yellow collection is long." — **False!** The lemon is round. One example that breaks a rule is enough to show it is false.
3. "There is a member of the blue collection." — **False.** The blue collection is empty.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Number 2 is my favourite. To show "every member is long" is false, I only need *one* member that is not long. The lemon does the job!

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"An empty box isn't a collection."** It is! The empty collection is a real collection — it just has no members. Mathematicians find it very useful.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If I move things around, I get a different collection."** Moving members around changes the *order*, not the collection. Same members means same collection.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Bigger things count more."** A big coconut and a small strawberry each count as **one** member.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Where collections lead">

In the next lesson, collections get a grown-up name: **sets**. Mathematicians write a set with curly brackets, like $\{\text{banana}, \text{lemon}\}$, and they write the empty set as $\varnothing$.

The rule "two collections are the same when they have the same members" is one of the most important rules about sets. It is called the **principle of extensionality**.

<ForGrownUps title="sorting rules are functions">

A sorting rule that sends every item to exactly one box is an example of a **function** — the subject of lesson 4. Functions, and the ways they combine, eventually lead to *category theory*. Young learners do not need any of these words; sorting is enough.

</ForGrownUps>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Counting around the world, long ago">

People have been counting collections for a very long time.

- In southern Africa, a baboon bone with notches cut into it — the **Lebombo bone** — is more than 40,000 years old. Some researchers think the notches were tally marks for counting, though nobody knows for certain.
- In the Andes mountains of South America, Inca record-keepers used **khipu**: cords with knots tied in them to record numbers of people, animals and goods.
- Many cultures counted with fingers, toes, pebbles and knots. The word *calculate* comes from the Latin word for a small pebble.

</Section>
