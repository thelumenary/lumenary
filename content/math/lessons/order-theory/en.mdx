<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Which phone is best?">

A student in Accra is choosing a phone. She cares about two things: price (lower is better) and battery life (longer is better).

| Phone | Price (cedis) | Battery (hours) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 1 200 | 20 |
| B | 1 500 | 30 |
| C | 1 800 | 25 |
| D | 2 400 | 40 |
| E | 1 400 | 18 |

Phone B is better than C in both respects, and A is better than E. But A and B cannot be compared: A is cheaper, B lasts longer. So "is at least as good in both respects as" orders the phones only **partially**. There is no single best phone; instead there are several that nothing beats — A, B and D — which economists call **Pareto optimal**, after the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto, and mathematicians call **maximal**.

Partial orders are everywhere once you look: train and coach journeys from Osaka to Tokyo compared by fare and travel time (the bullet train is fastest, the overnight coach is cheapest, and neither beats the other), courses and their prerequisites (this curriculum is organised by one), tasks in a building project, versions of a document, subsets ordered by inclusion, whole numbers ordered by divisibility. This lesson gives the precise language for them — and shows how carefully one must use words like "best", "bound" and "least" when not everything can be compared.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="preorder, partial order, total order">

A relation $\le$ on a set $P$ is a **preorder** if it is **reflexive** ($a \le a$) and **transitive** ($a \le b$ and $b \le c$ imply $a \le c$). It is a **partial order** if moreover it is **antisymmetric** ($a \le b$ and $b \le a$ imply $a = b$); then $(P, \le)$ is a **partially ordered set**, or **poset**. Elements with $a \le b$ or $b \le a$ are **comparable**. A partial order in which every two elements are comparable is a **total** (or **linear**) order. We write $a < b$ for "$a \le b$ and $a \neq b$".

</Definition>

<Definition term="chain, antichain, cover">

A **chain** is a subset in which every two elements are comparable; an **antichain** is one in which no two distinct elements are. $b$ **covers** $a$ if $a < b$ and there is no $c$ with $a < c < b$; the covering pairs are the edges of the Hasse diagram.

</Definition>

<Definition term="maximal and greatest elements">

An element $m \in P$ is **maximal** if there is no $x \in P$ with $m < x$, and **greatest** if $x \le m$ for every $x \in P$. **Minimal** and **least** elements are defined dually.

</Definition>

<Definition term="upper bounds and suprema">

Let $S \subseteq P$. An **upper bound** of $S$ is an element $b \in P$ with $s \le b$ for all $s \in S$. A **least upper bound** or **supremum** of $S$, written $\sup S$ or $\bigvee S$, is an upper bound that is $\le$ every upper bound. **Lower bounds** and the **greatest lower bound** (**infimum**, $\inf S$ or $\bigwedge S$) are defined dually. Note that $\sup S$ is an element of $P$; it need not belong to $S$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="lattice, complete lattice">

A **lattice** is a poset in which every pair $a, b$ has a supremum $a \vee b$ (the **join**) and an infimum $a \wedge b$ (the **meet**). A **complete lattice** is a poset in which *every* subset, including $\varnothing$ and infinite ones, has a supremum and an infimum.

</Definition>

<Definition term="well-order">

A **well-order** is a total order in which every non-empty subset has a least element.

</Definition>

<Definition term="monotone map">

A map $f : P \to Q$ between posets is **monotone** (order-preserving) if $a \le b$ implies $f(a) \le f(b)$. A **fixed point** of $f : P \to P$ is an element with $f(a) = a$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Zorn's lemma">

**Zorn's lemma:** if $P$ is a non-empty poset in which every chain has an upper bound in $P$, then $P$ has a maximal element. (We state it now and discuss its status below.)

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Examples and non-examples">

<Example title="Orders on numbers">

- $(\N, \le)$, $(\Z, \le)$, $(\Q, \le)$ and $(\R, \le)$ are total orders.
- $(\N, \mid)$, divisibility, is a partial order but not a total one ($2$ and $3$ are incomparable). Its least element is $1$, which divides everything; its greatest element is $0$, which every number divides.
- On $\Z$, divisibility is only a preorder: $2 \mid -2$ and $-2 \mid 2$ but $2 \neq -2$. Identifying each integer with its negative (passing to equivalence classes) turns it into a partial order — a general way of making a preorder into a partial order.
- The strict relation $<$ is not a partial order in our sense, because it is not reflexive; it is a **strict order**. Partial orders and strict orders correspond one-to-one via $a < b \iff (a \le b$ and $a \neq b)$.

</Example>

<Example title="Orders on pairs">

On $\R^2$, the **product order** $(a, b) \le (c, d) \iff a \le c$ and $b \le d$ is partial: $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$ are incomparable. This is the Pareto order of the motivating example, with prices negated. The **lexicographic order** — compare first coordinates, and use the second only to break ties, as in a dictionary — is total.

</Example>

<Example title="Maximal but not greatest">

In $(\{1, \dots, 12\}, \mid)$ the maximal elements are $7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12$ — exactly the numbers with no proper multiple in the set — and there is no greatest element. The least element is $1$. In the phones example, A, B and D are maximal and none is greatest.

</Example>

<Example title="Bounds and suprema">

- In $(\mathcal{P}(A), \subseteq)$, every family of subsets has a supremum, its union, and an infimum, its intersection: $\mathcal{P}(A)$ is a complete lattice.
- In $(\N, \mid)$, $\gcd(a, b)$ is the meet of $a$ and $b$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$ is the join; $(\N, \mid)$ is a lattice (in fact a complete one). But $\{1, \dots, 12\}$ with divisibility is not a lattice: $2$ and $7$ have no upper bound in the set at all.
- In $(\Q, \le)$, the set $\{q : q < 1\}$ has supremum $1$, which is not in the set, while $\{q : q^2 < 2\}$ is bounded above but has no supremum in $\Q$ (see <TopicLink id="math.number.real-numbers">real numbers</TopicLink>).

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Maximal elements and suprema">

<Theorem name="Proposition 1 (greatest versus maximal)">

In a poset, a greatest element, if it exists, is unique and is the only maximal element. In a total order, an element is maximal if and only if it is greatest.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $g$ and $g'$ are greatest, then $g \le g'$ and $g' \le g$, so $g = g'$ by antisymmetry. A greatest $g$ is maximal: if $g < x$ then also $x \le g$, and antisymmetry gives $x = g$, contradicting $g < x$. If $m$ is any maximal element, then $m \le g$, and maximality forbids $m < g$, so $m = g$. In a total order, let $m$ be maximal and $x$ arbitrary; $x$ and $m$ are comparable, and $m < x$ is impossible, so $x \le m$: $m$ is greatest.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (finite posets have maximal elements)">

Every finite non-empty poset has a maximal element.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*First proof (climbing).* Start at any element $a_0$. If it is not maximal, pick $a_1 > a_0$; if $a_1$ is not maximal, pick $a_2 > a_1$, and so on. By transitivity $a_i < a_j$ whenever $i < j$, so the $a_i$ are distinct (if $a_i = a_j$ with $i < j$ we would have $a_i < a_i$). A finite set cannot contain infinitely many distinct elements, so the climb stops — at a maximal element.

*Second proof (counting, the one checked in Lean).* For each $y$ let $D(y) = \{x : x \le y\}$ be its down-set, and choose $m$ with $|D(m)|$ as large as possible. If $m \le x$, then $D(m) \subseteq D(x)$ by transitivity; by the choice of $m$, $|D(x)| \le |D(m)|$, so $D(m) = D(x)$. As $x \in D(x) = D(m)$, we get $x \le m$, and antisymmetry gives $x = m$. So nothing is strictly above $m$.

</Proof>

The first proof makes a sequence of choices, which is harmless for finitely many steps; for infinite posets the climb may go on for ever ($0 < 1 < 2 < \cdots$ in $\N$), and Zorn's lemma is exactly the principle that rescues it when every chain is bounded.

<Theorem name="Corollary 3 (linear extensions)">

Every partial order on a finite set can be extended to a total order: there is a total order $\preceq$ with $a \le b \Rightarrow a \preceq b$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Induction on the number of elements. The empty poset is totally ordered. Otherwise let $m$ be maximal (Theorem 2), totally order $P \setminus \{m\}$ extending its partial order (induction hypothesis), and put $m$ at the end. Since nothing in $P$ is strictly above $m$, no comparison $m < x$ is violated.

</Proof>

This is the mathematics behind **topological sorting**: listing courses so that every course comes after its prerequisites, or scheduling tasks so that each starts after the tasks it depends on.

<Theorem name="Proposition 4 (suprema are unique; unions and gcds)">

A subset has at most one supremum. In $(\mathcal{P}(A), \subseteq)$, $\sup\{S, T\} = S \cup T$ and $\inf\{S, T\} = S \cap T$; in $(\N, \mid)$, $\inf\{a, b\} = \gcd(a, b)$ and $\sup\{a, b\} = \operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $s$ and $s'$ are both suprema, each is an upper bound and each is below every upper bound, so $s \le s'$ and $s' \le s$: $s = s'$.

$S \cup T$ contains $S$ and $T$, and any set $U$ containing both contains $S \cup T$; so $S \cup T$ is the least upper bound. Dually for $S \cap T$. In $(\N, \mid)$, $\gcd(a, b)$ divides $a$ and $b$, and every common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $\gcd(a, b)$ (a consequence of Bézout's identity; see <TopicLink id="math.number.divisibility-gcd">divisibility</TopicLink>), so $\gcd(a, b)$ is the greatest lower bound in the divisibility order. Dually, every common multiple is a multiple of $\operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$.

</Proof>

In any lattice the operations satisfy associative and commutative laws, $a \vee a = a$, and the **absorption laws**

$$
a \vee (a \wedge b) = a, \qquad a \wedge (a \vee b) = a.
$$

(For the first: $a$ is an upper bound of $a$ and of $a \wedge b \le a$, so $a \vee (a \wedge b) \le a$, and the reverse inequality always holds.) Conversely, a set with two operations obeying these laws is a lattice, ordered by $a \le b \iff a \wedge b = a$: lattices can be defined algebraically, as in <TopicLink id="math.algebra.algebraic-structures">algebraic structures</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="well-orders" kind="proof" title="Well-orders">

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (ℕ is well-ordered)">

Every non-empty subset of $\N$ has a least element.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose $S \subseteq \N$ has no least element. We show by strong induction that no $k$ lies in $S$. Assume that no $n < k$ lies in $S$. If $k \in S$, then — as $k$ is not the least element of $S$ — some $n \in S$ satisfies $n < k$, contradicting the assumption. So $k \notin S$. Hence $S = \varnothing$.

</Proof>

The well-ordering principle and induction are two faces of the same fact (see <TopicLink id="math.found.proof-techniques">proof techniques</TopicLink>): induction can also be derived from well-ordering, by considering the least counterexample.

<Theorem name="Proposition 6 (ℤ and ℚ are not well-ordered)">

$(\Z, \le)$ is not a well-order: $\Z$ itself has no least element. $(\Q, \le)$ is not a well-order either, and even $\{q \in \Q : q > 0\}$ has no least element.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For every $m \in \Z$, $m - 1 < m$. For every positive rational $m$, $m/2$ is a smaller positive rational.

</Proof>

Being well-ordered depends on the order, not just the set: $\Z$ *can* be well-ordered, for example as $0, -1, 1, -2, 2, \dots$ (compare by absolute value, negatives first). Cantor's **ordinal numbers** are the "lengths" of well-orders, continuing the natural numbers into the infinite: $0, 1, 2, \dots, \omega, \omega + 1, \dots$ Every well-order supports proofs by **transfinite induction**, the same argument as Theorem 5. Zermelo's **well-ordering theorem** (1904) says every set can be well-ordered — even $\R$ — but its proof uses the axiom of choice, and no explicit well-order of $\R$ can be written down.

</Section>

<Section id="fixed-points" kind="proof" title="Fixed points and Zorn's lemma">

<Theorem name="Theorem 7 (Knaster–Tarski)">

Let $L$ be a complete lattice and $f : L \to L$ a monotone map. Then $f$ has a least fixed point, namely

$$
a = \inf\{x \in L : f(x) \le x\}.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $X = \{x : f(x) \le x\}$. For every $x \in X$ we have $a \le x$, so $f(a) \le f(x) \le x$ by monotonicity. Thus $f(a)$ is a lower bound of $X$, and $f(a) \le a$ since $a$ is the *greatest* lower bound. Applying $f$, $f(f(a)) \le f(a)$, so $f(a) \in X$, and therefore $a \le f(a)$. Hence $f(a) = a$. Any fixed point $b$ satisfies $f(b) \le b$, so $b \in X$ and $a \le b$.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why take the infimum of the *pre*-fixed points? Think of $f(x) \le x$ as "$x$ is closed under the rules $f$ describes". The least fixed point is then the intersection of everything closed under the rules — the same recipe that defines $\N$ as the smallest set containing $0$ and closed under $n \mapsto n + 1$.

</Narrator>

This theorem is how mathematics and computer science *define* things "inductively". For instance, the set of vertices reachable from a vertex $v_0$ in a graph is the least fixed point of the monotone map $S \mapsto \{v_0\} \cup \{\text{successors of } S\}$ on the complete lattice of sets of vertices. In a finite lattice it can also be computed by iterating $f$ from the bottom element, $\varnothing \subseteq f(\varnothing) \subseteq f(f(\varnothing)) \subseteq \cdots$, until the sequence stops growing. Knaster–Tarski also gives a slick proof of the Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem (see <TopicLink id="math.found.cardinals">cardinals</TopicLink>).

**Zorn's lemma.** Theorem 2 guarantees maximal elements in finite posets. In infinite posets they may not exist — $(\N, \le)$ has none — and Zorn's lemma gives a sufficient condition: if every chain has an upper bound, a maximal element exists. It is the standard tool for producing "maximal" objects in infinite settings. For example, to find a basis of a vector space $V$, consider the poset of linearly independent subsets of $V$ ordered by inclusion; the union of a chain of independent sets is independent and is an upper bound, so Zorn's lemma gives a maximal independent set, which must span $V$ (otherwise a vector outside the span could be added). The same pattern gives maximal ideals in rings. Zorn's lemma cannot be proved from the other axioms of set theory (ZF): over ZF it is **equivalent to the axiom of choice** (see <TopicLink id="math.found.axiom-of-choice">the axiom of choice</TopicLink>). In this lesson we use it as a tool, to be justified there.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Treating "maximal" and "greatest" as synonyms.** Greatest means above *everything*; maximal means *nothing is above it*. They agree in total orders, but a partial order can have many maximal elements and no greatest one: in $(\{1, \dots, 12\}, \mid)$ there are six maximal elements and no greatest. If a greatest element exists, it is the unique maximal element (Proposition 1) — but a unique maximal element need not be greatest in an infinite poset (take $\N$ together with one extra element $\top$ that is comparable only with itself: $\top$ is the only maximal element, yet it is not above $0$).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Assuming every partial order is total.** Divisibility, inclusion of sets and the product order on pairs all have incomparable elements. Arguments such as "either $a \le b$ or $b \le a$" are valid only after totality has been established.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The least upper bound of a set belongs to the set."** It need not: $\sup\{q \in \Q : q < 1\} = 1$ is not in the set, and in $(\mathcal{P}(A), \subseteq)$ the supremum of $\{\{1\}, \{2\}\}$ is $\{1, 2\}$. When the supremum does belong to the set, it is the set's greatest element.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A set with a least element is well-ordered."** Well-ordering requires that *every non-empty subset* has a least element. The non-negative rationals have a least element, $0$, but their subset of positive rationals has none.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Zorn's lemma is proved by climbing until you reach the top."** Climbing works for finite posets (Theorem 2). For infinite ones the climb must continue transfinitely, choosing an upper bound at every limit stage — infinitely many arbitrary choices, which is exactly what the axiom of choice provides. Without choice, Zorn's lemma cannot be proved.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Finite topologies are preorders">

Every topological space $X$ carries a **specialisation preorder**: $x \preceq y$ when every open set containing $x$ also contains $y$ (equivalently, $x$ lies in the closure of $\{y\}$, with the convention that open sets are "up-sets"). For finite spaces the correspondence is perfect: topologies on a finite set are in one-to-one correspondence with preorders on it, the open sets being exactly the up-closed sets, and the space is $T_0$ (points are distinguished by open sets) exactly when the preorder is a partial order. So the census counts topologies too: there are $29$ topologies on a three-point set, the same as the number of preorders. Continuous maps between finite spaces are exactly the monotone maps. In the other direction, every total order generates an **order topology**, and for a dense total order connectedness is equivalent to completeness — the reason $\R$ is connected and $\Q$ is not (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.point-set-topology">point-set topology</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="The Boolean lattice is a cube">

The Hasse diagram of $\mathcal{P}(\{1, \dots, n\})$ is the graph of the $n$-dimensional cube: identify a subset with its indicator vector in $\{0, 1\}^n$, and two subsets are joined when they differ in one coordinate. Rank (the size of a subset) is height in the cube; the meet and join are coordinatewise minimum and maximum. More generally, the faces of a convex polytope — vertices, edges, faces, …, ordered by inclusion — form a lattice, the **face lattice**, which captures the polytope's combinatorial shape; the face lattice of a cube, for instance, has $8 + 12 + 6$ elements between the empty face and the whole cube. Product orders on $\R^n$ are geometric too: the elements above a point form a closed orthant, and the Pareto front of a finite set is its set of maximal points.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="combinatorics" title="Chains, antichains and Dilworth's theorem">

In a finite poset, a chain and an antichain share at most one element, so covering the poset by chains needs at least as many chains as the size of the largest antichain. **Dilworth's theorem** (1950) says this bound is attained: the minimum number of chains covering a finite poset equals the maximum size of an antichain. Its dual, **Mirsky's theorem**, says the length of the longest chain equals the minimum number of antichains covering the poset. In $\mathcal{P}(\{1, \dots, n\})$, the largest antichain has $\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$ sets — all subsets of the middle size — which is **Sperner's theorem** (1928). For $(\{1, \dots, 12\}, \mid)$, the six maximal elements $7, \dots, 12$ form an antichain, and the chains $1 \mid 2 \mid 4 \mid 8$, $3 \mid 6 \mid 12$, $9$, $5 \mid 10$, $7$, $11$ cover the poset, so six is the answer to both questions (see <TopicLink id="math.discrete.combinatorics">combinatorics</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="logic" title="Implication is an order, and induction is a least fixed point">

Propositions ordered by implication ($p \le q$ when $p$ implies $q$, up to logical equivalence) form a lattice whose meet is "and" and whose join is "or": a **Boolean algebra**, the algebraic face of propositional logic (see <TopicLink id="math.found.propositional-logic">propositional logic</TopicLink>). The Knaster–Tarski theorem is the foundation of inductive definitions: the natural numbers, the well-formed formulas of a logic and the theorems of a proof system are each the *least* set closed under certain rules — the least fixed point of a monotone operator — and proofs by induction over them are proofs that a property defines a pre-fixed point. In computer science, the meaning of a recursive program is the least fixed point of a monotone map on a lattice of partial functions, and static analysers compute least fixed points by exactly the iteration from the bottom element used in the computation section.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="categories" title="A preorder is a category with at most one arrow">

A preorder $(P, \le)$ is the same thing as a category with at most one morphism between any two objects: put an arrow $a \to b$ exactly when $a \le b$; reflexivity provides identities and transitivity provides composition. Monotone maps are then exactly functors, a supremum is a **coproduct**, an infimum is a **product**, a greatest element is a **terminal object** and a least element an **initial object**. A pair of monotone maps $f : P \to Q$, $g : Q \to P$ with $f(p) \le q \iff p \le g(q)$ — a **Galois connection** — is precisely an **adjunction** between thin categories, and much of category theory can be first learned in this simplified, ordered setting (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.categories">categories</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.cat.adjunctions">adjunctions</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Orders of magnitude and of numbers.** Comparisons of size are as old as counting, but the idea of a relation that orders only *some* pairs emerged in the nineteenth century, in the algebra of logic of George Boole, Charles Sanders Peirce and Ernst Schröder, and in Richard Dedekind's work on ideals: Dedekind's "Dualgruppen" (1897, 1900) are what we now call lattices.
- **Well-orders and choice.** Georg Cantor introduced well-ordered sets and ordinal numbers in the 1880s and 1890s. Ernst Zermelo proved in 1904 that every set can be well-ordered, using the axiom of choice he made explicit for the purpose. Felix Hausdorff's *Grundzüge der Mengenlehre* (1914) developed the theory of ordered sets systematically and stated a maximal principle; Kazimierz Kuratowski (1922) and Max Zorn (1935) gave the forms now used as "Zorn's lemma".
- **Lattices and fixed points.** Garrett Birkhoff's *Lattice Theory* (1940) established lattices as a field of their own. Bronisław Knaster and Alfred Tarski proved the fixed-point theorem for sets in 1928, and Tarski gave the general lattice version in 1955. Hasse diagrams take their name from Helmut Hasse, who used them in algebraic number theory; Robert Dilworth proved his decomposition theorem in 1950.
- **Orders at work.** Algorithms for ordering tasks consistently with their dependencies — topological sorting — were developed for project planning and computing in the 1950s and 1960s, and today schedule everything from building projects to software compilation.

</Section>
