Every evening, Nomin helps her grandmother bring their goats home across the wide grassland of Mongolia. There are seven goats: Snowy, Patch, Socks, Star, Pebble, Moss and Biscuit.

<Section id="how-to-count" kind="intuition" title="Touch, say, and stop">

Good counters follow three rules.

1. **Touch each thing exactly once.** Don't skip any, and don't touch any twice.
2. **Say the number words in the same order every time:** one, two, three, four, five, …
3. **The last word you say tells how many.** If your last word is *seven*, there are seven goats.

When things stand in a line, rule 1 is easy. When they stand in a ring or in a muddle, it is harder! Two good tricks: **move** each thing to a new place as you count it, or **mark** the thing you started with, so you know when to stop.

<Section id="big-ideas" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="counting">

To **count** a collection, we match each member with exactly one number word, in order — *one, two, three, …* — without skipping or repeating. The last word we say is the **number** of members.

</Definition>

<Definition term="the same number">

Two collections have **the same number** of members when their members can be **paired up** exactly — one with one — with none left over.

</Definition>

<Definition term="zero">

**Zero** is the number of members of the empty collection. When there is nothing to count, we say no number words at all, and the answer is zero.

</Definition>

<Narrator who="gideon">

Look at the second big idea. It does not use any numbers! You can find out whether two collections are the same size just by pairing them up.

</Narrator>

You met collections and the empty collection in <TopicLink id="math.found.collections">the lesson on collections</TopicLink>. Now we can say *how many* members a collection has.

</Section>

<Section id="why" kind="human" title="Why counting works">

**Why does the last word tell how many?** When Nomin counts, every goat gets one number word: Snowy–*one*, Patch–*two*, …, Biscuit–*seven*. So the goats are **paired up** with the words *one* to *seven*, with none left over. The last word, *seven*, is not the name of Biscuit. It tells us how far along the number words the pairing reached — and that is exactly what "there are seven goats" means.

**Why doesn't the order matter?** Count again, starting with Moss. You make different pairs this time — Moss–*one*, and so on. But you still touch each goat exactly once. No goat was added and none was taken away, so you run out of goats at the same word: *seven*.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So the number belongs to the **herd**, not to the way we counted it. That is why everybody who counts carefully gets the same answer — in Mongolia, in Mexico, or anywhere at all.

</Narrator>

**Is there a biggest number?** Say any number you like: a hundred, a thousand, a million. I can always say *one more*: a million and one! Every counting number has a next one, so there is **no biggest counting number**. The counting words go on for ever.

<ForGrownUps title="the mathematics behind the three rules">

Write $[n] = \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, with $[0] = \varnothing$. Counting a collection $C$ correctly means building a **bijection** (a perfect pairing) between $C$ and some $[n]$; the last word said is $n$. The child's question "would a different order give a different answer?" becomes: could $C$ be paired perfectly with both $[m]$ and $[n]$ for $m \neq n$? Composing one pairing with the reverse of the other would then pair $[m]$ perfectly with $[n]$. So it is enough to prove:

<Theorem name="Uniqueness of counting">

If $[m]$ and $[n]$ can be paired perfectly, then $m = n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By induction on $m$. If $m = 0$, then $[m]$ is empty, so no member of $[n]$ can have a partner; since a perfect pairing leaves nothing over, $[n]$ is empty too, and $n = 0$. Now let $m \geq 1$ and suppose the statement holds for $m - 1$ (and every $n$). Since $[m]$ is not empty, neither is $[n]$, so $n \geq 1$. If $m$ is not already paired with $n$, swap partners: if $m$ is paired with $k$ and $j$ is paired with $n$, pair $m$ with $n$ and $j$ with $k$ instead. This is still a perfect pairing. Now remove the pair $(m, n)$: what remains is a perfect pairing of $[m-1]$ with $[n-1]$, so $m - 1 = n - 1$ by the induction hypothesis, and $m = n$.

</Proof>

Combining pairings in the same way shows that if two collections can be paired perfectly, counting them gives the same number. "There is no largest counting number" is the statement that every natural number $n$ has a successor $n + 1 > n$, which is built into the natural numbers in Dedekind's (1888) and Peano's (1889) descriptions of them. Developmental psychologists Rochel Gelman and Randy Gallistel (1978) described children's counting by five principles — one-to-one, stable order, cardinality, abstraction (anything can be counted) and order-irrelevance — which match the three rules above closely.

</ForGrownUps>

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon likes statements that are clearly **true** or clearly **false**. Are these true or false?

1. "Nomin counted seven goats, starting with Snowy. If she starts with Moss instead, she will get seven again." — **True.** The order of counting never changes the number.
2. "Ravi spread his counters out, so now he has more." — **False.** Pair them up: none are left over.
3. "If every child gets exactly one cup and no cups are left over, there are just as many cups as children." — **True.** That is what *the same number* means.
4. "There is a biggest counting number." — **False!**

<Narrator who="gideon">

Number 4 is my favourite. To show it is false, I don't have to try every number — there are far too many. I only need to show that whatever number you pick, the next number is bigger. Then no number can be the biggest.

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"Spreading things out makes more of them."** A long row can have the same number of things as a short row. Moving things changes where they are, not how many there are. To check, pair them up — or count them both.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"I counted that one already… or did I?"** When things are in a ring or a muddle, it is easy to count one twice or to skip one. Move each thing as you count it, or mark where you started.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The last number is the name of the last thing."** When Nomin says *seven* while touching Biscuit, Biscuit is not called "seven". *Seven* tells how many goats there are in the whole herd. If someone asks "how many goats?", the answer is "seven", not "Biscuit".

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Zero isn't a number."** Zero is the number of things in an empty collection, such as the goats in an empty pen. It is a very useful number — you will meet it again and again.

</Misconception>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="For grown-ups: where counting leads">

<Viewpoint lens="sets" title="Counting is pairing">

In set theory, "$C$ has $n$ members" means there is a bijection $C \to \{1, \dots, n\}$, and two sets have the **same cardinality** when there is a bijection between them. Gottlob Frege (1884) took this as the starting point of arithmetic: the number of $F$s equals the number of $G$s exactly when the $F$s and $G$s can be paired one-to-one (a principle now called **Hume's principle**). Pairing still works when counting is impossible: the even numbers can be paired perfectly with all the counting numbers ($n \leftrightarrow 2n$), and Georg Cantor showed (in 1874, and again with his famous diagonal argument in 1891) that some infinite collections, such as the real numbers, cannot be paired with the counting numbers at all. See <TopicLink id="math.found.countability">countability and Cantor's diagonal argument</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.found.cardinals">ordinals and cardinals</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="combinatorics" title="Counting the ways to count">

A collection of $n$ things can be counted in $n! = n \times (n-1) \times \dots \times 2 \times 1$ different orders, because there are $n$ choices for the first thing, then $n - 1$ for the second, and so on. Seven goats can be counted in $5040$ orders, all ending on *seven*. Combinatorialists also prize **double counting** — counting the same collection in two different ways to discover that two expressions are equal (for example, counting handshakes at a party by person and by pair). It is the respectable cousin of the counting slip children learn to avoid. See <TopicLink id="math.discrete.systematic-listing">systematic listing and counting</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Counting around the world">

People everywhere have counted, in many clever ways.

- The **Ishango bone**, found at Ishango near Lake Edward, in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo, is about 20,000 years old and carries groups of notches. Some researchers think the notches record counts, although nobody knows for certain.
- In ancient **Mesopotamia** (modern Iraq), people kept track of goods and animals with small clay tokens, one token for each thing — counting by pairing. Archaeologists have even found a hollow clay ball from the town of Nuzi that held small stones, with writing on the outside listing a herd of sheep and goats.
- Languages count in different ways. In **Yoruba**, spoken in Nigeria and Benin, twenty is an important counting unit, and some numbers are made by taking away: the Yoruba word for sixteen means "four less than twenty".
- The **Oksapmin** people of Papua New Guinea have traditionally counted by touching body parts in order: the thumb and fingers of one hand, then up the arm, across the face and down to the other hand — a counting sequence of 27 body parts.
- The Indian mathematician **Brahmagupta**, in 628 CE, gave rules for calculating with zero as a number, and the **Maya** of Central America used a sign for zero in their calendar.
- More than 2,200 years ago, **Archimedes** of Syracuse wrote *The Sand Reckoner*, showing how to name numbers bigger than the number of grains of sand it would take to fill the universe as he pictured it. There is always a bigger number.
