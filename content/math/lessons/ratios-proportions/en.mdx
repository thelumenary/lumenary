<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Better buys and stronger lemonade">

At a market in Lima, one stall sells **3 kg of rice for 12 soles**; the next sells **5 kg for 19 soles**. Which is the better buy? The second costs more in total — but you also get more rice.

Two jugs of lemonade are mixed from concentrate and water:

- jug A: 2 cups of concentrate and 3 cups of water;
- jug B: 3 cups of concentrate and 5 cups of water.

Which tastes more lemony? Jug B has one more cup of concentrate — and also two more cups of water.

And a bookshop in Dublin raises the price of a book by 10%, then a week later lowers it by 10%. Is it back where it started?

Each question compares quantities **multiplicatively** (“how many times as much?”) rather than **additively** (“how much more?”). That shift is what this lesson is about.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Thinking in times, not in more">

Here is a classic puzzle used by researchers studying how children reason. Mr Short is 4 buttons tall. Measured with paper clips, he is 6 clips tall. His friend Mr Tall is 6 buttons tall. How many paper clips tall is Mr Tall?

A tempting answer is $8$: “Mr Tall is 2 buttons more, so 2 clips more.” But a button and a clip are different sizes. Since 4 buttons match 6 clips, **1 button matches $\tfrac64 = 1.5$ clips**, so 6 buttons match $6 \times 1.5 = 9$ clips. The two heights are in the same **ratio** whatever we measure them with: $4 : 6$ buttons-to-clips, and $6 : 9$.

The tools of this lesson keep the multiplicative structure visible:

- a **ratio table**, whose rows are all scaled copies of each other;
- a **double number line**, with one quantity above and one below, matched at every point;
- the **unit rate**: how much of one quantity goes with *one* unit of the other.

| buttons | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| clips | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 1.5 |

<Narrator who="chloe">

In the table, every column is the first column times something. And I noticed something else: adding two columns gives another column — $4 + 2 = 6$ buttons and $6 + 3 = 9$ clips. Is that always allowed? We'll prove exactly when it is.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="ratio">

A **ratio** $a : b$ (“$a$ to $b$”) compares two quantities $a$ and $b$. Two ratios $a : b$ and $c : d$ of positive quantities are **equivalent** if one is a scaled copy of the other: $c = ka$ and $d = kb$ for one number $k > 0$.

</Definition>

So $2 : 3$, $4 : 6$ and $10 : 15$ are equivalent (scale factors $2$ and $5$), but $2 : 3$ and $3 : 4$ are not. A ratio can compare **part to part** (2 cups of concentrate to 3 cups of water) or **part to whole** (2 cups of concentrate in 5 cups of lemonade, so $\tfrac25$ of the jug is concentrate). A <TopicLink id="math.prob.probability-intro">probability</TopicLink> with equally likely outcomes is a part-to-whole ratio too: favourable outcomes to all outcomes.

<Definition term="rate and unit rate">

A **rate** is a ratio of two quantities measured in different units, such as 360 km in 4 hours. The **unit rate** is the amount of the first quantity per *one* unit of the second: $360 \div 4 = 90$ km per hour.

</Definition>

<Definition term="proportional relationship">

A quantity $y$ is **proportional** to a quantity $x$ if there is a fixed number $k$, the **constant of proportionality**, with

$$
y = kx \quad \text{for every value of } x.
$$

</Definition>

The constant $k$ is the unit rate: the value of $y$ when $x = 1$.

<Definition term="percent">

**Percent** means “per hundred”: $p\%$ means $\tfrac{p}{100}$, and $p\%$ of $x$ is $\tfrac{p}{100} \cdot x$.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="The better buy">

Compare unit prices: $12 \div 3 = 4$ soles per kg, and $19 \div 5 = 3.8$ soles per kg. The 5 kg bag is the better buy. (We could also compare kilograms per sol: $0.25$ against about $0.263$. Either unit rate works, as long as we compare like with like.)

</Example>

<Example title="The stronger lemonade">

Compare the part-to-whole fractions of concentrate: jug A is $\tfrac25 = 0.4$ concentrate, jug B is $\tfrac38 = 0.375$. Jug A is stronger. The ratios $2 : 3$ and $3 : 5$ are not equivalent: adding one cup of each is not scaling.

</Example>

<Example title="Scaling a recipe">

A jollof rice recipe for 4 people uses 3 cups of rice. How much for 10 people? The unit rate is $\tfrac34$ cup per person, so $10 \times \tfrac34 = 7.5$ cups. Or scale directly: $10$ people is $\tfrac{10}{4} = 2.5$ times as many, so $2.5 \times 3 = 7.5$ cups.

</Example>

<Example title="A proportional relationship in the real world">

Since 1999 the West and Central African CFA francs have had a fixed exchange rate with the euro: $1$ euro $= 655.957$ CFA francs. So the number of francs $y$ is proportional to the number of euros $x$: $y = 655.957\,x$. For example, $20$ euros is $13\,119.14$ francs.

</Example>

<Example title="Straight lines that are not proportional">

- A taxi charges a start fee of 200 plus 150 per km: fare $= 150x + 200$. Each extra kilometre adds the same amount, so the graph is a straight line — but $1$ km costs $350$ and $2$ km costs $500$, not $700$. The fare is **not** proportional to the distance.
- Amara is 5 and her brother is 8. When Amara is 10, her brother is 13, not 16: their ages differ by a constant, they are not in a constant ratio.

</Example>

<Example title="Percentages">

- $15\%$ of $240$ is $\tfrac{15}{100} \times 240 = 36$.
- Increasing $240$ by $15\%$ gives $240 + 36 = 276 = 1.15 \times 240$.
- A price after a $20\%$ discount is $240$. The original price $P$ satisfies $0.8P = 240$, so $P = 300$. (Not $240 + 20\%$ of $240 = 288$: the discount was $20\%$ of the *original* price.)

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proof" kind="proof" title="Why the methods work">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (cross-multiplication)">

For positive quantities, $a : b$ is equivalent to $c : d$ exactly when $ad = bc$, that is, exactly when $\tfrac ab = \tfrac cd$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $c = ka$ and $d = kb$, then $ad = a(kb) = b(ka) = bc$. Conversely, suppose $ad = bc$. Let $k = \tfrac ca$, which is positive. Then $ka = c$, and $kb = \tfrac{bc}{a} = \tfrac{ad}{a} = d$. So $c : d$ is $a : b$ scaled by $k$.

</Proof>

For the lemonade: $2 \cdot 5 = 10$ but $3 \cdot 3 = 9$, so $2 : 3$ and $3 : 5$ are not equivalent.

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (spotting proportionality)">

$y$ is proportional to $x$ exactly when $y = 0$ for $x = 0$ and the ratio $\tfrac yx$ is the same for every $x \neq 0$. The common ratio is $k$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $y = kx$, then $y = 0$ when $x = 0$, and $\tfrac yx = k$ whenever $x \neq 0$. Conversely, if $\tfrac yx = k$ for all $x \neq 0$, then $y = kx$ for those $x$; and for $x = 0$ we are told $y = 0 = k \cdot 0$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (the graph)">

The graph of $y = kx$ is a straight line through the origin: it contains $(0, 0)$, and the slope between any two of its points is $k$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$x = 0$ gives $y = 0$. For two points $(x_1, kx_1)$ and $(x_2, kx_2)$ with $x_1 \neq x_2$, the slope is

$$
\frac{kx_2 - kx_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{k(x_2 - x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = k.
$$

A constant slope means the points lie on one straight line.

</Proof>

So a proportional graph is a straight line through the origin — but, as the taxi shows, a straight line that misses the origin is **not** proportional. In <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-relationships">linear relationships</TopicLink> you will study all straight lines $y = mx + c$; the proportional ones are exactly those with $c = 0$.

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (ratio tables)">

If $y = f(x)$ is proportional, then for all $x_1, x_2$ and every number $c$:

$$
f(x_1 + x_2) = f(x_1) + f(x_2) \qquad \text{and} \qquad f(cx) = c\,f(x).
$$

So adding two rows of a ratio table, or multiplying a row by a number, gives another correct row.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$f(x_1 + x_2) = k(x_1 + x_2) = kx_1 + kx_2 = f(x_1) + f(x_2)$ by the distributive law, and $f(cx) = k(cx) = c(kx) = c f(x)$.

</Proof>

For the taxi, adding rows fails: $1$ km ($350$) plus $2$ km ($500$) would predict $850$ for $3$ km, but the fare is $650$ — the start fee has been counted twice.

There is a converse for whole numbers. If the price of $m + n$ identical items is always the price of $m$ items plus the price of $n$ items, then the price of $n$ items is $n$ times the price of one: $f(n) = f(1 + 1 + \cdots + 1) = f(1) + \cdots + f(1) = n\,f(1)$. “Combining amounts combines the costs” is exactly proportionality.

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (percentage changes multiply)">

A rise of $p\%$ multiplies an amount by $1 + \tfrac{p}{100}$, and a fall of $p\%$ multiplies it by $1 - \tfrac{p}{100}$. So successive changes **multiply**, and their order does not matter. In particular, a rise of $p\%$ followed by a fall of $p\%$ multiplies the amount by

$$
\left(1 + \tfrac{p}{100}\right)\left(1 - \tfrac{p}{100}\right) = 1 - \left(\tfrac{p}{100}\right)^2,
$$

which is less than $1$ whenever $p \neq 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

A rise of $p\%$ turns $x$ into $x + \tfrac{p}{100}x = \left(1 + \tfrac{p}{100}\right)x$; similarly for a fall. Two changes multiply $x$ by both factors, and multiplication is commutative, so the order does not matter. Writing $r = \tfrac{p}{100}$, $(1 + r)(1 - r) = 1 - r + r - r^2 = 1 - r^2$. If $r \neq 0$ then $r^2 > 0$ (a positive times a positive, or a negative times a negative), so $1 - r^2 < 1$.

</Proof>

For $p = 10$: the factor is $1.1 \times 0.9 = 0.99$, so the price ends $1\%$ lower. The fall is $10\%$ of a **bigger** number than the rise was.

<Theorem name="Theorem 6 (undoing a rise)">

A rise of $p\%$ (with $p \ge 0$) is undone by a fall of $\dfrac{100p}{100 + p}\%$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$\left(1 + \tfrac{p}{100}\right)\left(1 - \tfrac{p}{100 + p}\right) = \tfrac{100 + p}{100} \cdot \tfrac{100}{100 + p} = 1.$

</Proof>

A $25\%$ rise is undone by a $20\%$ fall; a $100\%$ rise (doubling) by a $50\%$ fall (halving).

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Thinking additively instead of multiplicatively.** “Mr Tall is 2 buttons taller, so he is 2 clips taller” gives $8$ clips instead of $9$. When two quantities are proportional, ask “how many times as much?”, not “how much more?”.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“Every straight-line graph is proportional.”** $y = 2x + 1$ is a straight line, but doubling $x$ does not double $y$, and the line misses the origin. Proportional means $y = kx$: a straight line **through the origin**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Adding successive percentage changes.** A $10\%$ rise and a $10\%$ fall do not cancel (the result is $1\%$ lower), and five $10\%$ rises make $61.051\%$, not $50\%$. Percentage changes multiply.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Mixing up part-to-part and part-to-whole.** In a $2 : 3$ mix of concentrate to water, concentrate is $\tfrac25$ of the jug, not $\tfrac23$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Confusing percent with percentage points.** If an interest rate rises from $4\%$ to $5\%$, it rises by $1$ *percentage point* — which is a $25\%$ increase in the rate.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Proportion is similarity">

Two figures are **similar** when one is a scaled copy of the other: all lengths are multiplied by the same factor $k$, so corresponding lengths are in the same ratio. A map at scale $1 : 50\,000$ is similar to the land it shows; the shadows of a pole and of a tree at the same moment form similar triangles, which lets you measure the tree. Thales' intercept theorem says that parallel lines cut two lines through a point in proportional pieces. Similar triangles also explain Theorem 3 geometrically: the points $(x, kx)$ all make similar right triangles with the $x$-axis, so they lie on one straight line through the origin. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">rigid motions and similarity</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Laws of proportion — within limits">

Many physical laws are proportionalities: distance $=$ speed $\times$ time at a steady speed; the stretch of a spring is proportional to the force on it (Hooke's law); the current through many conductors is proportional to the voltage (Ohm's law). A rate carries **units** — km per hour, newtons per metre — and checking units is a powerful way to catch errors. But such laws are models, true only within limits: stretch a spring too far and Hooke's law fails. Physics discovers proportionalities by measurement; mathematics tells us what follows *if* they hold.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Proportional relationships are linear maps">

A proportional relationship $f(x) = kx$ is exactly a function that respects addition and scaling (Theorem 4): an algebraist calls it a **linear map**. Composing two of them multiplies the constants: converting euros to CFA francs ($k = 655.957$) and then francs to some other currency ($k'$) is again proportional, with constant $k'k$. That is why chains of unit conversions work. In <TopicLink id="math.found.functions">functions</TopicLink> and later in linear algebra this idea grows into matrices.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **China.** *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* (compiled by about the 1st century CE) has a chapter on exchanging one grain for another at fixed rates, and a chapter on distributing goods and taxes in proportion.
- **India.** The *rule of three* (*trairāśika*) — given three quantities of a proportion, find the fourth — appears in Āryabhaṭa's *Āryabhaṭīya* (499 CE) and was used throughout Indian mathematics.
- **Greece.** Book V of Euclid's *Elements* (about 300 BCE) develops a general theory of ratio and proportion, traditionally credited to Eudoxus of Cnidus, which even works for lengths that have no common measure.
- **Trade.** Through mathematicians writing in Arabic, the rule of three reached Europe; Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* (1202) uses it for currency exchange and partnership problems. Percentages grew out of merchants' “per hundred” calculations; the Roman emperor Augustus had already levied a tax of one hundredth on goods sold at auction.
- **Learning.** Robert Karplus and colleagues used the “Mr Short and Mr Tall” problem in the 1970s to study how students move from additive to multiplicative reasoning — a transition researchers still study today.

</Section>
