<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Two calculators, two answers">

Kojo and Ama are shopping at the market in Kumasi, in Ghana. They buy a bag for **2 cedis** and **3 bottles of water at 4 cedis each**. Kojo types into his small calculator:

$$
2 + 3 \times 4 =
$$

His calculator says **20**. Ama types exactly the same keys into the calculator on her phone. It says **14**.

Which one is right? The shopkeeper knows: the bag costs 2 cedis, and the three bottles cost $3 \times 4 = 12$ cedis, so the total is $2 + 12 = 14$ cedis. Kojo's calculator worked from left to right as he typed, $2 + 3 = 5$, then $5 \times 4 = 20$ — as if he had bought 5 bottles!

Mathematicians all over the world have agreed on a rule for reading an expression like $2 + 3 \times 4$, so that everybody gets the same answer. In this lesson you will learn that rule, and then use it to find numbers that are hiding.

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Names for numbers">

An **expression** like $3 \times 4 + 2$ is a name for a number. It names 14. The number 14 has many other names: $10 + 4$, $2 \times 7$, $20 - 6$, $(3 + 4) \times 2$.

**Brackets** say "work this out first". In $(2 + 3) \times 4$, we add first: $5 \times 4 = 20$. In $2 + (3 \times 4)$, we multiply first: $2 + 12 = 14$.

What if there are no brackets? Think about how we talk. "Three bags of 4 oranges, and 2 more oranges" is $3 \times 4 + 2$. It would be a nuisance to write brackets every time: $(3 \times 4) + 2$. So everybody agrees: **multiplication and division are done before addition and subtraction**, unless brackets say otherwise. Then $3 \times 4 + 2$ and $2 + 3 \times 4$ both mean "some groups, plus some more" — just what we usually want.

Sometimes a number is **hidden**, and we only know something about it:

$$
3 \times \square = 24.
$$

"Three times some number makes 24." Which number is in the box? You may know straight away: 8. In this lesson you will learn a way that works even when the numbers are big — by **undoing** what was done to the hidden number.

<Narrator who="gideon">

I'm Gideon. The order of operations is an **agreement**, like driving on the same side of the road. It is not a law of nature. But once everybody agrees, $2 + 3 \times 4$ means exactly one number: 14.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="expression">

An **expression** is a combination of numbers and operations ($+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$), possibly with brackets, that names a number.

</Definition>

<Definition term="order of operations">

To work out an expression:

1. work out anything in **brackets** first;
2. then do **multiplication and division**, from left to right;
3. then do **addition and subtraction**, from left to right.

</Definition>

<Definition term="equation">

An **equation** says that two expressions have the **same value**. The equals sign "=" means "has the same value as". So $3 + 4 = 7$, $7 = 3 + 4$ and $3 + 4 = 6 + 1$ are all true equations.

</Definition>

<Definition term="unknown">

An **unknown** is a number we are asked to find. It is shown by a box, $\square$, or by a letter, such as $n$. **A letter in an expression always stands for a number.**

</Definition>

<Definition term="inverse operations">

Two operations are **inverse operations** when one **undoes** the other.

- Adding 17 is undone by subtracting 17 (and subtracting 17 is undone by adding 17).
- Multiplying by 3 is undone by dividing by 3 (and dividing by 3 is undone by multiplying by 3).

Multiplying by any number **except 0** can be undone in this way.

</Definition>

<Definition term="rule">

An **input–output rule** tells you what to do to any input number to get the output. "Double, then add 1" is a rule. Written as an expression, with the letter $n$ standing for the input, it is $2 \times n + 1$.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Let's try together">

<Example title="Using the order of operations">

- $2 + 3 \times 4 = 2 + 12 = 14$ (multiply first).
- $(2 + 3) \times 4 = 5 \times 4 = 20$ (brackets first).
- $20 - 8 \div 4 = 20 - 2 = 18$ (divide first).
- $18 \div 3 \times 2 = 6 \times 2 = 12$. Division and multiplication are on the same level, so we go **from left to right**. (Not $18 \div 6 = 3$!)
- $10 - 4 - 3 = 6 - 3 = 3$, also from left to right. (Not $10 - 1 = 9$.)

</Example>

<Example title="Finding an unknown by undoing">

**$\square + 17 = 40$.** Something plus 17 makes 40. To find it, undo "add 17" by subtracting 17: $\square = 40 - 17 = 23$. **Check:** $23 + 17 = 40$ ✓.

**$3 \times \square = 24$.** Three times something makes 24. Undo "multiply by 3" by dividing by 3: $\square = 24 \div 3 = 8$. **Check:** $3 \times 8 = 24$ ✓.

**$24 = 3 \times \square$.** This is the same equation written the other way round — "24 has the same value as 3 times something". The answer is still 8.

**$\square - 9 = 15$.** Undo "subtract 9" by adding 9: $\square = 15 + 9 = 24$. **Check:** $24 - 9 = 15$ ✓.

**$36 \div \square = 4$.** Careful: here the unknown is the number we divide *by*. 36 shared into groups of some size gives 4 groups, so the size is $36 \div 4 = 9$. **Check:** $36 \div 9 = 4$ ✓. Always check your answer in the original equation!

</Example>

<Example title="Think of a number">

Ama says: "I thought of a number, multiplied it by 3, then added 1. I got 22." What was her number?

Two things happened to the number, in this order: **multiply by 3**, then **add 1**. To undo them, we start from the end and work backwards, undoing the **last** step first:

- undo "add 1": $22 - 1 = 21$;
- undo "multiply by 3": $21 \div 3 = 7$.

Her number was 7. **Check:** $3 \times 7 + 1 = 21 + 1 = 22$ ✓. As an equation: $3 \times \square + 1 = 22$, so $\square = 7$.

<Example title="Rules and tables">

The rule "double, then add 1" is $2 \times n + 1$. To complete a table, put each input in place of $n$:

| input $n$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 10 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| output $2 \times n + 1$ | 3 | 5 | 7 | 9 | 21 |

Going **backwards**: which input gives the output 15? Undo the steps in reverse order: $15 - 1 = 14$, then $14 \div 2 = 7$. The input is 7.

Now a table without its rule:

| input $n$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| output | 4 | 7 | 10 | 13 |

Each time the input goes up by 1, the output goes up by **3**. That suggests "multiply by 3": $3 \times 1 = 3$, $3 \times 2 = 6$, … — always 1 less than the output. So the rule is "multiply by 3, then add 1", or $3 \times n + 1$. Check: $3 \times 4 + 1 = 13$ ✓. Now we can predict: input 10 gives $3 \times 10 + 1 = 31$.

</Example>

<Example title="Letters are numbers">

At a fruit stall, one apple costs $a$ cedis and one banana costs $b$ cedis. Then 4 apples and 3 bananas cost

$$
4 \times a + 3 \times b \text{ cedis.}
$$

Here $a$ is not "an apple": it is **the number of cedis one apple costs**. If $a = 5$ and $b = 2$, the cost is $4 \times 5 + 3 \times 2 = 20 + 6 = 26$ cedis.

</Example>

<Section id="why" kind="proof" title="Why undoing finds the unknown — and why it is the only answer">

<Theorem name="Finding an unknown by subtracting">

The only number that makes $\square + 17 = 40$ true is $23$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The proof has two parts.

**If a number works, it must be 23.** Suppose some number makes $\square + 17 = 40$ true. Then $\square + 17$ and $40$ are the **same number**. Take 17 away from this same number, and you get the same result either way:

$$
(\square + 17) - 17 = 40 - 17.
$$

On the left, adding 17 and then taking 17 away gets you back where you started, so the left side is just $\square$. On the right, $40 - 17 = 23$. So $\square = 23$.

**And 23 does work:** $23 + 17 = 40$.

</Proof>

The same reasoning shows that $3 \times \square = 24$ has exactly one answer: if $3 \times \square = 24$, divide both sides by 3. Dividing by 3 undoes multiplying by 3, so $\square = 24 \div 3 = 8$; and $3 \times 8 = 24$.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Did you notice the two parts? "If there is an answer, it must be 23" is not the same as "23 is an answer". The first part shows there are no *other* answers. The second part shows that the answer really exists. Checking your answer is the second part!

</Narrator>

**When there is no answer.** Not every equation has a whole-number answer.

- $3 \times \square = 25$: we know $3 \times 8 = 24$ (too small) and $3 \times 9 = 27$ (too big), and the bigger the number in the box, the bigger $3 \times \square$ gets. So no whole number works. (A **fraction** does: $25 \div 3 = 8\tfrac{1}{3}$. You can meet it in <TopicLink id="math.number.fractions">Fractions and decimals</TopicLink>.)
- $0 \times \square = 5$: zero times **any** number is 0, never 5. No number works at all.
- $0 \times \square = 0$: this is true for **every** number, so it does not tell us what is in the box.

That is why "multiplying is undone by dividing" needs the number to be **non-zero**. Multiplying by 0 sends 3 to 0 and 7 to 0 — and once you only see the 0, nothing can tell you whether it came from 3 or from 7. It cannot be undone.

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon only plays with statements that are exactly true or exactly false. Decide, and give a reason.

1. "$2 + 3 \times 4 = 20$." — **False.** Multiplication comes first: $2 + 12 = 14$.
2. "$(2 + 3) \times 4 = 20$." — **True.** The brackets are worked out first.
3. "The only number that makes $\square + 17 = 40$ true is 23." — **True.** Subtracting 17 from both sides shows it must be 23, and $23 + 17 = 40$.
4. "$3 \times \square = 25$ has a whole-number answer." — **False.** $3 \times 8 = 24$ and $3 \times 9 = 27$; 25 is in between.
5. "$24 = 3 \times \square$ is not a proper equation, because the answer must come after the equals sign." — **False!** "=" means "has the same value as". The equation is fine, and $\square = 8$.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**Working strictly from left to right.** $2 + 3 \times 4$ is not 20. Multiplication and division come before addition and subtraction, so it is $2 + 12 = 14$. Only operations on the **same** level are done from left to right: $18 \div 3 \times 2 = 12$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The answer must come after the equals sign."** The equals sign means "has the same value as", not "and the answer is". $24 = 3 \times \square$ is a perfectly good equation, and so is $3 + 4 = 6 + 1$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Writing a chain of calculations with "=".** "$3 \times 4 = 12 + 2 = 14$" looks like working, but it claims that $3 \times 4$ has the same value as 14, which is false. Write instead: $3 \times 4 + 2 = 12 + 2 = 14$, where every "=" is true.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A letter stands for a thing."** In $4 \times a + 3 \times b$, the letter $a$ does not mean "apples". It stands for a **number** — for example the price of one apple, or the number of apples in a bag.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Undoing steps in the original order.** To undo "multiply by 3, then add 1", subtract 1 **first**, then divide by 3. Undoing in the original order gives the wrong answer: for the output 22, dividing by 3 first does not even give a whole number.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="The algebraist: one line for every number">

A rule like $2 \times n + 1$ is a tiny piece of **algebra**: one short line describes infinitely many calculations, one for each number $n$. Algebra lets us reason about all of them at once. For example, $2 \times n + 1$ is always **odd**, whatever whole number $n$ is, because $2 \times n$ is even and one more than an even number is odd. Next you will write expressions with letters more freely, and solve harder equations, in <TopicLink id="math.algebra.variables-expressions">variables and algebraic expressions</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-equations">linear equations</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="Computers follow the same agreement">

Scientific calculators, spreadsheets and programming languages all use the order of operations: in Python, `2 + 3 * 4` gives `14`. Lean, the proof checker in this lesson, reads `2 + 3 * 4` the same way. A computer cannot ask what you meant, so this agreement matters even more for machines than for people.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="The set of answers">

For an equation with an unknown, we can collect **all** the numbers that make it true into a <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">set</TopicLink>, called its **solution set**:

- $\square + 17 = 40$ has the solution set $\{23\}$: exactly one answer.
- $3 \times \square = 25$ has no whole-number answer: its solution set (among whole numbers) is the **empty set** $\{\}$.
- $0 \times \square = 0$ is true for every whole number: its solution set is the set of **all** whole numbers.

"Solve the equation" means "find the solution set" — and the two parts of our proof show exactly that the solution set of $\square + 17 = 40$ is $\{23\}$: nothing else is in it, and 23 is.

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="Undoing a chain">

"Multiply by 3, then add 1" is a **chain** of two actions, one after the other. Undoing it means undoing the steps in the **reverse** order: first "subtract 1", then "divide by 3" — just as you take off your shoes before your socks. You can explore chains of actions, and why undoing goes backwards, in <TopicLink id="math.cat.chaining-actions">One thing after another</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Egypt.** The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, copied by the scribe Ahmes around 1550 BCE from an older text, contains problems with an unknown quantity, called *aha* ("heap"). Problem 24 asks for a quantity which, together with one seventh of itself, makes 19. The scribe solved it by "false position": try 7 (then 7 plus its seventh is 8), and then scale up, giving $16\tfrac{5}{8}$.
- **China.** *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, compiled about two thousand years ago, solves problems with several unknown quantities at once, using counting rods laid out on a board.
- **India.** Indian mathematicians, from Brahmagupta in the 7th century onwards, named unknown quantities with words, such as *yāvat-tāvat* ("as much as"), and used the names of colours — "black", "blue", "yellow" — when there were several unknowns at once.
- **Baghdad.** Around 820 CE, Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī wrote a book on solving equations. Its title contains the word *al-jabr*, "restoring": moving a quantity that is taken away on one side of an equation to the other side. That word became our word **algebra**.
- **The equals sign.** In 1557 the Welsh mathematician Robert Recorde introduced "=" in *The Whetstone of Witte*, choosing two parallel lines because "no two things can be more equal".
- **Letters.** In 1591 François Viète in France used letters for unknown and known numbers; in 1637 René Descartes used $x$, $y$ and $z$ for unknowns, as we still do.
- **The order of operations** grew up gradually as mathematicians wrote more and more algebra; it has no single inventor. Today it is taught, with small differences in the names (such as BODMAS, BIDMAS and PEMDAS), all over the world.
