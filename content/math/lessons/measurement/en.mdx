<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Whose is longer?">

In a school playground in Lima, Lucía and Wei are having a jumping contest. Lucía jumps from the line. Wei jumps from a chalk mark a little way in front of the line. "Mine is longer!" says Wei.

Is it? Wei started further forward, so it is hard to tell. And when they measure their jumps with their feet, heel to toe, Lucía counts 9 feet and Wei counts 11. But Wei has smaller feet than Lucía…

Measuring sounds easy, but there are some clever ideas hiding inside it. By the end of this lesson you will know how to settle the contest fairly.

Hello! I'm Chloe. Why do Lucía and Wei get different answers for the same kind of jump? Let's find out what makes a measurement *fair*.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="ideas" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="unit">

A **unit** is a fixed amount that we use for measuring: a paper clip, a cube, a hand span, a centimetre, a metre. Every copy of a unit is exactly the same size.

</Definition>

<Definition term="length">

The **length** of an object is **how many units fit along it, end to end**. There must be **no gaps** between the units and **no overlaps**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="measuring">

To **measure** a length, start at one end of the object, lay copies of the unit along it, one after another, and **count** them. If the last unit does not fit exactly, say so: "a bit more than 4 clips", or "between 4 and 5 clips".

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Let's measure together">

<Example title="A pencil in paper clips">

Amara's pencil is exactly 5 small paper clips long. Her brother measures the same pencil with large paper clips and gets 3. The pencil did not change! The **number** changed because the **unit** changed.

</Example>

<Example title="Smaller unit, bigger number">

The same table is 6 hand spans wide, or 24 paper clips wide, or 72 centimetres wide. The smaller the unit, the more copies you need, so **the smaller the unit, the bigger the number**.

</Example>

<Example title="Settling the jumping contest">

Lucía's 9 feet and Wei's 11 feet cannot be compared, because their feet are different units. To be fair, both must jump **from the same line**, and both jumps must be measured with **the same unit** — for example with a tape measure marked in centimetres, starting at 0 on the line.

</Example>

<Section id="ruler" kind="human" title="How a ruler works">

A ruler is just a row of **centimetre units glued end to end**, with numbers to count them for you. The number at a mark tells you **how many units** there are from **0** to that mark.

So to measure, put one end of the object at **0** and read the number at the other end. If the object starts at 3 instead, the number at the other end counts 3 units that are *not* under the object. Take them away: an object from 3 to 18 is $18 - 3 = 15$ centimetres long.

Be careful to count the **spaces**, not the marks. From 0 to 4 there are 5 marks (0, 1, 2, 3, 4) but only 4 spaces — and it is the spaces that are the units.

<RulerMeasure object="crayon" start={3} title="This crayon does not start at 0" subtitle="Read the start and the end. How long is the crayon really?" />

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why do rulers start at 0 and not at 1? Because before you have gone anywhere, you have measured *zero* units! The 1 is at the end of the first unit, not the beginning.

</Narrator>

<Section id="bending" kind="human" title="A wiggly worm is not shorter">

Take a piece of string and lay it straight next to a ruler. Now make it wiggly, like a worm. Its two ends are closer together — but is the **string** shorter?

No! Nothing was cut off. If you straighten it again it reaches exactly as far as before. **Bending a string changes where its ends are, not how long it is.** To measure a wiggly string, straighten it first, or measure each straight piece and add them up.

<Section id="time" kind="human" title="Telling the time">

We measure **time** with units too: seconds, minutes, **hours** and days. A clock counts them for us.

An analogue clock has two hands. The **short hand** points to the **hour**. The **long hand** shows the **minutes**: it goes once all the way round the clock every hour.

- At **3 o'clock** the long hand points straight up to 12, and the short hand points to 3. A digital clock shows **3:00**.
- At **half past 3** the long hand has gone halfway round and points down to 6. The short hand has moved halfway from 3 towards 4. A digital clock shows **3:30**: thirty minutes, half of the sixty minutes in an hour.

Timetables use the same times. Lena lives in a village in Switzerland. Her school bus leaves at **7:30**, half past 7, and arrives at school at **8:00**, 8 o'clock: the ride takes half an hour.

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's yes-or-no game">

Gideon reads a statement. Is it **true** or **false**?

1. "If the rope is longer than the stick, and the stick is longer than the pencil, then the rope is longer than the pencil." — **True**, always.
2. "Measuring with smaller clips gives a smaller number." — **False!** Smaller units give *bigger* numbers.
3. "If a pencil lies from 3 to 18 on a ruler, it is 18 centimetres long." — **False.** It is $18 - 3 = 15$ centimetres long. Reading the end number only works when the pencil starts at 0.
4. "A bent string is shorter than a straight one." — **False.** Bending moves the ends closer, but the string is just as long.
5. "Two half hours make one hour." — **True**, whatever time you start at.

<ForGrownUps title="the same game in Lean, a proof checker">

Lengths are whole numbers of units, so they are natural numbers (`Nat`), and "A is longer than B" is written `b < a`. The first statement is a library theorem; the string trick is a one-line rewrite.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**Starting to measure at 1.** The units are the *spaces* between the marks. The first centimetre runs from 0 to 1, so line the end of the object up with **0**. If it starts somewhere else, subtract the starting number.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Leaving gaps or letting units overlap.** Gaps make the number too small; overlaps make it too big. Lay each unit so it **touches** the one before, in a straight line.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A bent string is shorter."** Bending brings the ends closer together, but no string was cut off. Straighten it and it is as long as before.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A bigger number always means a longer thing."** Only when the unit is the same! "11 of Wei's feet" can be shorter than "9 of Lucía's feet", because Wei's feet are smaller. That is why people agreed on **standard units** like the centimetre.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Mixing up the hands of the clock.** The **short** hand shows the **hour**; the **long** hand shows the **minutes**. At half past 3 the short hand is between 3 and 4, not on 3 and not on 6.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Two ways of looking at measuring">

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="The scientist: every measurement is approximate">

A scientist knows that **no measurement is perfectly exact**. A pencil is never *exactly* 15 centimetres: with a better ruler you might find 15.2, and with a microscope 15.23. So scientists always say how precise a measurement is. To make measurements agree all over the world, everyone uses the same units: since 1983 the metre has been defined as the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299 792 458 of a second. Lengths that fall between whole units lead to <TopicLink id="math.number.fractions">fractions</TopicLink>, and good guesses lead to <TopicLink id="math.analysis.estimation">estimation</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="The geometer: length does not change when you move">

A geometer notices something we used all through this lesson: a stick has the **same length** wherever you carry it and however you turn it. That is what lets a string carry the length of the table to the door, and a ruler carry its centimetres to any object. Moves that keep lengths the same — slides, turns and flips — are the heart of geometry, as you can see in <TopicLink id="math.geom.shapes">Shapes</TopicLink>. Later, length leads on to area and angles in <TopicLink id="math.geom.area-angles">Area, perimeter and angles</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Measuring around the world, long ago">

- **Body units.** Many peoples measured with their bodies: the foot, the hand span, the pace, and the **cubit** — the length from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger. In ancient Egypt, builders used carefully made cubit rods, so that everyone's cubit was the same.
- **The Indus valley.** In cities of the Indus valley civilisation, more than 4,000 years ago, people used standard weights, and archaeologists have found measuring scales with fine marks, such as a piece of shell from Mohenjo-daro.
- **The metre.** In the 1790s, scientists in France defined the **metre** as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. The metric system is now used by almost every country in the world.
- **Time.** Shadow clocks and water clocks were used in ancient Egypt, Babylon, China and India. Dividing the hour into 60 minutes goes back to the Babylonian way of counting in sixties. In China, Su Song built a great water-powered astronomical clock tower in the 1090s, and in 1206 the engineer al-Jazarī, working in what is now Turkey, described ingenious water clocks in his *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*.
