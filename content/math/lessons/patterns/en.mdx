<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Thandi's necklace">

Thandi lives in Mpumalanga, South Africa. Her grandmother is an expert beadworker, and today she is teaching Thandi to make a necklace. "The best necklaces follow a pattern," says Gogo. Thandi threads the beads: **red, blue, green, red, blue, green, red, blue, green, …**

The necklace will have 100 beads. "What colour will the very last bead be?" asks Gogo.

Thandi doesn't want to thread 99 beads just to find out! Is there a way to **predict** it?

<Narrator who="chloe">

Hello! I'm Chloe. Patterns are everywhere — in beads, in songs, in the days of the week, and in numbers. Mathematicians love patterns because a pattern lets you know things before you see them. But we will also discover that patterns can play tricks on us!

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Patterns all around">

Some patterns **repeat** the same part again and again.

- A clapping game: *clap, clap, stamp, clap, clap, stamp, …*
- The days of the week: Monday, Tuesday, …, Sunday, and then Monday again.
- In many places: wet season, dry season, wet season, dry season, … In others: spring, summer, autumn, winter, and then spring again.

Other patterns **grow**: each step changes by the same rule.

- Shoes: 1 child has 2 shoes, 2 children have 4 shoes, 3 children have 6 shoes, … Each new child adds 2 shoes.
- A staircase of blocks: 1 block, then 2, then 3, then 4, … Each step adds 1 block.

To continue a pattern, you need to know its **rule**: what repeats, or how it grows.

</Section>

<Section id="big-ideas" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="repeating pattern">

A **repeating pattern** is made by repeating the same part again and again. The part that repeats is called the **unit**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="growing pattern">

A **growing pattern** changes by the same **rule** at each step, such as "add 2".

</Definition>

<Definition term="rule">

The **rule** of a pattern says exactly how to make every item: which unit repeats, or what happens at each step. Once the rule is stated, every item of the pattern is decided.

</Definition>

<Narrator who="gideon">

I'm Gideon. Look at the last big idea carefully. A pattern is not just the items you can see — it is the *rule*. Two patterns can look the same at the start and still be different patterns.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Example title="Predicting the 30th bead">

In Thandi's necklace the unit is red, blue, green — 3 beads. So every third bead is green: beads 3, 6, 9, 12, … Count in threes: *3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27, 30.* Bead 30 is green!

<Example title="The days of the week">

Today is Monday. What day will it be in 7 days? The week has 7 days, so 7 days later it is **Monday** again. In 14 days — two whole weeks — it is Monday again too. And in 8 days? One week and one more day: **Tuesday**.

</Example>

<Example title="Growing patterns and their rules">

- 2, 4, 6, 8, … The rule is **add 2**. Next come 10 and 12.
- 5, 10, 15, 20, … The rule is **add 5** — like counting fingers on hands. Next comes 25.
- 1, 3, 5, 7, … The rule is also **add 2**, but starting from 1. Next comes 9.

You can describe 2, 4, 6, 8, … in a second way: the 1st number is $2 \times 1$, the 2nd is $2 \times 2$, the 3rd is $2 \times 3$. So the 10th number is $2 \times 10 = 20$ — no need to list them all!

</Example>

<Section id="why" kind="human" title="Why predicting works — and when it doesn't">

**Why predicting works.** In Thandi's necklace, the unit has 3 beads. Each new unit starts with red, so the bead 3 places further on always has the same colour. Jump 3 beads, and you land on the same colour; jump 3 again, same colour again. So to find any bead, you only need to know where it sits **inside** its unit: first, second or third.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So that is the secret: in a repeating pattern, a big jump is just lots of jumps by one unit — and those don't change anything. Only the steps left over matter.

</Narrator>

**When it doesn't.** A few items can never tell you the rule for sure. 1, 2, 4 could go on with 8 or with 7 — or with something else. Even a long start can fool you! Draw points on a circle and join every pair with straight lines. With 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 points, the circle is cut into at most 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 pieces — it looks exactly like doubling. But with 6 points, the most you can get is **31**, not 32.

<ForGrownUps title="the mathematics of patterns">

A pattern is a function from positions $1, 2, 3, \dots$ to items. A repeating pattern with a unit of $k$ items is **periodic**: $p(n + k) = p(n)$ for every $n$. Applying this $q$ times gives $p(n + kq) = p(n)$ (induction on $q$). Dividing $n$ by $k$ with remainder, $n = kq + r$ with $0 \le r \lt k$, gives $p(n) = p(r)$, where position $0$ behaves like position $k$ (the last item of the unit). For the necklace, $100 = 3 \times 33 + 1$, so bead 100 matches bead 1: red. This is the beginning of <TopicLink id="math.number.modular-arithmetic">modular arithmetic</TopicLink>.

A growing pattern given by a step rule, $a_1 = 2$ and $a_{n+1} = a_n + 2$, and one given by a formula, $a_n = 2n$, are the same pattern: they agree at $n = 1$, and if they agree at $n$ they agree at $n + 1$ — a proof by induction. Formulas like "the $n$-th term is $2 \times n$" are children's first <TopicLink id="math.algebra.expressions-and-unknowns">algebraic expressions</TopicLink>.

No finite list of first terms determines a sequence: any finite start can be continued in infinitely many ways (for example, for any numbers $a_1, \dots, a_m$ and any next value $c$, there is a polynomial $P$ of degree at most $m$ with $P(i) = a_i$ for $i \le m$ and $P(m + 1) = c$). The circle example is **Moser's circle problem**: with $n$ points in general position the number of regions is $\binom{n}{4} + \binom{n}{2} + 1$, which gives $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 31, 57, \dots$. Philosophers have discussed the same issue: Ludwig Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) imagines a pupil who, told to "add 2", writes 1000, 1004, 1008, … and insists he is going on in the same way.

</ForGrownUps>

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon looks at Thandi's necklace: red, blue, green, red, blue, green, … True or false?

1. "Bead 4 and bead 7 are the same colour." — **True.** They are 3 beads apart, and the unit has 3 beads. Both are red.
2. "The unit of the necklace is one red bead." — **False.** The unit is red, blue, green. After red comes blue, not red.
3. "1, 2, 4, … must continue with 8." — **False!** Diego's rule continues with 7.
4. "In the pattern 2, 4, 6, 8, …, adding 2 each time, the 10th number is 20." — **True.** The $n$-th number is $2 \times n$.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Number 3 surprises people. To show "must continue with 8" is false, I only need one rule that fits 1, 2, 4 and does *not* give 8. Diego found one!

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"A pattern must go on the way I first guessed."** The first few items don't decide a pattern. 1, 2, 4 might continue with 8 or with 7. Always say your rule, and ask other people for theirs.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The unit is the first thing in the pattern."** The unit is the whole part that repeats. In ● ▲ ▲ ● ▲ ▲, the unit is ● ▲ ▲ — three items — not just ●.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"After the last item I can see, the pattern starts again."** Only if you have reached the end of a unit. In ● ▲ ▲ ● ▲, the next item is ▲, because the second unit is not finished yet.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Growing patterns always add the same number."** Many do, like 2, 4, 6, 8. But some grow in other ways: doubling gives 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and adding one more each time gives 1, 2, 4, 7, 11. The rule tells you which.

</Misconception>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="For grown-ups: other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Remainders and interlocking cycles">

The item of a repeating pattern depends only on the position modulo the length of the unit. Two repeating patterns running side by side return to their starting combination after the **least common multiple** of their unit lengths. The Maya *tzolk'in* combines 13 day numbers with 20 day names into a cycle of $260 = \operatorname{lcm}(13, 20)$ days. The traditional Chinese calendar combines 10 heavenly stems with 12 earthly branches into a cycle of $60 = \operatorname{lcm}(10, 12)$ years — not 120, because only half of the 120 possible pairs ever occur. See <TopicLink id="math.number.modular-arithmetic">clock arithmetic</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="A pattern is a symmetry">

A repeating strip pattern, such as a beaded band or a woven border, looks exactly the same after a **slide** (translation) by one unit: the pattern is invariant under a group of translations. Adding reflections, half-turns and glide reflections, mathematicians have proved that there are exactly **seven** types of symmetry for infinite strip patterns — the seven frieze groups — and all seven appear in textiles, pottery and architecture from many cultures. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.symmetry-transformations">symmetry: slides, turns and flips</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="logic" title="Seeing a pattern is not proving it">

Guessing a rule from examples is **induction** in the everyday sense, and it can fail, as Moser's circle shows. Mathematics turns a guess into knowledge by stating the rule and then proving facts about it — often by **mathematical induction**, as in the Lean proofs above. See <TopicLink id="math.found.proof-techniques">proof techniques</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Patterns around the world">

- **Kente** cloth, woven by Asante and Ewe weavers in Ghana and Togo, is made of narrow strips whose woven designs repeat along their length; each design has its own name.
- **Ndebele** artists in South Africa are famous for bold repeating patterns in beadwork and on painted house walls.
- Weavers in the **Andes** have made textiles with intricate repeating bands for thousands of years.
- Knitters on **Fair Isle** and elsewhere in Shetland, Scotland, are known for jumpers knitted with bands of small motifs that repeat all the way round.
- Calendars are patterns in time. The **Maya** *tzolk'in* repeats every 260 days, and the traditional **Chinese** calendar names years in a 60-year cycle, including the repeating 12 animals of the zodiac. The **seven-day week**, used around the world today, is very old.
- The English mathematician G. H. Hardy wrote in *A Mathematician's Apology* (1940): "A mathematician, like a painter or a poet, is a maker of patterns."
- Today, the **On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences**, founded by Neil Sloane, collects hundreds of thousands of number patterns — including several different ones that begin 1, 2, 4.

</Section>
