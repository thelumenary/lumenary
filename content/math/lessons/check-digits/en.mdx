<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A number that knows when it is wrong">

A librarian in Kampala types the 13-digit number from the back of a new book. A cashier in Lima scans a packet of rice; the scanner misreads one bar. A student in Seoul types her card number into a website and swaps two digits. In each case the computer answers at once: *that number is not valid — please check it.*

How can a computer know that a number is wrong without knowing what the right number was? The trick is to add one extra digit — a **check digit** — calculated from all the others. If any digit is typed wrongly, the calculation no longer comes out right. The calculation is clock arithmetic, from <TopicLink id="math.number.modular-arithmetic">the previous lesson</TopicLink>, and the reason it works is a small, beautiful theorem about the number 11.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="A digit that summarises the others">

Here is a first attempt. Add up the digits of a number, and choose one extra digit so that the total ends in $0$ — that is, so that the digit sum is a multiple of $10$. For the number $2\,7\,4\,1$ the digit sum is $14$, so we append $6$ and send $2\,7\,4\,1\,6$ (total $20$).

- If one digit is mistyped, say $2\,7\,\mathbf{9}\,1\,6$, the total becomes $25$. It no longer ends in $0$: **caught**. Changing one digit changes the total by a number between $-9$ and $9$ that is not $0$, so the total can never land on another multiple of $10$.
- If two neighbouring digits are swapped, $2\,\mathbf{4}\,\mathbf{7}\,1\,6$, the total is still $20$. A plain sum does not care about order: **missed**.

Swapping neighbours is one of the most common mistakes people make when they copy numbers. To catch it, the check must notice *where* each digit is. The idea: multiply each digit by a **weight** that depends on its position before adding up. Then moving a digit changes its weight, and so changes the total. But which weights, and which modulus? That is where the mathematics comes in.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="check digit">

A **check digit** is an extra digit computed from the other digits of a code number so that certain errors can be detected. A code number is **valid** if its check digit is the right one.

</Definition>

<Definition term="ISBN-10">

An **ISBN-10** (International Standard Book Number, used until 2006) has ten digits $d_1 d_2 \dots d_{10}$ with
$$
1 \cdot d_1 + 2 \cdot d_2 + 3 \cdot d_3 + \dots + 10 \cdot d_{10} \equiv 0 \pmod{11}.
$$
The check digit $d_{10}$ can be $10$, which is printed as **X** (the Roman numeral for ten).

</Definition>

Some books state the rule with the weights in the other order, $10 \cdot d_1 + 9 \cdot d_2 + \dots + 1 \cdot d_{10}$. The two rules accept exactly the same numbers, because the two sums add up to $11 \cdot (d_1 + \dots + d_{10})$, a multiple of 11.

<Definition term="ISBN-13">

An **ISBN-13** (used since 2007; the same system as the EAN-13 barcodes on shop goods) has thirteen digits with weights alternating $1, 3, 1, 3, \dots$:
$$
d_1 + 3 d_2 + d_3 + 3 d_4 + \dots + 3 d_{12} + d_{13} \equiv 0 \pmod{10}.
$$

</Definition>

<Definition term="parity bit">

A message of bits (0s and 1s) is protected by a **parity bit**: one extra bit chosen so that the total number of 1s is even. The receiver counts the 1s; an odd count means something went wrong.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Computing an ISBN-10 check digit">

The first nine digits of a sample ISBN are $3\text{-}16\text{-}148410$. The weighted sum of these nine is
$$
1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 6 + 4 \cdot 1 + 5 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 8 + 7 \cdot 4 + 8 \cdot 1 + 9 \cdot 0 = 131.
$$
We need $131 + 10 \cdot d_{10}$ to be a multiple of 11. Since $10 \equiv -1 \pmod{11}$, this says $131 - d_{10} \equiv 0$, so $d_{10} \equiv 131 \equiv 10 \pmod{11}$ (as $131 = 11 \cdot 11 + 10$). The check digit is $10$, printed **X**: the full ISBN is **3-16-148410-X**. Check: $131 + 100 = 231 = 21 \cdot 11$. ✓

In general, **the ISBN-10 check digit is the weighted sum of the first nine digits, modulo 11.**

</Example>

<Example title="Verifying an ISBN-13">

For **978-3-16-148410-0**:
$$
9 + 3 \cdot 7 + 8 + 3 \cdot 3 + 1 + 3 \cdot 6 + 1 + 3 \cdot 4 + 8 + 3 \cdot 4 + 1 + 3 \cdot 0 + 0 = 100,
$$
a multiple of 10. Valid. If the librarian types 978-3-16-148**7**10-0 instead (a $4$ in a weight-3 position became $7$), the sum grows by $3 \cdot 3 = 9$ to $109$, which is not a multiple of 10: **caught**.

</Example>

<Example title="A swap that ISBN-13 cannot see">

Swap the neighbouring digits $1$ and $6$ of 978-3-16-148410-0 to get 978-3-61-148410-0. Positions 5 and 6 have weights $1$ and $3$, so their contribution changes from $1 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 6 = 19$ to $1 \cdot 6 + 3 \cdot 1 = 9$: down by $10$. The total becomes $90$, still a multiple of 10. The wrong number is accepted! The same swap in the ISBN-10, 3-**61**-148410-X, is caught: its sum is $226 = 20 \cdot 11 + 6$.

</Example>

<Example title="A parity bit">

The 7-bit message $1011001$ contains four 1s, an even number, so its parity bit is $0$ and we send $1011001\mathbf{0}$. If noise flips one bit and $10100010$ arrives, it has three 1s — odd — and the receiver asks for the message again. But if two bits flip, say $00100011$, the count is again even and the damage goes unnoticed.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="What each scheme catches, and why">

All the proofs below use one observation: **if two code numbers are both valid, the difference of their check sums is a multiple of the modulus.** So to show that an error is always caught, we show that it changes the check sum by something that is *not* a multiple of the modulus.

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (ISBN-10 catches every single-digit error)">

If one digit of a valid ISBN-10 is changed, the result is not a valid ISBN-10.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose the digit in position $k$ changes from $x$ to $y \ne x$. Only one term of the check sum changes, from $k x$ to $k y$, so the sum changes by $k (y - x)$. If the new number were valid too, 11 would divide $k(y - x)$. Now $1 \le k \le 10$ and $1 \le |y - x| \le 10$, so neither factor is a multiple of 11. Because 11 is **prime**, it divides a product only if it divides one of the factors (Euclid's lemma). So 11 does not divide $k(y - x)$, and the error is caught. (Without Euclid's lemma: the multiplication table modulo 11 has no $0$ outside row $0$ and column $0$ — every non-zero row is a shuffle, as you can check in the explorer below.)

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (ISBN-10 catches every swap of neighbours)">

If two different neighbouring digits of a valid ISBN-10 are swapped, the result is not a valid ISBN-10.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let the neighbours be $x$ in position $k$ and $y$ in position $k + 1$, with $x \ne y$. Their contribution to the sum is $k x + (k + 1) y$ before the swap and $k y + (k + 1) x$ after. The difference is
$$
\big(kx + (k+1)y\big) - \big(ky + (k+1)x\big) = y - x,
$$
and $1 \le |y - x| \le 10$, so it is not a multiple of 11. The swap is caught.

</Proof>

In fact ISBN-10 catches the swap of *any* two different digits, neighbours or not: swapping positions $j$ and $k$ changes the sum by $(k - j)(x - y)$, and the argument of Theorem 1 applies to this product.

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (ISBN-13 catches every single-digit error)">

If one digit of a valid ISBN-13 is changed, the result is not a valid ISBN-13.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Changing $x$ to $y \ne x$ changes the sum by $y - x$ (weight 1) or by $3(y - x)$ (weight 3), where $1 \le |y - x| \le 9$. In the first case the change is not a multiple of 10. In the second case, suppose $3(y - x)$ were a multiple of 10. Then so would be $7 \cdot 3 (y - x) = 21(y - x) = 20 (y - x) + (y - x)$, and hence $y - x$ itself — impossible. (We used that $7$ is the partner of $3$ modulo $10$: $3 \cdot 7 = 21 \equiv 1$.)

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (the ISBN-13 blind spot)">

Swapping two neighbouring digits $a \ne b$ of a valid ISBN-13 gives another valid ISBN-13 exactly when $a$ and $b$ differ by $5$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Neighbouring positions have weights $1$ and $3$ (in some order). Say $a$ has weight 1 and $b$ weight 3. The contribution changes from $a + 3b$ to $b + 3a$, a change of $2(a - b)$. This is a multiple of 10 exactly when $a - b$ is a multiple of 5, that is (for digits $a \ne b$) when $|a - b| = 5$. The other order of weights gives the change $2(b - a)$ and the same conclusion.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (parity bits)">

A parity bit detects every error in which an odd number of bits are flipped, and no error in which an even number are flipped.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Flipping one bit changes the number of 1s by exactly one (up or down), so it changes the count from even to odd or from odd to even. Flipping $m$ bits changes the parity $m$ times. The sent word has an even number of 1s; the received word therefore has an odd number of 1s exactly when $m$ is odd. The receiver rejects exactly those words.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Look at what made ISBN-10 so good: the weights $1, \dots, 10$ are all different modulo 11, none of them is $0$, and 11 is prime. ISBN-13 works modulo 10, which is not prime — and the weak spot is exactly the factor 5 of 10.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="correct" kind="human" title="Detecting is not correcting">

When a number fails the test, can the computer work out what it should have been? With one check digit, **no**. Suppose 3-**2**6-148410-X arrives. It fails the ISBN-10 test (its sum is $233 \equiv 2 \pmod{11}$). It could be 3-16-148410-X with the second digit mistyped — but it could equally be 1-26-148410-X with the *first* digit mistyped, and that is also a valid ISBN-10. In general, for an invalid number, almost every position has some digit that would make it valid again.

Mathematicians measure this with the **Hamming distance**: the number of positions in which two code numbers differ. Theorem 1 says any two valid ISBN-10s are at distance at least 2. That is enough to *notice* one error, but a damaged number can sit at distance 1 from two valid ones, so there is no way to choose. To *correct* one error, valid code numbers must be at distance at least 3 from each other. Codes with that property exist — Richard Hamming and Marcel Golay published early examples around 1950 — but they need more than one check symbol (see <TopicLink id="math.applied.information-theory">information theory</TopicLink>).

</Section>

<Section id="luhn" kind="human" title="Bank cards and phones: Luhn's scheme">

Bank card numbers, and the 15-digit IMEI number that identifies every mobile phone, end in a check digit computed by the **Luhn scheme**, patented by Hans Peter Luhn of IBM (filed 1954). Reading from the **right**, leave the last digit alone, double the next one, leave the next, double the next, and so on; whenever doubling gives a two-digit number, add its digits ($2 \cdot 7 = 14 \to 1 + 4 = 5$). The number is valid if the total is a multiple of 10.

<Example title="The Luhn test">

For $7\,9\,9\,2\,7\,3\,9\,8\,7\,1\,3$, from the right: $3$; $1 \to 2$; $7$; $8 \to 16 \to 7$; $9$; $3 \to 6$; $7$; $2 \to 4$; $9$; $9 \to 18 \to 9$; $7$. The total is $3 + 2 + 7 + 7 + 9 + 6 + 7 + 4 + 9 + 9 + 7 = 70$: valid.

</Example>

Doubling-and-adding-digits sends the digits $0, 1, 2, \dots, 9$ to $0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9$ — a shuffle, with no two digits landing on the same value. That is why Luhn's scheme catches every single-digit error. Neighbour swaps are caught too, with exactly one exception: swapping $0$ and $9$ ($09 \leftrightarrow 90$) leaves the total unchanged. Neither ISBN-13 nor Luhn catches swaps of digits *two* places apart, because those positions get the same treatment; ISBN-10 does.

</Section>

<Section id="explore" kind="visualization" title="Explore: good weights and parity">

A weight $w$ catches every single-digit error modulo $m$ exactly when the row of $w$ in the multiplication table modulo $m$ is a shuffle (no repeated values). <Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**“A check digit can correct the error.”** A single check digit only *detects* errors. As the example 3-26-148410-X shows, a damaged number can be one digit away from several valid numbers, so there is no way to tell which digit was wrong. Correcting errors needs more redundancy.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“Every possible error is detected.”** Each scheme catches certain kinds of errors and misses others. ISBN-13 misses neighbour swaps of digits differing by 5; Luhn misses $09 \leftrightarrow 90$; even ISBN-10 misses some errors that change two digits at once, such as a damaged number that happens to be another valid ISBN. A valid check digit means “no *likely* error”, not “certainly correct”.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“The X in an ISBN is a misprint.”** In ISBN-10 the check digit is a remainder modulo 11, so it can be $10$, which is written X. ISBN-13 works modulo 10 and never needs an X.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“The more bits go wrong, the more likely a parity bit is to notice.”** Not at all: a second flipped bit undoes the effect of the first on the count, so two flipped bits are never noticed. On a very noisy line a large share of the damaged words slip through, as the simulation above shows.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Why 11 — and what Verhoeff did instead">

A weighted scheme modulo $m$ catches every single error when each weight has a partner modulo $m$, and every neighbour swap when each *difference* of neighbouring weights has a partner. Modulo 10 that is impossible: the weights with partners are $1, 3, 7, 9$, all odd, so the difference of two of them is even, and an even difference times $a - b = 5$ is a multiple of 10. **No weighted scheme modulo 10 can catch all single errors and all neighbour swaps.** ISBN-10 escapes by working modulo the prime 11, at the price of the extra symbol X. In 1969 the Dutch mathematician Jacobus Verhoeff found a cleverer escape: he replaced addition modulo 10 by the multiplication table of the **dihedral group** of order 10 — the ten rigid motions of a regular pentagon (five turns and five flips) — combined with a shuffle of the digits. Because that group is not commutative ($a \ast b \ne b \ast a$ in general), it notices order, and Verhoeff's scheme catches every single error and every neighbour swap using the digits $0$–$9$ only. India's Aadhaar identity numbers end in a Verhoeff check digit. See <TopicLink id="math.algebra.groups">groups</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">rigid motions</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="combinatorics" title="Codes as points at a distance">

Think of all strings of 10 digits as points, and say two strings are at **distance** $d$ when they differ in $d$ positions (the Hamming distance). The valid ISBN-10s are a set of points no two of which are at distance 1: around each valid code is a “ball” of the strings one error away, containing no other valid code. Detecting one error needs minimum distance 2; correcting one error needs minimum distance 3, so that the balls of radius 1 do not overlap. Counting points in balls gives limits on how many valid codes there can be — the start of coding theory.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="How likely is an undetected error?">

If a number is damaged in a completely random way, its check sum modulo 11 is essentially a random remainder, so it passes the ISBN-10 test with probability about $\tfrac{1}{11}$. But real errors are not random: studies of people copying numbers, such as Verhoeff's, found that single-digit errors are by far the most common, followed by neighbour swaps. A good scheme is designed for the errors people actually make — and for those, ISBN-10 is perfect.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **1948–1950.** Claude Shannon's theory of communication (1948) showed that errors can in principle be corrected efficiently; Marcel Golay (1949) and Richard Hamming (1950), both working in the USA, published some of the first error-*correcting* codes.
- **1954.** Hans Peter Luhn of IBM filed a patent for a device that computes his check digit (granted in 1960). His scheme now protects bank cards worldwide and the IMEI numbers of mobile phones.
- **1966–1970.** A 9-digit Standard Book Numbering system was devised in the United Kingdom in 1966 and became the international ISBN-10 standard (ISO 2108) in 1970.
- **1969.** Jacobus Verhoeff in the Netherlands published his dihedral-group scheme, together with a study of the errors people make when copying numbers.
- **1974.** The first product with a Universal Product Code barcode (weights $3, 1$ modulo 10) was scanned in a supermarket in Ohio, USA. The European Article Number (EAN-13) followed and spread around the world.
- **2007.** Books switched to 13-digit ISBNs, identical to EAN-13 barcodes with the prefix 978 or 979.
- **Today.** International bank account numbers (IBANs), used in dozens of countries, carry two check digits computed modulo 97; India's Aadhaar numbers use Verhoeff's scheme.

</Section>
