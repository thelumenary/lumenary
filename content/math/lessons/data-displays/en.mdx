<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A headline and a pile of cubes">

Two schools in the same town had a paper-recycling competition. Riverside School collected **52 kg** of paper and Hilltop School collected **48 kg**. The next day the Riverside newsletter printed a bar chart under the headline *"Riverside crushes Hilltop!"* — and in the chart, Riverside's bar was one and a half times as tall as Hilltop's.

Was it really a crushing win? The numbers are 52 and 48. Something about the chart was not telling the truth.

Meanwhile, four friends — Ana, Ben, Chen and Dina — have built towers of cubes: 4, 6, 8 and 2 cubes tall. They decide to share the cubes **fairly**, so that everyone has the same. How many cubes will each of them get?

This lesson is about pictures of numbers: how to read them, how they can fool us, and what the "average" of some numbers really is.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Read the labels first">

A data display is a picture of numbers. Before you look at the bars or dots, read the words and numbers around them:

1. **The title:** what was counted or measured?
2. **The labels:** what does each bar or dot stand for?
3. **The scale:** how much is each step on the axis worth — 1, 2, 5, 10? And does the axis start at 0?

Only then compare the bars.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Bars are lengths, and lengths are easy to compare by eye. That's why bar charts are useful — and why a bar chart with a strange scale can trick our eyes so easily.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Words we need">

<Definition term="bar graph">

A **bar graph** shows each category as a bar. The length of the bar shows the count or amount for that category, measured against the axis.

</Definition>

<Definition term="scale">

The **scale** of an axis says how many items (or how much) each step along the axis stands for. If each grid line is worth 5, a bar that reaches the fourth grid line stands for $4 \times 5 = 20$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="line plot">

A **line plot** (or dot plot) draws a number line and puts one dot (or cross) above the line for every value in the data. Stacks of dots show which values are common. Measurements in halves or quarters of a unit fit on a number line marked in halves or quarters.

</Definition>

<Definition term="mean">

The **mean** of a list of numbers is their total divided by how many numbers there are. It is the amount each one would have if the total were **shared out equally**.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Reading displays">

<Example title="A bar graph with a scale">

Ms Wanjiku's class in Kisumu asked: *How do you come to school?* On their bar graph each grid line stands for **2** children.

- The "walk" bar reaches the 6th grid line: $6 \times 2 = 12$ children walk.
- The "bus" bar stops halfway between the lines for 6 and 8: that is **7** children.
- The "bicycle" bar reaches the line for 4, and the "car" bar stops halfway between the lines for 2 and 4: **3** children.

How many more children walk than come by bus? $12 - 7 = 5$. How many children answered? Every child gave exactly one answer, so we add the bars: $12 + 7 + 4 + 3 = 26$.

</Example>

<Example title="A line plot of measurements">

Kenji's group grew bean seedlings and measured them to the nearest quarter of a centimetre:

$2\tfrac14,\ 2\tfrac12,\ 2\tfrac12,\ 2\tfrac34,\ 3\tfrac14,\ 3\tfrac12,\ 3\tfrac12,\ 3\tfrac34$ cm.

On a line plot marked in quarters, $2\tfrac12$ and $3\tfrac12$ each have two dots. The tallest seedling is $3\tfrac34 - 2\tfrac14 = 1\tfrac12$ cm taller than the shortest. If all eight seedlings were laid end to end they would stretch $24$ cm.

</Example>

<Theorem name="Raised axes exaggerate">

If the axis of a bar graph starts above zero, the bigger of two bars looks **relatively bigger** than it really is: the ratio of the drawn bars is larger than the ratio of the numbers.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Starting the axis at 40 is like chopping 40 kg off the bottom of *both* bars. Both lose the same amount, but that amount is a bigger share of the shorter bar. From 48 we keep only 8; from 52 we keep 12. The short bar has lost $\frac{40}{48}$ of itself, the tall bar only $\frac{40}{52}$, so what is left of the short bar is much shorter compared with what is left of the tall bar. The higher the axis starts, the worse it gets: start at 47, and the bars are 5 and 1 — Riverside looks five times as big!

</Proof>

Other ways a display can mislead: an axis whose steps are not evenly spaced, pictures of different sizes in a pictograph, a missing scale, or a missing title. A careful reader always checks.

<Note>

Starting an axis above zero is not always dishonest. For a temperature graph or a line graph of a child's height over the years, zooming in can show small changes clearly. But for **bar graphs**, where we compare the lengths of the bars, the axis should start at zero — or the graph must say very clearly that it does not.

</Note>

<Section id="fair-share" kind="proof" title="The mean as a fair share">

Back to the four towers: Ana 4, Ben 6, Chen 8, Dina 2. To share fairly, move cubes one at a time from tall towers to short towers:

- Ben gives Ana one cube: 5, 5, 8, 2.
- Chen gives Dina one cube, three times: 5, 5, 7, 3, then 5, 5, 6, 4, then **5, 5, 5, 5**.

Everyone now has 5. Notice: moving a cube never changes the **total** (20 cubes) or the **number of towers** (4). So when the towers are level, there must be $20 \div 4 = 5$ in each. That is the mean.

<DataChart data={[{ label: "Ana", value: 4 }, { label: "Ben", value: 6 }, { label: "Chen", value: 8 }, { label: "Dina", value: 2 }]} view="fair" showMean={true} title="Share the cubes fairly" />

<Theorem name="Mean × number of values = total">

If towers are levelled by moving cubes from tower to tower, then

$$
\text{level} \times \text{number of towers} = \text{total}.
$$

So the fair share is always total ÷ number of towers, whichever moves are made.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Each move takes one cube off one tower and puts it on another, so the total stays the same and the number of towers stays the same. At the end, every tower has the same height — the level. Level towers hold level × number of towers cubes, and this must still equal the total we started with.

</Proof>

Some surprising facts follow:

- **The mean need not be one of the values.** The towers were 4, 6, 8 and 2; the mean is 5, which nobody started with.
- **The mean need not be a whole number.** Towers of 3, 7, 2 and 6 hold 18 cubes. $18 \div 4 = 4\tfrac12$: you could only level them by cutting a cube in half. The bean seedlings above have mean $24 \div 8 = 3$ cm, and measurements in quarters can easily have a mean like $2\tfrac78$ cm.
- **The mean lies between the smallest and the largest value.** If the level were taller than the tallest tower, the level towers would hold more cubes than all the towers had at the start — but sharing never makes new cubes. In the same way, the level can never be shorter than the shortest tower.
- **If you know the mean and how many values there are, you know the total.** If five friends have a mean of 8 stickers, they have $5 \times 8 = 40$ stickers altogether.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So the mean is not "the middle number" or "the most common number". It is the *fair share*: what everyone would get if we put everything in one pile and shared it out equally.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon only plays with statements that are exactly true or exactly false. Decide, and give a reason.

1. "In the newsletter chart, Riverside's bar is 1.5 times as tall, so Riverside collected 1.5 times as much paper." — **False.** 52 kg is less than 1.1 times 48 kg. The axis started at 40.
2. "Moving cubes from one tower to another can change the total." — **False.** One tower loses exactly what another gains.
3. "The mean of 4, 6, 8 and 2 is one of those numbers." — **False.** It is 5.
4. "The mean of some towers can be taller than the tallest tower." — **False.** Level towers that tall would need more cubes than there are.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Statement 4 is about *every* possible set of towers — infinitely many! Checking examples cannot settle it. But the reason — "level towers taller than the tallest tower would need more cubes than there are" — works for all of them at once. That is a proof.

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**Reading bar heights without checking the scale.** A bar that reaches the 4th grid line stands for 4 only if each step is worth 1. If each step is worth 5, it stands for 20. Two graphs with different scales cannot be compared bar against bar.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Twice as tall means twice as much" — whatever the axis.** That is only true when the axis starts at zero. With the axis starting at 40, a bar for 52 looks one and a half times as tall as a bar for 48.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The mean must be one of the data values."** The mean of 4, 6, 8 and 2 is 5, which is not in the list. The mean can even be a fraction, like $4\tfrac12$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The mean is the middle number."** The middle value of a sorted list is called the *median*, and it is often different from the mean. For 1, 2 and 9 the middle number is 2, but the fair share is $12 \div 3 = 4$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="The mean is the balance point">

Imagine the line plot as a light ruler with a small equal weight at the place of every dot. Where must you put your finger so that the ruler balances? Exactly at the **mean**. Physicists call this the *centre of mass*. For the values 2 and 8, the balance point is at 5; add a third weight at 8, and the balance point moves to 6, because $(2 + 8 + 8) \div 3 = 6$. The heavier side pulls the balance point towards it — just as one very large value pulls the mean towards it.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="A sample is not the whole story">

Ms Wanjiku's class is only one class. If we asked another class, or the whole school, the bars would come out a little different. Statisticians study how much a **sample** can differ from the whole **population**, and how sure we can be about the whole from a part — see <TopicLink id="math.prob.statistical-inference-intro">sampling and statistical reasoning</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Categories and totals">

When every child gives exactly one answer, the categories split the class into groups with nobody left out and nobody in two groups. In the language of <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">sets</TopicLink>, the categories form a **partition** of the class. That is why the bar heights add up to the number of children — a rule from <TopicLink id="math.prob.data-and-tallies">data, tallies and picture graphs</TopicLink>. If children could choose two answers ("bus *and* walk"), the bars would add up to more than the number of children.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- In the Andes of South America, Inca record-keepers stored counts of people, animals and goods on **khipu**, cords with knots — data recorded long before paper charts.
- The Scottish engineer **William Playfair** published some of the first bar charts and line graphs in *The Commercial and Political Atlas* (1786), showing trade between countries; he later introduced the pie chart (1801).
- **Florence Nightingale** used her "rose" diagrams (1858) to show that more soldiers in the Crimean War died from disease in badly run hospitals than from their wounds, and helped to change how hospitals were run.
- For the 1900 World's Fair in Paris, the African American scholar **W. E. B. Du Bois** and his students at Atlanta University made striking hand-drawn charts about the lives of Black Americans.
- In 1954 **Darrell Huff** wrote *How to Lie with Statistics*, a funny little book that warned readers about tricks such as bar charts with axes that do not start at zero.

</Section>
