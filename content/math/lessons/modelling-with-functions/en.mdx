<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Three questions, three functions">

- A taxi company in Nairobi charges a fixed fee plus an amount per kilometre. What will a $12$ km trip cost?
- A bus driver in Mumbai sees a child step into the road. How far does the bus travel before it stops, and how much farther at twice the speed?
- A family in Lima saves in an account that pays interest every year. When will their savings double?

Each question is about the real world, and each has an answer that comes from a **function**: linear for the taxi, quadratic for the stopping distance, exponential for the savings. But no formula *is* a taxi, a bus or a bank account. To get from the situation to a formula we must **simplify**: decide what matters, what to ignore and what to assume. To trust the answer we must **check** it against reality. This lesson is about that whole journey, which is called **mathematical modelling**.

</Section>

1. **Simplify.** Decide which features matter and state your **assumptions** ("the fare is the same at every time of day", "the road is dry", "the interest rate stays the same").
2. **Formulate.** Choose variables and a function that links them.
3. **Solve.** Do the mathematics: evaluate, solve an equation, find a maximum.
4. **Interpret.** Translate the answer back: units, sensible ranges, what it means.
5. **Validate.** Compare predictions with observations the model was *not* built from. If they disagree, revise the assumptions and go round again.

The statistician George Box put it memorably in 1979: "all models are wrong, but some are useful". A model leaves things out on purpose, and it is judged by whether it serves its purpose, not by whether it is "true".

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="mathematical model">

A **mathematical model** is a simplified mathematical description of a real situation, *together with its assumptions*. It relates **variables** (quantities that change, such as time or distance) through a function whose **parameters** (constants such as a rate or a starting value) are chosen to suit the situation.

</Definition>

<Definition term="fit, residual and validation">

**Fitting** a model means choosing its parameters to match data. The **residual** of a data point is observed value minus predicted value. **Validation** compares a model's predictions with observations, preferably ones not used in fitting.

</Definition>

<Definition term="interpolation and extrapolation">

Using a model *inside* the range of the data used to build it is **interpolation**; using it *outside* that range is **extrapolation**.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="fingerprints" kind="proof" title="Fingerprints of three families">

Suppose a quantity is measured at equally spaced times $0, 1, 2, \dots$, giving values $u_0, u_1, u_2, \dots$ How can a table tell you which kind of function to try?

<Theorem name="Constant differences mean linear">

If $u_{n+1} - u_n = d$ for every $n$, then $u_n = u_0 + nd$ for every $n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Induction: true for $n = 0$, and if $u_n = u_0 + nd$ then $u_{n+1} = u_n + d = u_0 + (n + 1)d$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Constant ratios mean exponential">

If $u_{n+1} = b\,u_n$ for every $n$, then $u_n = u_0\, b^n$ for every $n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Induction: $u_{n+1} = b\,u_n = b\,u_0 b^n = u_0 b^{n+1}$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Constant second differences mean quadratic">

If the differences of the differences are constant, $u_{n+2} - 2u_{n+1} + u_n = c$ for every $n$, then

$$
u_n = u_0 + n\,(u_1 - u_0) + c\,\frac{n(n-1)}{2},
$$

a quadratic function of $n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

We prove the formula for $n$ and for $n + 1$ together, by induction on $n$. For $n = 0$ both are immediate. If the formula holds for $u_n$ and $u_{n+1}$, then $u_{n+2} = c + 2u_{n+1} - u_n$, and substituting the two formulas and simplifying gives the formula for $n + 2$.

</Proof>

<Example title="Reading a table">

| $n$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 7 | 10 | 13 | 16 | 19 |
| B | 2 | 5 | 10 | 17 | 26 |
| C | 3 | 6 | 12 | 24 | 48 |

Column A has constant differences $3$: linear, $u_n = 7 + 3n$. Column B has differences $3, 5, 7, 9$ and constant second differences $2$: quadratic, $u_n = 2 + 3n + n(n - 1) = n^2 + 2n + 2$. Column C has constant ratio $2$: exponential, $u_n = 3 \cdot 2^n$. Real data are noisy, so in practice you look for differences or ratios that are *roughly* constant.

</Example>

<Theorem name="Two points determine a line">

If $x_1 \ne x_2$, there is exactly one line $y = mx + c$ through $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$, namely $m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$ and $c = y_1 - m x_1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

These values work: $mx_1 + c = y_1$, and $mx_2 + c = y_1 + m(x_2 - x_1) = y_2$. If another line $m'x + c'$ passes through both points, subtracting the two equations gives $(m - m')(x_1 - x_2) = 0$, so $m = m'$, and then $c = c'$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Doubling time">

A quantity $A e^{rt}$ with $A > 0$ and continuous growth rate $r > 0$ doubles in time $T = \dfrac{\ln 2}{r} \approx \dfrac{0.693}{r}$, whatever its starting value. With yearly compounding at rate $r$ the doubling time is $\dfrac{\ln 2}{\ln(1 + r)}$, which is at least as long.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$Ae^{rT} = 2A \iff e^{rT} = 2 \iff rT = \ln 2$. For yearly compounding, $(1 + r)^T = 2 \iff T = \tfrac{\ln 2}{\ln(1 + r)}$. Since $1 + r \le e^r$, we have $\ln(1 + r) \le r$, and dividing $\ln 2$ by a smaller positive number gives a larger answer.

</Proof>

At $r = 7\%$ the doubling time is about $0.693 / 0.07 \approx 9.9$ years. This is the "rule of 70": divide $70$ by the percentage rate.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Building models">

<Example title="Linear: the taxi fare (illustrative numbers)">

Suppose a $4$ km trip costs $500$ shillings and a $10$ km trip costs $950$. Assuming the fare is a fixed fee plus a constant amount per kilometre, the fare is linear in the distance $x$. By the two-point theorem the slope is $m = \tfrac{950 - 500}{10 - 4} = 75$ shillings per km, and the intercept is $c = 500 - 75 \cdot 4 = 200$ shillings. So $F(x) = 200 + 75x$, and a $12$ km trip costs $F(12) = 1100$ shillings.

*Interpret:* $75$ is the price per kilometre and $200$ is the fixed fee. $F(0) = 200$ is the price of getting in and going nowhere. The model is meaningful only for $x \ge 0$, and it ignores waiting time, surge pricing and tolls.

</Example>

<Example title="Quadratic: stopping distance">

A vehicle's stopping distance has two parts. During the driver's **reaction time** $t_r$ the vehicle keeps its speed $v$ and travels $v\,t_r$. While **braking** with constant deceleration $a$ it travels $\tfrac{v^2}{2a}$. With the assumptions $t_r = 1$ s and $a = 7\ \text{m/s}^2$ (a dry road),

$$
d(v) = v + \frac{v^2}{14} \quad \text{metres, for } v \text{ in m/s}.
$$

At $50$ km/h ($13.9$ m/s), $d \approx 13.9 + 13.8 = 27.7$ m. At $100$ km/h ($27.8$ m/s), $d \approx 27.8 + 55.1 = 82.9$ m: doubling the speed doubles the reaction distance but **quadruples** the braking distance. On a wet road ($a \approx 4\ \text{m/s}^2$, say) the braking distance would be much longer. Changing an assumption changes the prediction, and a good modeller reports both.

</Example>

<Example title="Exponential: savings and a warning from biology">

Savings growing at $5\%$ a year follow $S(t) = S_0 \cdot 1.05^t$ and double in $\tfrac{\ln 2}{\ln 1.05} \approx 14.2$ years.

Exponential models can be spectacularly right for a while and then spectacularly wrong. In ideal laboratory conditions a bacterium such as *E. coli* can divide about every $20$ minutes. If that continued for $48$ hours, one cell would become $2^{144} \approx 2 \times 10^{43}$ cells, weighing far more than the Earth. In reality nutrients run out and growth slows. A better model then is the **logistic** model, in which the growth rate falls as the population approaches a limit (see <TopicLink id="math.applied.mathematical-biology">mathematical biology</TopicLink>).

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="fitting" kind="proof" title="Fitting a line to data: least squares">

Real data never lie exactly on a line. Which line fits "best"? The standard answer, due to Legendre and Gauss, is the line that makes the **sum of squared residuals**

$$
S(m, c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - (m x_i + c)\big)^2
$$

as small as possible. Squaring treats overshoots and undershoots alike and punishes large misses heavily.

<Theorem name="The best intercept is set by the means">

Fix a slope $m$ and put $r_i = y_i - m x_i$, with mean $\bar r = \tfrac1n \sum r_i = \bar y - m \bar x$. Then for every $c$,

$$
\sum_{i} (r_i - c)^2 = \sum_{i} (r_i - \bar r)^2 + n\,(c - \bar r)^2.
$$

So, for a given slope, the sum of squared residuals is smallest exactly when $c = \bar y - m\bar x$: the best line passes through the point of means $(\bar x, \bar y)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $r_i - c = (r_i - \bar r) + (\bar r - c)$ and expand:

$$
\sum (r_i - c)^2 = \sum (r_i - \bar r)^2 + 2(\bar r - c)\sum (r_i - \bar r) + n(\bar r - c)^2.
$$

The middle sum is $\sum r_i - n\bar r = 0$: deviations from the mean add up to zero. The last term is never negative and is zero only when $c = \bar r$.

</Proof>

Now substitute $c = \bar y - m\bar x$. Writing $S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2$, $S_{xy} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)$ and $S_{yy} = \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2$, the sum of squares becomes

$$
\sum \big((y_i - \bar y) - m(x_i - \bar x)\big)^2 = S_{yy} - 2m S_{xy} + m^2 S_{xx},
$$

a quadratic in $m$ with positive leading coefficient $S_{xx}$ (if the $x_i$ are not all equal). By the vertex formula of the <TopicLink id="math.algebra.quadratics">quadratics</TopicLink> lesson, its minimum is at

$$
m = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad c = \bar y - m \bar x.
$$

That is the **least-squares line**. To fit an exponential $P = A e^{rt}$, take logarithms: $\ln P = \ln A + rt$ is linear in $t$, so fit a line to the points $(t, \ln P)$. This minimises errors on the logarithmic scale, which means roughly *relative* errors.

</Section>

<Section id="validation" kind="human" title="Validation: where models fail">

A model earns trust by predicting what it was not built from. Three warnings:

- **Fitting the past is not forecasting the future.** In the computation section, a linear and an exponential model both fit world population for 1950–1980 closely, yet their forecasts for 2020 differ by more than $2.5$ billion people, and both miss.
- **A perfect fit proves nothing.** Through any $n$ data points with different $x$-values there is a polynomial of degree less than $n$ passing *exactly* through all of them. A model with as many parameters as data points will always fit perfectly, and it typically forecasts nonsense. This is called **overfitting**.
- **Data cannot decide extrapolation.** The theorem below shows that infinitely many models agree exactly with any finite set of data and yet predict *any* value you like at a new point. What justifies an extrapolation is not the data alone but the *reasons* behind the model: a mechanism (such as Newton's law of cooling) or a well-tested assumption.

<Theorem name="Extrapolation is not determined by data">

Let $f$ be any model and $x_1, \dots, x_n$ the points where we have data. For every number $k$, the model

$$
g(x) = f(x) + k\,(x - x_1)(x - x_2)\cdots(x - x_n)
$$

agrees with $f$ at every data point. At any new point $x_0$ (different from all $x_i$), choosing $k = \dfrac{v - f(x_0)}{(x_0 - x_1)\cdots(x_0 - x_n)}$ makes $g(x_0) = v$, for any value $v$ we like.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

At $x = x_i$ the product contains the factor $x_i - x_i = 0$, so $g(x_i) = f(x_i)$. At $x_0$ the product is a product of non-zero numbers, hence non-zero, so we may divide by it; substituting the chosen $k$ gives $g(x_0) = f(x_0) + (v - f(x_0)) = v$.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Treating a model's output as fact.** "The model says the population will be $9.5$ billion in 2020" is a statement about the model's *assumptions* (here, a constant growth rate), not about the world. Always report predictions together with the assumptions they rest on.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Choosing the model that best fits past data, whatever it is.** A model with enough parameters can fit any data perfectly, and the fit says nothing about its forecasts. Prefer models with a plausible mechanism and few parameters, and test them on data they were not fitted to.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A good fit inside the data means it is safe to extrapolate far outside."** Extrapolation is where models most often fail. The data constrain a model only where the data are, and the extrapolation theorem shows they cannot pin down predictions anywhere else.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Exponential growth can go on for ever."** Every real exponential process eventually meets a limit — food, space, money or people. The exponential model describes a phase, not a destiny.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Least squares is a perpendicular">

List the observations as one vector $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_n)$ in $n$-dimensional space. The predictions of all lines, $(mx_1 + c, \dots, mx_n + c)$, form a plane through the origin, spanned by $\mathbf{1} = (1, \dots, 1)$ and $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$. The least-squares line picks the point of this plane *closest* to $\mathbf y$, which is the foot of the perpendicular from $\mathbf y$ to the plane. Perpendicularity to $\mathbf 1$ says the residuals add up to zero (the "means" theorem above); perpendicularity to $\mathbf x$ gives the slope formula. Fitting is projection (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">linear algebra</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="Why squares?">

If each measurement equals the true value plus an independent random error with a normal ("bell curve") distribution, then the least-squares line is the line under which the observed data are *most likely*. Gauss justified least squares in roughly this way in 1809. With other kinds of error, for instance occasional wild outliers, other criteria such as minimising the sum of absolute residuals can be better. Statistics also says how uncertain the fitted parameters are, which is the next question a careful modeller asks (see <TopicLink id="math.prob.statistical-inference-intro">statistical reasoning</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Models from mechanisms, and units as a check">

Physicists prefer models derived from a mechanism. Newton's law of cooling assumes that heat flows out at a rate proportional to the temperature difference $T - T_{\text{room}}$, and that assumption *forces* the exponential form $T_{\text{room}} + (T_0 - T_{\text{room}})e^{-kt}$. A cheap test of any model is **units**: in $d = v t_r + \tfrac{v^2}{2a}$, both terms are lengths (m/s × s, and (m/s)² ÷ m/s²). A formula that adds metres to seconds is wrong before any data are consulted.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Interpolation in astronomy.** Calendar makers needed values between their observations. In China, Liu Zhuo (around 600 CE) used second-order interpolation, equivalent to fitting quadratics through equally spaced values. Guo Shoujing's *Shoushi* calendar (1281) used third-order differences. In India, Brahmagupta's *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* (665 CE) gave a second-order interpolation rule for sine tables.
- **Least squares.** Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805. Carl Friedrich Gauss, who said he had used it since 1795, published his probabilistic justification in 1809, after famously predicting where the dwarf planet Ceres would reappear.
- **Population and growth.** Thomas Malthus (1798) contrasted populations that grow geometrically with food supplies that grow arithmetically. Pierre-François Verhulst (1838) proposed the logistic model, in which growth slows as a population approaches a limit. In 1927, William Kermack and Anderson McKendrick founded mathematical epidemiology with their model of epidemics.
- **Newton's law of cooling** was published, anonymously, by Isaac Newton in 1701.

</Section>
