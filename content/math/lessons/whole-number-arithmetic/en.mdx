<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Eggs on the farm">

Neema helps on her family's farm near Arusha, in Tanzania. One morning the hens have laid **347** eggs. The eggs go to market in trays, and each tray holds **30** eggs.

- How many trays can Neema fill?
- How many eggs will be left over?
- Next week the farm sells **23** full trays. How many eggs is that?

The first two questions are about **dividing**, the last one about **multiplying**. You already know how to add and subtract using tens and ones (<TopicLink id="math.number.addition-subtraction">addition, subtraction and place value</TopicLink>). You may also know methods for multiplying and dividing. In this lesson we find out *why* those methods work, so that you never have to trust a rule you don't understand.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Equal groups and arrays">

**Multiplication counts equal groups.** Four trays with $6$ eggs in each hold $4 \times 6 = 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24$ eggs.

We can also set things out in rows and columns, as an **array**. An array with $4$ rows of $6$ has $4 \times 6$ objects. Now turn the array on its side: it becomes $6$ rows of $4$. Nothing was added and nothing was taken away, so

$$
4 \times 6 = 6 \times 4.
$$

That works for *any* array. Turning it never changes how many things are in it.

<AreaGrid mode="rectangle" width={6} height={4} title="An array of unit squares" subtitle="Change the width and height. Can you find two different rectangles with the same number of squares?" />

<Narrator who="chloe">

Turning the array is my favourite proof. It doesn't check $4 \times 6$ and $6 \times 4$ by working them out. It shows *why* they must be equal — for every pair of numbers at once.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The words and laws we need">

<Definition term="multiplication">

For whole numbers $a$ and $b$, the **product** $a \times b$ is the total number of objects in $a$ equal groups of $b$. It is also the number of squares in a rectangle (an array) with $a$ rows and $b$ columns.

</Definition>

Multiplication obeys three laws. They are true for all whole numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$:

| Law | In symbols | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commutative | $a \times b = b \times a$ | $4 \times 6 = 6 \times 4$ |
| Associative | $(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)$ | $(25 \times 4) \times 9 = 25 \times (4 \times 9)$ |
| Distributive | $a \times (b + c) = a \times b + a \times c$ | $7 \times 12 = 7 \times 10 + 7 \times 2$ |

Two more facts complete the toolkit: $a \times 1 = a$ and $a \times 0 = 0$.

Division often does not come out exactly. When it doesn't, something is **left over**.

<Definition term="division with remainder">

Let $a$ and $b$ be whole numbers with $b > 0$. **Dividing $a$ by $b$** means finding whole numbers $q$ and $r$ with

$$
a = q \times b + r \qquad\text{and}\qquad 0 \le r < b.
$$

The number $q$ is the **quotient** (how many full groups) and $r$ is the **remainder** (how many are left over). We write $a \div b = q$ remainder $r$. Many people shorten "remainder" to "r", as in $23 \div 5 = 4$ r $3$.

</Definition>

The condition $r < b$ matters. If $r$ were $b$ or more, we could make one more full group!

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Calculating cleverly">

- $7 \times 98$: think of $98$ as $100 - 2$. So $7 \times 98 = 7 \times 100 - 7 \times 2 = 700 - 14 = 686$.
- $25 \times 36$: think of $36$ as $4 \times 9$. So $25 \times 36 = (25 \times 4) \times 9 = 100 \times 9 = 900$.
- $4 \times 17 \times 25$: swap and regroup to $17 \times (4 \times 25) = 17 \times 100 = 1700$.
- Neema's $23$ trays of $30$ eggs: $23 \times 30 = (23 \times 3) \times 10 = 69 \times 10 = 690$ eggs.

Each step uses one of the laws. That is what makes these tricks *correct*, not just clever.

</Example>

<Example title="Neema's eggs">

How many trays of $30$ can be filled from $347$ eggs? Keep taking $30$ away: after $11$ trays, $11 \times 30 = 330$ eggs are used and $17$ are left. Since $17 < 30$, no more full trays can be made:

$$
347 = 11 \times 30 + 17.
$$

So $347 \div 30 = 11$ remainder $17$.

</Example>

<Example title="Small and exact cases">

- $23 \div 5 = 4$ remainder $3$, because $23 = 4 \times 5 + 3$ and $3 < 5$.
- $30 \div 6 = 5$ remainder $0$. A remainder of $0$ means the division is **exact**.
- $4 \div 7 = 0$ remainder $4$, because $4 = 0 \times 7 + 4$. No full group at all!

</Example>

<Example title="A non-example">

The equation $23 = 3 \times 5 + 8$ is true, but it does **not** say that $23 \div 5$ is $3$ remainder $8$. The leftover $8$ is bigger than $5$, so one more group of $5$ fits.

</Example>

<Example title="What does the remainder mean?">

The farm has $1000$ eggs. Then $1000 = 33 \times 30 + 10$. So $33$ trays can be filled. But to *hold every egg* the farm needs $34$ trays, because the last $10$ eggs need a tray too. The same thing happens on a school trip. A school in Leeds, in England, is taking $75$ children to a museum, and each minibus seats $16$. Since $75 = 4 \times 16 + 11$, four minibuses would be full and $11$ children would still be waiting, so the school needs $5$ minibuses. The same division can answer different questions, so always ask what the question wants.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="why" kind="proof" title="Why the methods work">

### Why the laws are true

- **Commutative:** turn the array (see above).
- **Associative:** build a box of cubes, $a$ long, $b$ wide and $c$ tall. Count it as $c$ layers of $a \times b$ cubes, or as $a$ slices of $b \times c$ cubes. Either way it is the same box, so $(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)$.
- **Distributive:** a rectangle $a$ tall and $b + c$ wide splits into one $a$ by $b$ rectangle and one $a$ by $c$ rectangle, as in the explorer.

### Why the written multiplication method works

The distributive law, used twice, says exactly this:

$$
\begin{aligned}
23 \times 47 &= (20 + 3) \times (40 + 7) \\
&= 20 \times 40 + 20 \times 7 + 3 \times 40 + 3 \times 7 \\
&= 800 + 140 + 120 + 21 = 1081.
\end{aligned}
$$

The **column method** adds the same four pieces, grouped into rows:

```text
      4 7
    × 2 3
    -----
    1 4 1     ← 47 × 3  = 3 × 40 + 3 × 7  = 120 + 21
    9 4 0     ← 47 × 20 = 20 × 40 + 20 × 7 = 800 + 140
    -----
  1 0 8 1
```

In the first row, $7 \times 3 = 21$ ones. We write $1$ in the ones column and **carry** the $2$. Carrying is not a trick. Twenty ones *are* two tens, so we move them to the tens column. Then $4 \times 3 = 12$ tens, plus the $2$ carried tens, makes $14$ tens: $4$ tens and $1$ hundred. The second row starts with a $0$ because we are multiplying by $2$ **tens**: $47 \times 20 = 47 \times 2 \times 10$.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So the column method is the area model in disguise. Every digit you write is one of the four pieces of the rectangle, or a regrouping of tens and hundreds.

</Narrator>

### Why long division works

Long division shares out one place value at a time. Here is $953 \div 4$, sharing $953$ among $4$ people:

1. **Hundreds.** $9$ hundreds shared by $4$ is $2$ hundreds each, using $8$. $1$ hundred is left.
2. Trade the leftover hundred for $10$ tens. With the $5$ tens we had, that makes $15$ **tens**. Each person gets $3$ tens, using $12$. $3$ tens are left.
3. Trade those $3$ tens for $30$ ones. With the $3$ ones, that makes $33$ **ones**. Each person gets $8$, using $32$. $1$ is left.

Each person got $2$ hundreds, $3$ tens and $8$ ones, so

$$
953 = 238 \times 4 + 1, \qquad 953 \div 4 = 238 \text{ remainder } 1.
$$

### Why there is only one quotient and one remainder

<Theorem name="division with remainder">

If $b > 0$, every whole number $a$ can be written as $a = q \times b + r$ with $0 \le r < b$, and there is **only one** such pair $q$, $r$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

**There is always a pair.** Take $b$ away from $a$ again and again, as long as at least $b$ is left. The amount left gets smaller each time, so you must stop. When you stop, fewer than $b$ are left: that is $r$. The number of times you took $b$ away is $q$.

**There is only one pair.** Picture the multiples of $b$ on a number line: $0, b, 2b, 3b, \dots$. They are like fence posts, exactly $b$ apart. The number $a$ lies in exactly one gap, from some post $q \times b$ up to (but not including) the next post $q \times b + b$. The remainder $r$ is how far $a$ is past that post, and it is less than $b$ because the next post is $b$ further on.

Could another pair work? A smaller quotient would put $a$ more than one whole gap past its post, so its "remainder" would be $b$ or more. A larger quotient would need a post *beyond* $a$, and then the "remainder" would have to be less than $0$. Neither is allowed.

</Proof>

### Why we cannot divide by zero

"$6 \div 2 = 3$" is a short way to say "$3$ is the number with $3 \times 2 = 6$". So what would $5 \div 0$ be? It would be a number $q$ with $q \times 0 = 5$. But $q \times 0 = 0$ for every number $q$. **No** number works, so $5 \div 0$ has no answer.

And $0 \div 0$? Now *every* number works, because $q \times 0 = 0$ is always true. A division that has every number as its answer tells us nothing, so $0 \div 0$ has no answer either. That is why we say **division by zero is undefined**. (Division with remainder also needs $b > 0$: no remainder $r$ could be smaller than $0$.)

</Section>

<Section id="formal" kind="formal" title="Gideon's precise statements">

<Narrator who="gideon">

I like statements that are *exactly* true or *exactly* false. Here are five. Decide which are true before you read my answers.

</Narrator>

1. "$7 \times 98 = 686$." — **True.** $7 \times 100 - 7 \times 2 = 700 - 14 = 686$.
2. "For every whole number $n$, $n \times 1 = n$." — **True.** $n$ groups of $1$ make $n$.
3. "Multiplying always makes a number bigger." — **False!** $9 \times 1 = 9$, which is not bigger than $9$. One counterexample is enough to show that an "always" statement is false.
4. "$23 \div 5$ is $3$ remainder $8$." — **False.** $23 = 3 \times 5 + 8$ is a true equation, but the rule says the remainder must be less than $5$. Being precise about the rule matters.
5. "Some number times $0$ is $5$." — **False.** Every number times $0$ is $0$.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"Multiplying always makes a number bigger."** Not always: $9 \times 1 = 9$ and $9 \times 0 = 0$. Later you will multiply by fractions such as $\tfrac12$, and then the answer is *smaller*: half of $9$ is $4\tfrac12$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The remainder can be any size."** In $23 \div 5$, writing "$3$ remainder $8$" is wrong even though $3 \times 5 + 8 = 23$. The remainder must be **less than** the number you divide by. If it isn't, another full group fits.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Carrying is just a trick."** Carrying is **regrouping**: ten ones are one ten, and ten tens are one hundred. When $7 \times 3 = 21$ ones, the "carried $2$" is $2$ tens moving to the tens column.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$5 \div 0 = 0$" or "$5 \div 0 = 5$".** Check by multiplying back: $0 \times 0 = 0$ and $5 \times 0 = 0$, never $5$. No number works, so $5 \div 0$ has no answer.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Division is commutative too."** It isn't: $12 \div 3 = 4$, but $3 \div 12 = 0$ remainder $3$. Only addition and multiplication can be done in either order.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Multiplication is area and volume">

A geometer sees $a \times b$ as the **area** of an $a$ by $b$ rectangle, and $a \times b \times c$ as the **volume** of a box. Then the laws become facts about shapes. The commutative law says that turning a rectangle through a right angle does not change its area. The distributive law says that cutting a rectangle into two pieces does not change the total area. The associative law says that a box has the same volume however you slice it. These pictures keep working long after whole numbers. Later you will use them for lengths that are fractions or decimals (<TopicLink id="math.geom.area-angles">area and perimeter</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="The laws are the real rules of the game">

An algebraist notices that every clever calculation in this lesson used only a short list of laws. So *anything* that obeys those laws can be calculated with in the same way. For example, the area model works with letters: $(x + 3) \times (x + 7) = x \times x + 7x + 3x + 21$. Many other number systems obey the same laws, and mathematicians give such a system a name: a **ring** (<TopicLink id="math.algebra.rings">rings</TopicLink>). The laws you learn in primary school are, word for word, part of its definition.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Remainders are the start of number theory">

Division with remainder is where number theory begins. Repeating it gives Euclid's algorithm for the greatest common factor. Keeping only the remainder gives "clock arithmetic", where $9$ o'clock plus $5$ hours is $2$ o'clock because $14 = 1 \times 12 + 2$. See <TopicLink id="math.number.factors-primes">factors and primes</TopicLink>, <TopicLink id="math.number.modular-arithmetic">clock arithmetic</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.number.divisibility-gcd">Euclid's algorithm</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Multiplying and dividing with sets">

In the <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">sets</TopicLink> lesson you met the **Cartesian product** $A \times B$, the set of all pairs $(a, b)$. If Juma has $3$ shirts and $4$ pairs of trousers, he can make $3 \times 4 = 12$ outfits, one for each pair (shirt, trousers). In general, $|A \times B| = |A| \times |B|$. That is where the multiplication sign in $A \times B$ comes from.

Division with remainder **sorts** a set. Dividing $347$ eggs by $30$ splits the set of eggs into $11$ trays of $30$ and one leftover group of $17$. These groups do not overlap and together they contain every egg. A splitting like this is called a **partition**.

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="A first glimpse of structure">

Think of "multiply by $3$" as a machine that changes any number into three times that number. Feed its output into a "multiply by $4$" machine, and the result is the same as one "multiply by $12$" machine. Chaining machines one after another is called **composition** (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.chaining-actions">one thing after another</TopicLink>). The associative law says it does not matter how we bracket a chain of three machines. The machine "multiply by $1$" changes nothing. These two rules — brackets don't matter, and there is a do-nothing machine — are the rules that define a **category**, an idea you can explore much later.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Egypt.** The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, copied by the scribe Ahmes around 1550 BCE from an older text, multiplies by **doubling**. To find $13 \times 12$: $12, 24, 48, 96$ are $1, 2, 4, 8$ times $12$, and $13 = 1 + 4 + 8$, so $13 \times 12 = 12 + 48 + 96 = 156$. This is the distributive law at work.
- **Mesopotamia.** Babylonian scribes, around 1800 BCE, worked in base $60$. They kept tables of products and tables of reciprocals. To divide by a number, they multiplied by its reciprocal.
- **China.** The Tsinghua bamboo slips, dated to about 305 BCE, contain a decimal multiplication table. It is the oldest one known anywhere in the world.
- **Greece.** Euclid's *Elements* (around 300 BCE) finds the greatest common measure of two numbers by subtracting the smaller from the larger again and again. Division with remainder is hidden inside this method.
- **India and the Islamic world.** Indian mathematicians developed decimal place value with a symbol for zero. Around 825 CE, Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī of Baghdad wrote a book on calculating with these "Hindu numerals". Its Latin translation spread the methods to Europe, and the word **algorithm** comes from his name.
- **Today.** In her book *Knowing and Teaching Elementary Mathematics* (1999), Liping Ma compared primary-school teachers in China and the United States. Many of the Chinese teachers, though they had spent fewer years in school, explained the methods through place value and the laws. She called this a "profound understanding of fundamental mathematics".

</Section>
