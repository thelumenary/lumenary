<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Questions that expect different answers">

Compare two questions:

1. How many minutes did it take Thandiwe to get to school this morning?
2. How many minutes does it take students in our class to get to school?

The first has one answer: Thandiwe can look at a clock. The second has many answers, because students live in different places and travel in different ways. It is a **statistical question**: it expects **variability** in the data, and the answer is not one number but a description of how the data are spread out.

Statistical questions are everywhere. How much rain falls in Nairobi in April, or in Vancouver in November? How tall are ten-year-olds in Peru? Which of two maize varieties gives a bigger harvest? To answer them we collect data, picture its **distribution**, and summarise it with a few well-chosen numbers. Choosing them well — and knowing what each one hides — is what this lesson is about.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="statistical question">

A **statistical question** is one that anticipates variability in the data used to answer it. “How tall am I?” is not statistical; “How tall are the students in my school?” is.

</Definition>

Let $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ be $n$ numerical data values.

<Definition term="mean, median, mode">

- The **mean** is $\bar x = \dfrac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n}$: the fair share if the total were shared out equally, as in <TopicLink id="math.prob.data-displays">data displays and the mean as a fair share</TopicLink>. The mean of whole numbers is often not a whole number but a <TopicLink id="math.number.rational-numbers">rational number</TopicLink>, such as $\tfrac{257}{15}$.
- The **median** is the middle value when the data are sorted; if $n$ is even, it is the mean of the two middle values.
- A **mode** is a value that occurs most often. (There may be several modes, or — if every value occurs once — none worth reporting.)

</Definition>

<Definition term="range, quartiles, interquartile range">

- The **range** is the largest value minus the smallest.
- The **lower quartile** $Q_1$ is the median of the lower half of the sorted data, and the **upper quartile** $Q_3$ the median of the upper half. In this lesson, when $n$ is odd the median itself is left out of both halves.
- The **interquartile range** is $\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$: the spread of the middle half of the data.

</Definition>

<Note>

Statisticians and software use several slightly different rules for quartiles. They agree on large data sets but can differ a little on small ones. Always say which rule you use; the computation section shows three rules disagreeing.

</Note>

<Definition term="five-number summary, box plot, outlier">

The **five-number summary** is: minimum, $Q_1$, median, $Q_3$, maximum. A **box plot** draws it: a box from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ with a line at the median, and whiskers out to the smallest and largest values. An **outlier** is a value far from the rest. A common rule of thumb (due to John Tukey) calls a value an outlier if it is more than $1.5 \times \mathrm{IQR}$ below $Q_1$ or above $Q_3$; outliers are then drawn as separate dots and the whiskers stop at the most extreme values that are not outliers.

</Definition>

A **histogram** groups numerical data into intervals of equal width (bins) and draws a bar for each bin whose height is the number of values in it. In this lesson a bin includes its left end but not its right end.

</Section>

<Example title="Same mean, different spread">

Two bus routes in Toronto are timed over 10 days. Waiting times in minutes:

- Route 1: $9, 10, 10, 11, 9, 10, 11, 10, 9, 11$ — mean $10$, range $2$.
- Route 2: $2, 18, 5, 15, 10, 1, 19, 8, 12, 10$ — mean $10$, range $18$.

The means (and even the medians) are equal, but route 1 is reliable and route 2 is not. Reporting only the mean would hide the most important difference.

</Example>

<Example title="Choosing a summary">

- Household incomes, house prices and journey times are usually **skewed**: a few very large values. The median describes a typical household better than the mean, which the largest values pull upwards.
- When the **total** matters — the fuel a fleet of buses uses, the rice a family eats in a month — the mean is the right tool, because mean $\times\, n =$ total.
- For categories, such as favourite sports, only the mode makes sense: there is no “mean sport”.

</Example>

<Section id="proof" kind="proof" title="Why the summaries behave as they do">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (the mean is the balance point)">

The deviations from the mean add up to zero: $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x) = 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i - n\bar x = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i - \sum_{i=1}^n x_i = 0$, because $n \bar x$ is the total.

</Proof>

So the values above the mean exactly balance the values below it. If the dot plot were a see-saw with equal weights at the dots, it would balance at the mean.

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (shifting and scaling)">

If a number $c$ is added to every value, the mean, the median and the mode increase by $c$, while the range and the IQR do not change. If every value is multiplied by a positive number $a$, all five of these summaries are multiplied by $a$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The new mean is $\tfrac{1}{n}\sum (x_i + c) = \tfrac1n \sum x_i + \tfrac1n \cdot nc = \bar x + c$, and similarly $\tfrac1n \sum a x_i = a \bar x$. Adding $c$ (or multiplying by $a > 0$) does not change the order of the values, or which values are equal, so the median, the quartiles and the mode are the old ones plus $c$ (or times $a$). A difference such as $Q_3 - Q_1$ is unchanged by adding $c$ to both, and multiplied by $a$ when both are.

</Proof>

Converting travel times from minutes to hours ($a = \tfrac{1}{60}$) therefore needs no recomputation.

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (the mean feels every value)">

If one value is increased by $d$, the mean increases by $\dfrac dn$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The total increases by $d$, and the mean is the total divided by $n$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (the median resists outliers)">

Suppose there are $n \ge 3$ values. Replacing the largest value by any larger value does not change the median.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The sorted order is unchanged: the largest value stays in the last position, number $n$. The median uses only the middle position — position $\tfrac{n+1}{2}$ if $n$ is odd, positions $\tfrac n2$ and $\tfrac n2 + 1$ if $n$ is even — and for $n \ge 3$ these all come before position $n$. So the median is computed from the same values as before.

</Proof>

In class A, changing $45$ to $900$ moves the mean from about $17.1$ to about $74.1$ minutes (Theorem 3: up by $\tfrac{855}{15} = 57$) but leaves the median at $15$. A summary that hardly reacts to a few extreme values is called **resistant**: the median is resistant and the mean is not.

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (same centre, different spread)">

Two data sets can have the same mean and the same median but very different ranges and IQRs.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The bus routes are an example: both have mean $10$ and median $10$, but ranges $2$ and $18$ and IQRs $2$ and $10$.

</Proof>

### Going further: two best centres

Suppose we must choose one number $c$ to stand for all the data. How should we measure the error? Two natural choices give the two centres.

<Theorem name="Theorem 6 (the mean minimises squared distance)">

For every number $c$,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - c)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2 + n(\bar x - c)^2.
$$

Hence $\sum (x_i - c)^2$ is smallest exactly when $c = \bar x$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $x_i - c = (x_i - \bar x) + (\bar x - c)$ and square:

$$
(x_i - c)^2 = (x_i - \bar x)^2 + 2(x_i - \bar x)(\bar x - c) + (\bar x - c)^2.
$$

Add these up for $i = 1, \ldots, n$. The middle terms give $2(\bar x - c)\sum (x_i - \bar x)$, which is $0$ by Theorem 1. The last terms give $n(\bar x - c)^2$. This proves the identity. The extra term $n(\bar x - c)^2$ is never negative, and it is zero only when $c = \bar x$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 7 (the median minimises total distance)">

The total distance $\sum_{i=1}^n |x_i - c|$ is smallest when $c$ is the median.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Sort the values, $x_1 \le x_2 \le \cdots \le x_n$, and pair the smallest with the largest, the second smallest with the second largest, and so on. For a pair $a \le b$, the distance from $c$ to $a$ plus the distance from $c$ to $b$ is at least $b - a$, with equality exactly when $c$ lies between $a$ and $b$. The intervals $[x_1, x_n] \supseteq [x_2, x_{n-1}] \supseteq \cdots$ are nested, and the median lies in all of them. (If $n$ is odd, the middle value is left unpaired; its distance $|x_{\text{mid}} - c|$ is zero exactly when $c$ is the median.) So at the median every pair achieves its smallest possible total, and no other choice of $c$ can do better.

</Proof>

When $n$ is even, every $c$ between the two middle values gives the same smallest total — another reason the median of an even number of values is a convention.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**“The mean is always the best summary of the centre.”** For skewed data or data with outliers, the median is often more typical: a few very rich households raise the mean income far above what most households earn. Choose the summary that fits the question.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Comparing groups by their centres alone.** The two bus routes have the same mean and median, yet one is reliable and the other is not. A good comparison reports centre **and** spread, and looks at the whole distribution.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Taking the middle of the unsorted list.** The median of $7, 2, 9$ is $7$ (sort first: $2, 7, 9$), not $2$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“The mean of two group means is the overall mean.”** If a class of $10$ has mean $60$ and a class of $30$ has mean $80$, the overall mean is $\tfrac{10 \times 60 + 30 \times 80}{40} = 75$, not $70$. The larger group counts for more.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“The mode is the typical value.”** In $1, 1, 7, 8, 9, 10$ the mode is $1$, which is not typical at all. The mode is most useful for categories.

</Misconception>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="The mean is a centre of mass">

Put equal weights at the positions $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ on a light rod. The rod balances at the point $c$ where the turning effects on the two sides cancel: $\sum (x_i - c) = 0$. By Theorem 1 that point is the mean. So the mean is the **centre of mass** of the data, and an outlier, like a weight far out on a see-saw, has a large effect. (The physics is an analogy that explains the formula; the proof is Theorem 1.)

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Two ways to measure distance">

Theorem 6 is a form of Pythagoras' theorem: the total squared distance from $c$ splits into the total squared distance from $\bar x$ plus a square, with no cross term, because the deviations $x_i - \bar x$ add up to zero. In higher dimensions this becomes the method of **least squares**, used to fit lines to data. Theorem 7 answers a different geometric question: where on a straight road should a village build one well to make the total walking distance of its houses as small as possible? At the median house. Squared distances and plain distances lead to different “centres”.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="Data as a sample">

Usually our data are a **sample** from a larger population — 15 students out of a whole school. Had we chosen 15 other students, the mean and median would have come out a little different. Statistics asks how much such summaries vary from sample to sample, and so how far they can be trusted; see <TopicLink id="math.prob.statistical-inference-intro">sampling and statistical reasoning</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.prob.probability-intro">probability</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Counting people and harvests.** States have collected data for thousands of years — censuses in ancient Egypt, China and Rome, and the land and tax records of many empires. The word *statistics* comes from the same root as *state*.
- **Averaging measurements.** Astronomers in the 17th and 18th centuries increasingly combined repeated measurements by taking their mean. Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805, and Carl Friedrich Gauss developed it further; Theorem 6 is its simplest case. Pierre-Simon Laplace studied the median as a way of combining observations.
- **Quartiles and the median.** Francis Galton popularised the median and quartiles in Britain in the late 19th century as simple, robust summaries.
- **Pictures of data.** Florence Nightingale's diagrams of deaths in the Crimean War (1858) helped change hospital practice. Mary Eleanor Spear drew a “range bar” in 1952, and John Tukey introduced the box plot in his *Exploratory Data Analysis* (1977).
- **Global statistics.** P. C. Mahalanobis founded the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata in 1931 and pioneered large national sample surveys. Today statistical offices across the world, and international bodies such as the United Nations Statistics Division, measure health, education and poverty with exactly the tools of this lesson.
