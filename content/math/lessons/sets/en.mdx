<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Who plays what?">

In Ms Okafor's class, some students play football, some play chess, some play both, and some play neither. She wants to answer questions like:

- Who plays **both** games?
- Who plays **at least one** of them?
- Who plays football but **not** chess?

Each question is about **collections of people** and how they overlap. In mathematics, a collection whose identity depends only on its members is called a **set**, and questions like these have precise answers.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Sets and their elements">

<Definition term="set, element">

A **set** is a collection of objects, called its **elements**. We write $x \in A$ to mean "$x$ is an element of $A$", and $x \notin A$ to mean it is not.

</Definition>

We can describe a small set by listing its elements inside curly brackets — this is called **roster notation**:

$$
A = \{2, 4, 6, 8\}, \qquad 4 \in A, \qquad 5 \notin A.
$$

Two facts about sets surprise people at first:

1. **Order doesn't matter.** $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and $\{3, 1, 2\}$ are the same set.
2. **Repetition doesn't matter.** $\{1, 1, 2\}$ is the same set as $\{1, 2\}$. An element is either in the set or not; it can't be in "twice".

Both facts come from one principle:

<Definition term="extensionality">

Two sets are **equal** exactly when they have the same elements: $A = B$ means that for every $x$, $\;x \in A$ if and only if $x \in B$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="empty set">

The **empty set**, written $\varnothing$ or $\{\}$, is the set with no elements at all. By extensionality there is only one empty set.

</Definition>

<Definition term="subset">

$A$ is a **subset** of $B$, written $A \subseteq B$, if every element of $A$ is also an element of $B$.

</Definition>

For example $\{2, 4\} \subseteq \{2, 4, 6, 8\}$, and every set is a subset of itself. The empty set is a subset of *every* set: it has no elements, so none of its elements can fail to be in $B$.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Notice that $\in$ and $\subseteq$ are different. $4 \in \{2, 4, 6\}$ says the *number* 4 is an element. $\{4\} \subseteq \{2, 4, 6\}$ says the *set* containing 4 sits inside. Mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes in all of mathematics.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="operations" kind="definitions" title="Making new sets from old">

<Definition term="union">

The **union** $A \cup B$ is the set of things in $A$ **or** in $B$ (or in both).

</Definition>

<Definition term="intersection">

The **intersection** $A \cap B$ is the set of things in **both** $A$ and $B$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="difference and complement">

The **difference** $A \setminus B$ is the set of things in $A$ but **not** in $B$. When everything we are talking about lives in a universe $U$, the **complement** $A^{c}$ is $U \setminus A$: everything in the universe that is not in $A$.

</Definition>

In mathematics, "or" always means **inclusive or**: "$x \in A$ or $x \in B$" is true when $x$ is in both. That is why the overlap belongs to $A \cup B$.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Union and intersection">

Let $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and $B = \{3, 4, 5, 6\}$.

- $A \cup B = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ — we list $3$ and $4$ only once.
- $A \cap B = \{3, 4\}$.
- $A \setminus B = \{1, 2\}$ and $B \setminus A = \{5, 6\}$.

</Example>

<Example title="Complement">

In the universe $U = \{1, 2, \dots, 8\}$, the complement of $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ is $A^{c} = \{5, 6, 7, 8\}$. Change the universe and the complement changes: a complement always depends on $U$.

</Example>

<Example title="Set-builder notation">

Sometimes listing is impossible, so we describe the elements by a property:

$$
E = \{\, n \in \Z \mid n \text{ is even} \,\} = \{\dots, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, \dots\}.
$$

Read it as "the set of integers $n$ such that $n$ is even."

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proof" kind="proof" title="Proving things about sets">

How can we be sure a statement about sets is true for *every* set — not just for the ones we have tried? We follow a single, arbitrary element and see where it must go.

<Theorem name="intersection sits inside">

For all sets $A$ and $B$: $\;A \cap B \subseteq A$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

We must show that every element of $A \cap B$ is an element of $A$. So let $x$ be any element of $A \cap B$. By the definition of intersection, $x \in A$ and $x \in B$. In particular, $x \in A$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, every element of $A \cap B$ is in $A$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="De Morgan's law">

For all subsets $A, B$ of a universe $U$: $\;(A \cup B)^{c} = A^{c} \cap B^{c}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By extensionality we show that each side contains exactly the same elements.

Take any $x \in U$. Then $x \in (A \cup B)^{c}$ means $x$ is **not** in $A \cup B$, which means it is **not** the case that ($x \in A$ or $x \in B$). That is the same as saying $x \notin A$ **and** $x \notin B$ — which means $x \in A^{c}$ and $x \in B^{c}$, i.e. $x \in A^{c} \cap B^{c}$.

Every step was an "if and only if", so the two sets have the same elements and are equal.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

The secret ingredient is the phrase "take *any* $x$". We never said which $x$. So the argument works for every element of every set — that is what makes it a proof and not just an example.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="size-pairs" kind="definitions" title="Size, pairs and products">

<Definition term="cardinality">

For a finite set $A$, its **cardinality** $|A|$ is the number of elements. So $|\{a, b, c\}| = 3$ and $|\varnothing| = 0$.

</Definition>

Counting a union needs care, because the overlap would be counted twice:

$$
|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|.
$$

Sometimes order *does* matter — for coordinates on a map, or (row, seat) in a theatre. For that we use **ordered pairs**: $(a, b) = (c, d)$ exactly when $a = c$ and $b = d$. So $(1, 2) \neq (2, 1)$, even though $\{1, 2\} = \{2, 1\}$.

<Definition term="Cartesian product">

The **Cartesian product** $A \times B$ is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \in A$ and $b \in B$.

</Definition>

If $A = \{\text{red}, \text{blue}\}$ and $B = \{1, 2, 3\}$ then $A \times B$ has $2 \times 3 = 6$ elements, from $(\text{red}, 1)$ to $(\text{blue}, 3)$. In general $|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B|$ — that is where the multiplication sign comes from.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Confusing $\in$ with $\subseteq$.** $3 \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ is true, but $3 \subseteq \{1, 2, 3\}$ is not even meaningful in school mathematics — $3$ is a number, not a set. Correct: $\{3\} \subseteq \{1, 2, 3\}$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Thinking $\{\varnothing\}$ is empty.** $\{\varnothing\}$ is a box containing an empty box. It has exactly one element, so $|\{\varnothing\}| = 1$, while $|\varnothing| = 0$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Adding sizes for a union.** $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B|$ only when $A$ and $B$ have no elements in common.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"I checked lots of examples, so it's proved."** Examples can disprove a general statement, and they can build confidence, but they cannot prove a statement about *all* sets.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="foundations" kind="sets" title="Naive and axiomatic set theory">

In this lesson we have used **naive set theory**: we assumed that any clear description gives a set. That works perfectly for school mathematics. But in 1901, Bertrand Russell discovered that the description "the set of all sets that are not members of themselves" leads to a contradiction. Mathematicians responded by writing down precise **axioms** for which sets exist — most commonly the system called **ZFC**. You can meet that story on the *Boxes inside boxes* trail, and properly at university.

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="A first glimpse of structure">

Subsets of a universe $U$, ordered by $\subseteq$, have a beautiful property. The intersection $A \cap B$ is the **largest** set that sits inside both $A$ and $B$:

$$
C \subseteq A \text{ and } C \subseteq B \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C \subseteq A \cap B.
$$

Describing an object by how other objects relate to it, rather than by listing its elements, is called a **universal property**. Much later you will see that this exact property makes $A \cap B$ a *product* in the category of subsets of $U$ — and that Cartesian products of sets are characterised in the same spirit.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- Thinkers in many traditions reasoned about collections, wholes and parts long before modern set theory. Jain mathematical texts from ancient India, for example, are often described as distinguishing several kinds of infinite quantity.
- In the 1760s Leonhard Euler used circles to illustrate logical relationships in his *Letters to a German Princess*. John Venn introduced the diagrams that bear his name in 1880.
- Modern set theory was created in the 1870s–1890s, above all by **Georg Cantor**, with essential contributions from **Richard Dedekind**. Earlier, **Bernard Bolzano**'s *Paradoxes of the Infinite* (published 1851) had already explored infinite collections.
- The axioms now most widely used, ZFC, are named after **Ernst Zermelo** and **Abraham Fraenkel**, with the axiom of **C**hoice.

</Section>
