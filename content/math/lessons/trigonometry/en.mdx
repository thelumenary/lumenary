<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Measuring what you cannot reach">

How do you measure the height of a mountain you cannot climb, or the distance to a ship you cannot reach? In the 19th century the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India set out to map the subcontinent using nothing but carefully measured baselines and **angles**, observed with heavy theodolites from hilltops and towers. Among the "computers" who turned those observations into distances and heights was the Bengali mathematician **Radhanath Sikdar**. He is widely credited with the calculations, around 1852, showing that a distant Himalayan summit known as Peak XV was the highest mountain on Earth. In 1856 the Survey announced its height as $29{,}002$ feet. We now call it Everest, or Sagarmāthā in Nepali and Chomolungma in Tibetan.

The key fact is simple: in a right triangle, **an angle determines the ratios of the sides**. Measure one length and one angle, and trigonometry gives you the others. The same functions — sine and cosine — also describe anything that repeats: tides, sound waves, alternating current, the seasons. In this lesson we define them, prove the identities that connect them, and derive the two laws that solve *any* triangle.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Why a ratio can belong to an angle">

Take any two right triangles that share an acute angle $\theta$. Their third angles are both $90^\circ - \theta$, so they are **similar** (AA; see <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">rigid motions, congruence and similarity</TopicLink>). One is a scaled copy of the other, so every ratio of sides is the same in both:

$$
\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \text{ is the same for every right triangle with angle } \theta.
$$

That ratio depends only on $\theta$, so we can give it a name and tabulate it once and for all. This is exactly what astronomers in Greece, India and the Islamic world did, centuries before calculators: they made tables, and anyone could then convert angles into lengths.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So "$\sin$" is not a number multiplied by $\theta$. It's a *function*: you feed it an angle, and it gives back a ratio. Write $\sin(\theta)$ or $\sin\theta$, but never think "$\sin \times \theta$".

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="sine, cosine and tangent of an acute angle">

In a right triangle with an acute angle $\theta$, let the **hypotenuse** be the side opposite the right angle, the **opposite** side be the side facing $\theta$, and the **adjacent** side be the other side touching $\theta$. Then

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad
\cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad
\tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}.
$$

</Definition>

Angles can be measured in degrees, but mathematics prefers a natural unit that comes from the circle itself.

<Definition term="radian">

An angle of **one radian** at the centre of a circle cuts off an arc whose length equals the radius. In general, an angle of $\theta$ radians cuts off an arc of length $s = r\theta$ on a circle of radius $r$. A full turn is an arc of length $2\pi r$, so

$$
2\pi \text{ radians} = 360^\circ, \qquad \pi \text{ radians} = 180^\circ, \qquad 1 \text{ radian} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.3^\circ.
$$

</Definition>

To convert degrees to radians, multiply by $\pi/180$; to convert back, multiply by $180/\pi$. So $90^\circ = \pi/2$, $60^\circ = \pi/3$, $45^\circ = \pi/4$ and $30^\circ = \pi/6$.

The triangle definition only works for angles between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$. To go further, we use a circle.

<Definition term="sine and cosine of any angle (the unit circle)">

The **unit circle** has centre $O = (0, 0)$ and radius $1$. Start at $(1, 0)$ and turn through an angle $\theta$ about $O$ — anticlockwise if $\theta > 0$, clockwise if $\theta \lt 0$. The point you reach is, by definition,

$$
(\cos\theta, \sin\theta).
$$

Wherever $\cos\theta \ne 0$, we define $\tan\theta = \sin\theta / \cos\theta$, the slope of the line from $O$ to that point.

</Definition>

For $0 \lt \theta \lt 90^\circ$ the two definitions agree: drop a perpendicular from the point $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ to the $x$-axis. You get a right triangle with hypotenuse $1$, adjacent side $\cos\theta$ and opposite side $\sin\theta$.

Because turning through $2\pi$ brings you back to where you started, $\sin$ and $\cos$ are **periodic** with period $2\pi$ (that is, $360^\circ$):

$$
\sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta, \qquad \cos(\theta + 2\pi) = \cos\theta.
$$

Reflecting in the $x$-axis turns $\theta$ into $-\theta$, so $\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$ (sine is **odd**) and $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$ (cosine is **even**). The tangent has period $\pi$ and is undefined where $\cos\theta = 0$, at $\theta = \pi/2 + k\pi$.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Exact values from two special triangles">

Cut a square of side $1$ along a diagonal: you get a right triangle with angles $45^\circ, 45^\circ, 90^\circ$ and sides $1, 1, \sqrt2$. Cut an equilateral triangle of side $2$ in half: you get angles $30^\circ, 60^\circ, 90^\circ$ and sides $1, \sqrt3, 2$ (by Pythagoras, $\sqrt{2^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt3$). Reading off the ratios:

| $\theta$ | $0$ | $30^\circ = \pi/6$ | $45^\circ = \pi/4$ | $60^\circ = \pi/3$ | $90^\circ = \pi/2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\sin\theta$ | $0$ | $\tfrac12$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ | $1$ |
| $\cos\theta$ | $1$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ | $\tfrac12$ | $0$ |
| $\tan\theta$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}$ | $1$ | $\sqrt3$ | undefined |

</Example>

<Example title="Angles in every quadrant">

The point at $150^\circ$ on the unit circle is the mirror image, in the $y$-axis, of the point at $30^\circ$. So $\sin 150^\circ = \sin 30^\circ = \tfrac12$ and $\cos 150^\circ = -\cos 30^\circ = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. In the same way, $240^\circ = 180^\circ + 60^\circ$ is the point at $60^\circ$ reflected through $O$, so $\cos 240^\circ = -\tfrac12$ and $\sin 240^\circ = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. And $\sin(-30^\circ) = -\tfrac12$ because sine is odd.

In general $\sin(180^\circ - \theta) = \sin\theta$: an angle and its supplement have the same sine. This will matter for the law of sines.

</Example>

<Example title="The height of a tower you cannot reach">

From a point $A$ on level ground, the top of a tower is seen at an angle of elevation of $30^\circ$. After walking $100$ m straight towards the tower, to $B$, the angle is $45^\circ$. How tall is the tower?

Let the height be $h$ and the distance from $B$ to the foot of the tower be $x$. From $B$: $\tan 45^\circ = h / x$, so $h = x$. From $A$: $\tan 30^\circ = h / (x + 100)$, so $x + 100 = \sqrt3\,h$. Hence $h + 100 = \sqrt3\,h$, and

$$
h = \frac{100}{\sqrt3 - 1} = 50\left(\sqrt3 + 1\right) \approx 136.6 \text{ m}.
$$

Surveyors measuring a mountain do the same thing on a grander scale, with corrections for the curvature of the Earth and the bending of light in the air.

</Example>

<Example title="Radians in action">

A bicycle wheel of radius $0.35$ m turns through $3$ radians. A point on the rim travels $s = r\theta = 0.35 \times 3 = 1.05$ m. In degrees the same angle is $3 \times 180^\circ/\pi \approx 171.9^\circ$, and the formula would need an awkward factor: $s = r\theta\pi/180$. Radians make arc length, and (in calculus) the derivatives of $\sin$ and $\cos$, as simple as possible.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="The identities and the laws">

<Theorem name="Pythagorean identity">

For every angle $\theta$: $\;\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The point $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ is on the unit circle, so its distance from $O$ is $1$. By the distance formula (which is the Pythagorean theorem; see <TopicLink id="math.geom.pythagorean-theorem">the Pythagorean theorem</TopicLink>), $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1^2$. The distance formula squares the coordinates, so their signs do not matter: the identity holds in every quadrant. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

Here $\sin^2\theta$ means $(\sin\theta)^2$. Dividing the identity by $\cos^2\theta$ gives $1 + \tan^2\theta = 1/\cos^2\theta$ wherever $\cos\theta \ne 0$.

<Theorem name="Addition formulas">

For all angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$:

$$
\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta, \qquad
\cos(\alpha + \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $R_\theta$ be the rotation of the plane about $O$ through the angle $\theta$. We use three geometric facts about rotations.

1. **Rotations add.** Rotating by $\beta$ and then by $\alpha$ is rotating by $\alpha + \beta$. In particular, rotations about $O$ commute.
2. **A quarter turn.** Rotating the point $(x, y)$ by $90^\circ$ gives $(-y, x)$. (Rotate the right triangle with vertices $O$, $(x, 0)$ and $(x, y)$ by a quarter turn.)
3. **Rotations respect scaling and parallelograms.** A rotation about $O$ is a rigid motion fixing $O$, so it sends the point $t\,P$ to $t\,R(P)$ and the parallelogram with vertices $O$, $P$, $Q$, $P + Q$ to a parallelogram. Hence $R(xP + yQ) = x\,R(P) + y\,R(Q)$.

By the definition of sine and cosine, $R_\alpha(1, 0) = (\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)$. By facts 1 and 2,

$$
R_\alpha(0, 1) = R_\alpha R_{90^\circ}(1, 0) = R_{90^\circ} R_\alpha(1, 0) = R_{90^\circ}(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha) = (-\sin\alpha, \cos\alpha).
$$

Now write $(\cos\beta, \sin\beta) = \cos\beta\,(1, 0) + \sin\beta\,(0, 1)$ and use fact 3:

$$
R_\alpha(\cos\beta, \sin\beta) = \cos\beta\,(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha) + \sin\beta\,(-\sin\alpha, \cos\alpha)
= (\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta,\; \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta).
$$

But $(\cos\beta, \sin\beta) = R_\beta(1, 0)$, so by fact 1 the left side is $R_{\alpha + \beta}(1, 0) = (\cos(\alpha + \beta), \sin(\alpha + \beta))$. Comparing coordinates gives both formulas. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Corollaries">

1. $\sin(\alpha - \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta - \cos\alpha\sin\beta$ and $\cos(\alpha - \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta$.
2. **Double angles:** $\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

1. Replace $\beta$ by $-\beta$ and use $\sin(-\beta) = -\sin\beta$, $\cos(-\beta) = \cos\beta$.
2. Put $\alpha = \beta = \theta$, then use $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

For example, $\sin 75^\circ = \sin(45^\circ + 30^\circ) = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac{\sqrt6 + \sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.966$.

From now on, in a triangle $ABC$ we write $a = BC$, $b = CA$, $c = AB$ for the sides, and $A$, $B$, $C$ also for the angles at those vertices.

<Theorem name="Law of cosines">

In every triangle, $\;c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Place $C$ at the origin and $B$ at $(a, 0)$ on the positive $x$-axis, with $A$ above the axis. The point $A$ is at distance $b$ from $C$, in the direction at angle $C$ from the positive $x$-axis, so by the unit-circle definition $A = (b\cos C, b\sin C)$. This holds whether $C$ is acute, right or obtuse. By the distance formula,

$$
c^2 = (b\cos C - a)^2 + (b\sin C)^2 = b^2\left(\cos^2 C + \sin^2 C\right) - 2ab\cos C + a^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

</Proof>

When $C = 90^\circ$, $\cos C = 0$ and the law of cosines *is* the Pythagorean theorem. When $C$ is obtuse, $\cos C \lt 0$ and $c^2 > a^2 + b^2$: the side opposite an obtuse angle is longer than Pythagoras would give.

<Theorem name="Law of sines">

In every triangle, $\;\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = \dfrac{c}{\sin C}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

In the coordinates of the last proof, the height of $A$ above the line $CB$ is $b\sin C$, so the area of the triangle is

$$
\text{Area} = \tfrac12 \cdot a \cdot b\sin C.
$$

The same argument at the other vertices gives $\text{Area} = \tfrac12 bc\sin A = \tfrac12 ca\sin B$. So $\tfrac12 ab\sin C = \tfrac12 bc\sin A = \tfrac12 ca\sin B$. Dividing by $\tfrac12 abc$ gives $\dfrac{\sin C}{c} = \dfrac{\sin A}{a} = \dfrac{\sin B}{b}$, and taking reciprocals (the sines of the angles of a triangle are positive) gives the law. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Note>

The common ratio has a meaning: $\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = 2R$, where $R$ is the radius of the circumcircle. For acute $A$, draw the diameter $BD$ of the circumcircle. By the inscribed angle theorem $\angle BDC = A$, and by Thales' theorem the triangle $BCD$ has a right angle at $C$; so $a = BC = BD \sin A = 2R\sin A$. (See <TopicLink id="math.geom.euclidean-geometry">Euclidean geometry and proof</TopicLink>.)

</Note>

<Note>

**The ambiguous case returns.** If you know $a$, $b$ and $A$ and use the law of sines to find $B$, you get $\sin B = b\sin A / a$. Since $\sin B = \sin(180^\circ - B)$, there may be *two* possible angles — exactly the SSA ambiguity from Euclidean geometry. Always check whether $180^\circ - B$ also fits in the triangle.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Reading $\sin\theta$ as "$\sin$ times $\theta$".** $\sin$ is the name of a function, like $f$ in $f(x)$. You cannot "cancel" it: $\dfrac{\sin 2x}{\sin x}$ is not $2$ (it is $2\cos x$), and $\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ is not $\sin$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Using degrees where radians are required (or the reverse).** $\sin 30 \approx -0.988$ in radians, but $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$. Calculators and programming languages have a mode or expect radians: Python's `math.sin(30)` means 30 radians. Formulas such as $s = r\theta$ (and the derivative of $\sin x$ being $\cos x$) are true only in radians.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha + \sin\beta$."** Sine is not additive. With $\alpha = \beta = 90^\circ$: $\sin 180^\circ = 0$, but $\sin 90^\circ + \sin 90^\circ = 2$. The correct rule is the addition formula, $\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$. A quick test: $\sin 45^\circ + \sin 30^\circ \approx 1.21 > 1$, which no sine can be.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$\sin^{-1} x$ means $1/\sin x$."** By convention $\sin^2 x = (\sin x)^2$, but $\sin^{-1} x$ means the **inverse function** $\arcsin x$, the angle in $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ whose sine is $x$. The reciprocal $1/\sin x$ is written $\csc x$ (cosecant). The notation is inconsistent; that is why many authors prefer $\arcsin$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$\sin B = k$ has one solution in a triangle."** Both $B$ and $180^\circ - B$ have the same sine. When the law of sines gives an angle, check whether the supplementary angle also works — the SSA ambiguity in disguise.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Why radians are the natural unit">

For small angles measured in radians, $\sin x \approx x$: the chord and the arc of a thin slice are almost the same length. More precisely, $\dfrac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$ as $x \to 0$, and this limit is what makes the derivative of $\sin x$ equal to $\cos x$. In degrees, every derivative would carry an extra factor of $\pi/180$. Analysis then gives sine and cosine as power series,

$$
\sin x = x - \frac{x^3}{3!} + \frac{x^5}{5!} - \cdots, \qquad \cos x = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} - \cdots,
$$

valid for every real $x$. These series were found by Mādhava of Saṅgamagrāma in Kerala (14th century) and rediscovered in Europe by Newton and others in the 17th century. Mathlib uses them as the *definition*. See <TopicLink id="math.analysis.sequences-series">sequences and series</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.analysis.intro-calculus">introductory calculus</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Rotations, complex numbers and a group">

The rotations of the plane about a point form a **group**: you can compose them (angles add), there is an identity (turning by $0$) and every rotation has an inverse (turning back). In <TopicLink id="math.number.complex-numbers">complex numbers</TopicLink>, rotation by $\theta$ is multiplication by $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, and Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ turns the addition formulas into the law of exponents $e^{i(\alpha+\beta)} = e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta}$. De Moivre's formula $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta$ then gives multiple-angle formulas for free.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Waves and oscillations">

A mass on a spring, a swinging pendulum (for small swings) and the voltage in an alternating current all follow $x(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$: amplitude $A$, angular frequency $\omega$, phase $\varphi$. Sound is pressure varying like this; a pure tone at $440$ Hz is $\sin(2\pi \cdot 440\, t)$. Joseph Fourier showed that very general periodic signals are sums of sines and cosines, an idea at the heart of signal processing, music technology and the analysis of heat flow. See <TopicLink id="math.analysis.pde-intro">Fourier series</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Triangles on a sphere">

Astronomers and navigators needed trigonometry on the **sphere**, where the "sides" of a triangle are arcs of great circles. For a spherical triangle on the unit sphere with sides $a, b, c$ (measured as angles at the centre), the spherical law of cosines reads $\cos c = \cos a\cos b + \sin a \sin b \cos C$. For very small triangles, $\cos x \approx 1 - x^2/2$ and $\sin x \approx x$, and it turns back into the plane law of cosines — another sign that a small patch of a sphere looks flat. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.non-euclidean-geometry">non-Euclidean geometry</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Greece.** Hipparchus of Nicaea (2nd century BCE) is credited with the first table of **chords** (the chord of an angle $\theta$ in a circle of radius $R$ is $2R\sin(\theta/2)$). Claudius Ptolemy's *Almagest* (Alexandria, 2nd century CE) gave a chord table at half-degree steps and proved what amounts to the addition formulas, using a theorem about cyclic quadrilaterals now named after him.
- **India.** Indian astronomers replaced the chord by the **half-chord**, which is our sine. Āryabhaṭa's *Āryabhaṭīya* (499 CE) gives a table of sine differences for steps of $3\tfrac34^\circ$ in a circle of radius $3438$. Brahmagupta (7th century) gave a second-order interpolation rule for such tables, and Mādhava of Saṅgamagrāma (14th century) and the Kerala school found infinite series for sine and cosine.
- **The word "sine".** The Sanskrit *jyā* or *jīvā* (bow-string) became *jība* in Arabic. Written without vowels, it was later read as *jayb*, "fold" or "pocket", and translated into Latin as *sinus*, from which "sine" comes.
- **The Islamic world.** Al-Battānī (around 900) used sines and tangents in astronomy. Abū al-Wafāʾ al-Būzjānī (10th century) compiled tables of sines and tangents and is one of the mathematicians credited with the law of sines for spherical triangles. Al-Bīrūnī (11th century) estimated the radius of the Earth from the angle at which the horizon dips below the horizontal, seen from a mountain of known height. Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī (13th century) treated trigonometry as a subject in its own right, and Jamshīd al-Kāshī (15th century) computed $\sin 1^\circ$ to great accuracy; in France the law of cosines is called *théorème d'Al-Kashi*.
- **Europe.** Regiomontanus wrote *On Triangles of Every Kind* (1464, printed 1533), and Leonhard Euler (1748) treated sine and cosine as functions of a real number and connected them with the exponential function.
- **Surveying.** Triangulation, using a measured baseline and the law of sines, mapped countries from the 17th century onwards, including the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India (from 1802).

</Section>
