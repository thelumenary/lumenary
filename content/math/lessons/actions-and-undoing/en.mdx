<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="The do-it, undo-it game">

Rosa and her little brother Mateo, in Oaxaca in Mexico, are playing a game. Rosa **does** something. Then Mateo has to **undo** it — to put everything back exactly as it was.

- Rosa opens the window. Mateo closes it. Back as it was!
- Rosa puts on Grandpa's hat. Mateo takes it off her head.
- Rosa takes 3 big steps forward. Mateo says, "Now take 3 big steps back!"
- Rosa turns over a card, so the picture is hidden. Mateo turns it over again.

Then Grandma cracks an egg into the pan for breakfast. "Undo that!" laughs Rosa. Mateo looks at the egg. He thinks hard. Can anybody put a cracked egg back together?

<Narrator who="chloe">

Hello! I'm Chloe. I always ask *why*. Why can Mateo undo the window, but not the egg? What is different about them? Let's play and find out.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Actions change things">

An **action** is something you *do* that changes something: open a door, add a block to a tower, turn an arrow, walk forward. Every action has an effect: the door was closed, and now it is open.

Some actions can be **undone**. To undo an action, you do a second action that puts everything back the way it was before.

| Action | How to undo it |
| --- | --- |
| open the door | close the door |
| put on a hat | take off the hat |
| put one more block on the tower | take one block off |
| take 3 steps forward | take 3 steps back |
| turn a card over | turn it over again |
| crack an egg | ??? nobody can! |

<Narrator who="gideon">

I'm Gideon. I like to be precise. "Undo" means **everything** goes back exactly as it was — not just a bit. If Rosa walks 3 steps forward and Mateo walks her 2 steps back, that is *not* undoing. She is one step away from where she started.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="ideas" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="action">

An **action** changes an object or a situation. (Sometimes it leaves it the same — keep reading!)

</Definition>

<Definition term="undo">

An action **undoes** another action if doing the other action first, and then this one, puts **everything** back exactly as it was. For example, "close the door" undoes "open the door": open it, then close it, and the door is back as it was.

</Definition>

<Definition term="doing nothing">

**Doing nothing** is an action too: the action that leaves **every** thing exactly as it was.

</Definition>

Doing nothing is a funny action, but a very useful one. Doing nothing and then flipping a card is just the same as flipping the card. And doing an action and then undoing it has the same effect as doing nothing at all!

<Narrator who="chloe">

Here's a puzzle: how do you undo *doing nothing*? By doing nothing! And here is another way to do nothing: turn all the way round, a full turn, so you face the same way as before.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Let's try some together">

<Example title="Flip it again">

A card is face down. **Flip** it: now it is face up. To undo the flip, flip it again: face down, just as before. For a card, **the same action undoes itself**.

</Example>

<Example title="Walking is different">

Take **3 steps forward**. Does doing it again undo it? No! After 3 more steps forward you are 6 steps from where you started. To undo 3 steps forward you need a **different** action: **3 steps back**.

</Example>

How can we undo one quarter turn?

- Turn it a quarter turn **back** (the other way): right becomes up again.
- Or keep going: **three more** quarter turns the same way: right → down → left → up. Four quarter turns make a full turn, and a full turn is like doing nothing!

But one more quarter turn the same way does **not** undo it: the arrow would point **down**.

</Example>

<Section id="cannot" kind="human" title="Why some actions cannot be undone">

Some actions cannot be undone, however hard we try: cracking an egg, mixing two paints, cutting paper into pieces, squashing a sandcastle. Why not?

Here is a big clue. Kenji's class has a little whiteboard. One day it has a picture of a **cat** on it. Another day it has a picture of a **dog**. Each day, after school, the teacher **wipes** it clean.

Could some action undo the wiping? It would have to look at the blank board and bring back the picture. But **which** picture — the cat or the dog? Both boards turned into the very same blank board. Nothing about the blank board tells you which one it was.

So here is the rule: **if an action turns two different things into the same thing, it can never be undone.** The egg is like that, too: a whole egg and an egg that is already cracked both end up cracked. Orange paint could have come from red and yellow, or straight from a tin of orange.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So the trouble is *forgetting*. Wiping makes the board forget what was on it. Mixing makes the paint forget what colours went in. An action that forgets can't be undone.

</Narrator>

<Note>

Taking a block off a tower is a sneaky one. Taking a block off a tower of 1 leaves an empty spot. Trying to take a block from an empty spot also leaves an empty spot! Two different starts, one end — so "take a block away" cannot always be undone, even though "add a block" can.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's yes-or-no game">

Gideon reads a statement. Is it **true** or **false**?

1. "Flipping a card is undone by flipping it again." — **True.**
2. "Every action is undone by doing it again." — **False!** Walking 3 steps forward twice takes you 6 steps away. One counterexample is enough.
3. "A quarter turn can be undone by three more quarter turns." — **True.** Four quarter turns make a full turn.
4. "Every action can be undone." — **False.** Wiping the board cannot be undone.
5. "Doing nothing, and then flipping the card, is the same as just flipping the card." — **True.**

<Narrator who="gideon">

For number 4, a single action that cannot be undone is enough to show "every action can be undone" is false. Wiping the board does the job.

</Narrator>

<Section id="margaret" kind="computation" title="Margaret tries every action">

<Narrator who="margaret">

I'm Margaret, and I like to calculate. Imagine a tiny world with just 3 counters: red, blue and green. An action says what each counter becomes. There are exactly 27 different actions in this world! My computer tried every one of them, and for each one it tried all 27 possible ways of undoing it.

Margaret found that only 6 of the 27 actions can be undone: the ones that just **shuffle** the counters around, never turning two counters into the same one. And only 4 of those 6 are undone by doing them again. The other 2 move every counter one place round in a circle, and they are undone by going round **the other way**.

<ForGrownUps title="Margaret's program">

The program lists every action on worlds of 1, 2, 3 and 4 things, searches every possible undo, and checks the rule from the lesson: an action can be undone exactly when it never sends two things to the same result. Because every case is tried, the counts are certain for these tiny worlds — but a computer search cannot cover every world. <Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"Every action can be undone."** Many can, but not all. Cracking an egg, wiping a board and mixing paints cannot be undone, because two different starting points end up looking exactly the same.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Doing nothing isn't an action."** It is! It is the action that leaves everything as it was. Doing an action and then undoing it has the same effect as doing nothing.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"To undo something, just do it again."** That works for flipping a card or switching a light on and off. But walking 3 steps forward again takes you further away, and a quarter turn again makes the arrow point the opposite way. Often the undo is a *different* action.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Undoing part of it is good enough."** Undoing means *everything* goes back exactly as it was. Two steps back do not undo three steps forward.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Two ways of looking at doing and undoing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="The geometer: moves that can be undone">

Slides, turns and flips move a shape without changing it, and every one of them can be undone: slide back, turn back, flip again. The moves that leave a shape looking exactly the same are its **symmetries**. A square has 8 of them: doing nothing, three turns, and four flips. You will meet them in <TopicLink id="math.geom.symmetry-transformations">Symmetry: slides, turns and flips</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="The algebraist: collections of actions">

An algebraist looks at a whole collection of actions together: for example, the four turns of the arrow card (do nothing, a quarter turn, a half turn, three quarter turns). Doing one and then another always gives another one in the collection, doing nothing is in it, and every one can be undone. A collection like that is called a **group**, and it is one of the most important ideas in mathematics: see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.groups">Groups</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="where" kind="categories" title="Where doing and undoing lead">

Doing one action after another is the next step: <TopicLink id="math.cat.chaining-actions">One thing after another</TopicLink> asks how to undo a whole chain of actions (think about socks and shoes!).

<ForGrownUps title="the grown-up words">

Later on, actions become **functions**, "doing nothing" becomes the **identity function**, "doing one action after another" becomes **composition**, and an action that undoes another becomes an **inverse**. These ideas are the heart of <TopicLink id="math.cat.composition">Composition</TopicLink>, and in category theory an arrow that can be undone in both orders is called an **isomorphism**. The theorem `cannot_undo_of_same_result` is the statement that a function with a left inverse must be **injective**. Young children need none of this vocabulary: playing "do it, undo it" is the real idea.

</ForGrownUps>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Undoing in history">

- **"Algebra" means "restoring".** Around 820 CE, in Baghdad, Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī wrote a book about solving problems by two kinds of steps. One was called *al-jabr*, "restoring": when something has been taken away on one side of a problem, add it back, on both sides, so that nothing is missing. Our word **algebra** comes from it.
- **Groups.** In the 1830s the young French mathematician Évariste Galois studied collections of shuffles that can all be undone, to understand when equations can be solved. Today such collections are called groups.
- **A famous puzzle.** In 1974, Ernő Rubik, a teacher of design in Budapest, invented his cube puzzle. Every twist can be undone by twisting back — but undoing a long muddle of twists takes careful thinking about the order.

</Section>
