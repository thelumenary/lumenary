<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="When the integers run out">

With the <TopicLink id="math.number.integers">integers</TopicLink> you can always add, subtract and multiply. But you cannot always **divide**.

- Three friends in Lagos share 2 litres of zobo, a hibiscus drink. Each gets $2 \div 3$ litres — and no integer is $2 \div 3$.
- An underwater robot surveying a reef off Mombasa sinks from the surface to $-45$ metres in 6 equal stages. Each stage changes its depth by $-45 \div 6$ metres — a negative number that is not a whole number.

To divide freely we need fractions, positive **and** negative. They raise some surprising questions:

1. Is there a smallest positive fraction — a number “just after” $0$?
2. Why does $1 \div 7 = 0.142857\,142857\,142857\ldots$ repeat the same six digits for ever?
3. Is $0.999\ldots$ (nines for ever) a little less than $1$, or equal to it?
4. If we mark every fraction on the number line, is the line full?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to answer all four — with proofs.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="rational number">

A **rational number** is a number that can be written as $\dfrac{a}{b}$, where $a$ and $b$ are integers and $b \neq 0$. The set of all rational numbers is written $\Q$ (for *quotient*).

</Definition>

One rational number has many names: $\tfrac12 = \tfrac24 = \tfrac{-3}{-6}$. The rule for when two names mean the same number is

$$
\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad ad = bc \qquad (b, d \neq 0).
$$

(Multiply both sides of $\tfrac ab = \tfrac cd$ by $bd$ to get $ad = bc$; divide $ad = bc$ by $bd$ to go back.) For example, $\tfrac{6}{15} = \tfrac25$ because $6 \cdot 5 = 30 = 15 \cdot 2$.

- **Signs.** $\dfrac{-a}{b} = \dfrac{a}{-b} = -\dfrac{a}{b}$. We usually make the denominator positive.
- **Simplest form.** A fraction is in *simplest form* (lowest terms) when $b > 0$ and $a$ and $b$ have no common factor bigger than $1$. Every rational number has exactly one simplest form; the proof uses facts about primes from <TopicLink id="math.number.divisibility-gcd">divisibility</TopicLink>.
- **Integers are rational.** Every integer $n$ equals $\tfrac n1$, so $\Z \subseteq \Q$.

The four operations are defined by the familiar fraction rules, now allowing negative numerators:

$$
\frac ab + \frac cd = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}, \qquad
\frac ab - \frac cd = \frac{ad - bc}{bd}, \qquad
\frac ab \cdot \frac cd = \frac{ac}{bd}.
$$

<Definition term="reciprocal">

The **reciprocal** of a number $x$ is a number $y$ with $x \cdot y = 1$. The reciprocal of $\tfrac ab$ (with $a, b \neq 0$) is $\tfrac ba$. **Dividing** by a non-zero number means multiplying by its reciprocal:

$$
\frac ab \div \frac cd = \frac ab \cdot \frac dc \qquad (c \neq 0).
$$

</Definition>

<Definition term="terminating and repeating decimals">

A decimal **terminates** if it stops: $0.375 = \tfrac{375}{1000}$. A decimal **eventually repeats** if, from some place on, one block of digits repeats for ever. We mark the block with a bar: $0.41\overline{6} = 0.41666\ldots$ and $0.\overline{142857} = 0.142857142857\ldots$

</Definition>

A terminating decimal can be seen as repeating the block $0$ for ever: $0.375 = 0.375\overline{0}$.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Adding and subtracting with negatives">

- $-\tfrac34 + \tfrac56 = -\tfrac{9}{12} + \tfrac{10}{12} = \tfrac{1}{12}$.
- $\tfrac25 - \tfrac{7}{10} = \tfrac{4}{10} - \tfrac{7}{10} = -\tfrac{3}{10}$.

As with integers, subtracting means adding the opposite: $\tfrac25 - \tfrac7{10} = \tfrac25 + \left(-\tfrac7{10}\right)$.

</Example>

<Example title="Multiplying and dividing with negatives">

- $\left(-\tfrac23\right) \cdot \tfrac94 = -\tfrac{18}{12} = -\tfrac32$. The sign rules are the ones proved in the integers lesson.
- $\left(-\tfrac56\right) \div \left(-\tfrac{10}{9}\right) = \left(-\tfrac56\right) \cdot \left(-\tfrac{9}{10}\right) = \tfrac{45}{60} = \tfrac34$.
- The robot: $-45 \div 6 = -\tfrac{45}{6} = -\tfrac{15}{2} = -7.5$ metres per stage.

</Example>

<Example title="Dividing by a half doubles">

$3 \div \tfrac12 = 3 \cdot 2 = 6$. It makes sense: “how many halves fit into $3$?” Six. Dividing by a number between $0$ and $1$ makes a positive number **bigger**.

</Example>

<Example title="Fractions to decimals">

- $\tfrac38$: since $8 \cdot 125 = 1000$, $\tfrac38 = \tfrac{375}{1000} = 0.375$. It terminates.
- $\tfrac{5}{12}$ by long division. Keep track of the **remainders**:
  - $50 = 4 \cdot 12 + 2$: digit $4$, remainder $2$;
  - $20 = 1 \cdot 12 + 8$: digit $1$, remainder $8$;
  - $80 = 6 \cdot 12 + 8$: digit $6$, remainder $8$ — the same remainder again!

  From now on every step is the same, so $\tfrac{5}{12} = 0.41\overline{6}$.

</Example>

<Example title="Non-examples">

- The decimal $0.101001000100001\ldots$ (one more $0$ each time) never settles into a repeating block, so — by a theorem below — it is **not** a rational number.
- $\sqrt2$ and $\pi$ are not rational numbers. For $\sqrt2$ we prove it in this lesson; for $\pi$ the proof (Lambert, 1761) is harder.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proof" kind="proof" title="The main results, with proofs">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (closure)">

If $x$ and $y$ are rational, then so are $x + y$, $x - y$ and $xy$, and so is $x \div y$ if $y \neq 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $x = \tfrac ab$ and $y = \tfrac cd$ with integers $a, b, c, d$ and $b, d \neq 0$. Then

$$
x + y = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}, \qquad x - y = \frac{ad - bc}{bd}, \qquad xy = \frac{ac}{bd}.
$$

The numerators and denominators are integers, and $bd \neq 0$ because a product of non-zero integers is non-zero. If $y \neq 0$, then $c \neq 0$ (otherwise $y = \tfrac 0d = 0$), so $x \div y = \tfrac{ad}{bc}$ with $bc \neq 0$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (reciprocals)">

Every non-zero rational number has a reciprocal that is also rational. The number $0$ has no reciprocal.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $x = \tfrac ab \neq 0$ then $a \neq 0$, so $\tfrac ba$ is a rational number, and $\tfrac ab \cdot \tfrac ba = \tfrac{ab}{ba} = 1$. For $0$: every $y$ has $0 \cdot y = 0 \neq 1$.

</Proof>

This fills the empty box from the <TopicLink id="math.number.integers">integers</TopicLink> lesson, where only $1$ and $-1$ had reciprocals. A number system in which you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide by anything non-zero, with the usual rules, is called a **field** — $\Q$ is the first field most people meet.

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (density)">

If $p$ and $q$ are rational numbers with $p < q$, then their average $m = \tfrac{p + q}{2}$ is rational and $p < m < q$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$m$ is rational by closure. From $p < q$, adding $p$ to both sides gives $2p < p + q$, so $p < \tfrac{p+q}2$. Adding $q$ instead gives $p + q < 2q$, so $\tfrac{p+q}2 < q$.

</Proof>

So there is **no smallest positive rational number** (question 1): if $r > 0$ were the smallest, then $\tfrac r2$ would be a smaller positive rational. Repeating the averaging shows there are infinitely many rationals between any two.

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (decimals of fractions repeat)">

Let $a \ge 0$ and $b > 0$ be integers. The decimal expansion of $\tfrac ab$ either terminates or eventually repeats. If it does not terminate, the repeating block has at most $b - 1$ digits.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

In long division by $b$, each step takes the current remainder $r$, with $0 \le r < b$, and divides $10r$ by $b$: $10r = (\text{digit}) \cdot b + (\text{new remainder})$. The next digit and the next remainder depend **only on $r$**.

There are only $b$ possible remainders: $0, 1, \ldots, b - 1$. So among the first $b + 1$ remainders, two must be equal — this is the **pigeonhole principle**: $b + 1$ pigeons in $b$ holes means some hole holds two. Once a remainder comes back, the computation from that point on is an exact copy of what happened after its first appearance, so the digits repeat (question 2).

If the remainder $0$ ever appears, all later digits are $0$ and the decimal terminates. Otherwise only the $b - 1$ remainders $1, \ldots, b - 1$ occur, so a repeat happens within $b - 1$ steps and the block has at most $b - 1$ digits.

</Proof>

For $\tfrac17$ the block has the largest length allowed, $7 - 1 = 6$: all six non-zero remainders appear before one repeats.

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (which fractions terminate)">

A fraction $\tfrac ab$ in simplest form has a terminating decimal exactly when $b$ has no prime factors other than $2$ and $5$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $b = 2^m 5^n$, let $k$ be the larger of $m$ and $n$. Then $b \cdot 2^{k-m} 5^{k-n} = 2^k 5^k = 10^k$, so $\tfrac ab = \tfrac{a \cdot 2^{k-m}5^{k-n}}{10^k}$, which terminates after $k$ places.

Conversely, if $\tfrac ab = \tfrac{N}{10^k}$ for an integer $N$, then $a \cdot 10^k = N b$, so $b$ divides $a \cdot 10^k$. Since $a$ and $b$ have no common factor, $b$ must divide $10^k$ (this step is Euclid's lemma about primes). The only prime factors of $10^k = 2^k 5^k$ are $2$ and $5$, so the same is true of $b$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 6 (repeating decimals are fractions)">

Every terminating or eventually repeating decimal is a rational number.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

A terminating decimal with $k$ places is an integer divided by $10^k$. Now take a purely repeating decimal, say $x = 0.\overline{27}$. Shifting the decimal point two places gives $100x = 27.\overline{27} = 27 + x$, so $99x = 27$ and $x = \tfrac{27}{99} = \tfrac3{11}$. In general, if $x = 0.\overline{d_1 d_2 \ldots d_k}$ and $D$ is the whole number $d_1 d_2 \ldots d_k$, then $10^k x = D + x$, so

$$
x = \frac{D}{10^k - 1}.
$$

If some digits come before the block, as in $y = 0.41\overline{6}$, first shift them past the point: $100y = 41.\overline6 = 41 + \tfrac69$, so $y = \tfrac{41 + 2/3}{100} = \tfrac{125}{300} = \tfrac5{12}$. By closure, the result is always rational.

</Proof>

<Note>

“Shift the point and subtract the infinite tail” treats an infinite decimal as a number. That is justified once we define an infinite decimal to mean the number its finite truncations $0.2,\ 0.27,\ 0.272,\ \ldots$ approach — the subject of <TopicLink id="math.analysis.sequences-series">sequences and series</TopicLink>. Here we use it as known.

</Note>

<Theorem name="Theorem 7">

$0.999\ldots = 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Theorem 6 with $D = 9$ and $k = 1$ gives $x = \tfrac{9}{10 - 1} = 1$. Here is a second argument that avoids shifting an infinite decimal. The number with $n$ nines, $0.99\ldots9$, equals $1 - \tfrac{1}{10^n}$. If $0.999\ldots$ were less than $1$, the gap $d = 1 - 0.999\ldots$ would be a positive number. But $0.999\ldots$ is at least $0.99\ldots9$ for every $n$, so $d \le \tfrac{1}{10^n}$ for **every** $n$. That is impossible for a positive $d$: choose $n$ so large that $10^n > \tfrac1d$. So the gap is $0$ (question 3).

</Proof>

$0.999\ldots$ and $1$ are two names for the same number, just as $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac24$ are. In fact, every terminating decimal other than $0$ has a second name ending in nines: $0.5 = 0.4999\ldots$

</Section>

<Section id="gap" kind="human" title="A gap in the rational line">

Now question 4. Draw a square with sides of length $1$. By the <TopicLink id="math.geom.pythagorean-theorem">Pythagorean theorem</TopicLink> its diagonal $d$ satisfies $d^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2$. Swing the diagonal down onto the number line with a compass: it marks a real point, $\sqrt 2 \approx 1.414$. Is that point one of the fractions?

<Theorem name="Theorem 8 (√2 is irrational)">

There is no rational number whose square is $2$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose, for contradiction, that $\left(\tfrac ab\right)^2 = 2$ with $\tfrac ab$ in simplest form. Then $a^2 = 2b^2$, so $a^2$ is even. If $a$ were odd, $a^2 = a \cdot a$ would be odd (odd times odd is odd); so $a$ is even, say $a = 2c$. Then $4c^2 = 2b^2$, so $b^2 = 2c^2$, which is even, so $b$ is even too. But then $2$ is a common factor of $a$ and $b$, contradicting simplest form. So no such fraction exists.

</Proof>

So even after every fraction is marked, the number line has a hole at $\sqrt2$ — and, it turns out, infinitely many other holes ($\sqrt3$, $\pi$, …). The rationals are **dense** (no interval is free of them) but **not complete** (they have gaps). Filling the gaps gives the <TopicLink id="math.number.real-numbers">real numbers</TopicLink>.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So “there's always another fraction in between” and “the fractions leave gaps” are both true. Being crowded is not the same as being full!

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**“Dividing always makes a number smaller.”** Dividing by a number between $0$ and $1$ makes a positive number bigger: $3 \div \tfrac12 = 6$. And dividing a negative number by $2$ makes it bigger too: $-6 \div 2 = -3$, which is greater than $-6$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“$0.999\ldots$ is just less than $1$.”** The gap would have to be smaller than $\tfrac1{10^n}$ for every $n$, and the only such number that is not negative is $0$. So $0.999\ldots = 1$ (Theorem 7) — two names for one number.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“Once every fraction is marked, the number line is full.”** The fractions are dense, but $\sqrt2$ (Theorem 8), $\sqrt3$, $\pi$ and infinitely many other numbers are missing.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“To add fractions, add the tops and add the bottoms.”** $\tfrac12 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac56$, not $\tfrac25$. (Curiously, $\tfrac25$ lies *between* $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac12$ — it is their *mediant* — but it is not their sum.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“A fraction with a big denominator has a long decimal.”** $\tfrac{1}{1024} = 0.0009765625$ terminates because $1024 = 2^{10}$, while $\tfrac13$ repeats for ever. What matters is the prime factors of the denominator (Theorem 5), not its size.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Rational numbers are slopes through grid points">

Draw the square grid of points with integer coordinates. The line from $(0, 0)$ through the grid point $(b, a)$, with $b \neq 0$, has slope $\tfrac ab$ — a rational number. Conversely, a line through the origin with rational slope $\tfrac ab$ hits the grid point $(b, a)$. So the rational numbers are exactly the slopes of lines through the origin that meet another grid point. The line $y = \sqrt2\, x$ passes through no grid point except $(0, 0)$, however far you follow it: that is Theorem 8 in a picture. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.coordinate-plane">the coordinate plane</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Crowded but not complete">

The truncations $1,\ 1.4,\ 1.41,\ 1.414,\ \ldots$ of $\sqrt2$ are all rational, and they get closer and closer to one another — yet no rational number is the point they approach. An analyst says $\Q$ is **dense** but **not complete**. Building a number line with no gaps, the real numbers, is the starting point of calculus; see <TopicLink id="math.number.real-numbers">real numbers and irrationality</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.analysis.sequences-series">sequences and series</TopicLink>. Another surprise: although the rationals are dense, they can be listed one after another — $\Q$ is <TopicLink id="math.found.countability">countable</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Set-theoretic connection">

The number systems sit inside one another: $\N \subseteq \Z \subseteq \Q$. At university you may **build** $\Q$ from $\Z$: a rational number is a *family* of pairs of integers $(a, b)$ with $b \neq 0$, where $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ belong to the same family exactly when $ad = bc$. The family of $(1, 2)$ contains $(2, 4)$, $(-3, -6)$, $(50, 100)$, …

For this to work, “same family” must be an <TopicLink id="math.found.equivalence-relations">equivalence relation</TopicLink>, and the fraction rules must give answers in the same family whichever members we pick: replacing $\tfrac12$ by $\tfrac24$ in a sum must not change the result. Both can be proved; see <TopicLink id="math.number.number-system-constructions">constructing ℤ, ℚ and ℝ</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="A glimpse of structure">

$\Q$ is a **field**: addition and multiplication are commutative and associative, have identities $0$ and $1$, are linked by the distributive law, every number has an opposite, and every non-zero number has a reciprocal. It is also the **smallest** field that contains the integers: any field containing $\Z$ (with the same addition and multiplication) contains every $a \cdot b^{-1}$, and so contains a copy of $\Q$. Describing an object as “the smallest one with a property” is an example of a *universal property*, a central idea of <TopicLink id="math.algebra.algebraic-structures">algebraic structures</TopicLink> and of category theory.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Egypt.** The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (about 1550 BCE, copied by the scribe Ahmes from an older text) writes fractions as sums of different unit fractions, for example $\tfrac25 = \tfrac13 + \tfrac1{15}$.
- **Mesopotamia.** Babylonian scribes used base-$60$ fractions. The clay tablet YBC 7289 (about 1800–1600 BCE) gives $\sqrt2 \approx 1;24,51,10 = 1 + \tfrac{24}{60} + \tfrac{51}{60^2} + \tfrac{10}{60^3} \approx 1.4142130$, within about $6 \times 10^{-7}$ of the true value.
- **China.** *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* (compiled by about the 1st century CE) gives rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions, and reduces them to lowest terms by repeated subtraction — the method we now call Euclid's algorithm.
- **Greece.** Greek mathematicians of the 5th century BCE discovered that the side and the diagonal of a square have no common measure — in modern terms, that $\sqrt2$ is irrational. Aristotle refers to a proof by odd and even numbers. Later stories attribute the discovery to the Pythagoreans; the details are uncertain.
- **India and the Islamic world.** Indian mathematicians wrote a fraction as one number above another; mathematicians writing in Arabic, such as al-Ḥaṣṣār in 12th-century Morocco, are usually credited with adding the horizontal bar. The earliest known decimal fractions appear in the arithmetic book of al-Uqlīdisī (Damascus, 952–953). Jamshīd al-Kāshī used them systematically in *The Key to Arithmetic* (Samarkand, 1427), and Simon Stevin's booklet *De Thiende* (1585) spread them in Europe.
- **Repeating decimals.** Carl Friedrich Gauss studied the lengths of the repeating blocks of $\tfrac1p$ in his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801). Whether infinitely many primes $p$ give $\tfrac1p$ a block of the full length $p - 1$ (as $7$ and $17$ do) is still unproved: it is a case of Artin's conjecture on primitive roots.

</Section>
