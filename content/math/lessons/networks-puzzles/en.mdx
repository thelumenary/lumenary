<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Seven bridges and a Sunday walk">

About three hundred years ago, the city of Königsberg sat on both banks of a river. In the middle of the river was an island, and the river split in two around a piece of land to the east. Seven bridges joined the four pieces of land.

People liked to stroll around the city on Sundays, and a puzzle went round: **can you go for a walk that crosses every bridge exactly once?** You may start and finish anywhere, but you must not cross any bridge twice. Many people tried. Nobody managed it — but nobody could explain *why* not, either.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Networks, dots and degrees">

<Definition term="network">

A **network** is a collection of **dots** with **lines** joining some pairs of dots. Mathematicians also call a network a **graph**, the dots **vertices** and the lines **edges**. Two dots may be joined by more than one line, like the two bridges from the island to the north bank.

</Definition>

Only the connections matter. We may draw the lines curved or straight, long or short, and move the dots around: as long as the same dots are joined by the same number of lines, it is the same network. That is why we could turn the map of Königsberg into four dots and seven lines.

<Definition term="degree">

The **degree** of a dot is the number of line ends at that dot. A dot is **odd** if its degree is an odd number (1, 3, 5, …) and **even** if its degree is even (0, 2, 4, …).

</Definition>

<Definition term="tracing in one stroke">

To **trace a network in one stroke** means to walk along its lines, from dot to dot, using **every line exactly once**. You may start and finish at any dots, and you may pass through a dot as often as you like.

</Definition>

<Definition term="connected">

A network is **connected** if you can get from any dot to any other dot by walking along lines.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Counting line ends">

<Example title="The house">

Count the line ends at each corner of the house:

| Dot | A | B | C | D | E |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Degree | 3 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 2 |

Two dots are odd: **A** and **B**, the two bottom corners. Did you notice? The tracings that work start at one bottom corner and finish at the other — for example **A B C D A C E D B**.

</Example>

<Example title="The envelope and the bridges">

In the envelope (the house without its roof) every corner has degree 3: **four** odd dots. Nobody can trace it in one stroke.

In Königsberg the island has 5 bridges, and each of the other three pieces of land has 3. Again **four** odd dots. And again nobody could find the walk.

</Example>

<Example title="A careful non-example">

Draw two triangles that do not touch. Every dot has degree 2, so there are no odd dots at all — yet you cannot trace both triangles in one stroke, because you cannot get from one triangle to the other without lifting your pencil. The network is not **connected**. The pattern we are about to prove only talks about connected networks.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proof" kind="proof" title="Why the odd dots decide everything">

<Theorem name="The handshake rule">

In every network, the degrees of all the dots add up to **twice the number of lines**.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Every line has exactly two ends, and every end sits at exactly one dot. When we add up the degrees, we count every line end once — so we count every line twice.

</Proof>

The house has 8 lines, and its degrees add up to $3 + 3 + 4 + 4 + 2 = 16 = 2 \times 8$. Königsberg has 7 bridges and $5 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 14 = 2 \times 7$.

<Note>

It is called the handshake rule because of a party: if everyone counts how many hands they shook, the counts add up to twice the number of handshakes, since every handshake involves two hands. One surprising consequence: **the number of odd dots is always even.** (A sum of numbers is even only when it has an even number of odd numbers in it.) So no network has exactly one odd dot, or exactly three.

</Note>

Now the big question: why do the odd dots matter for tracing?

<Theorem name="Euler's observation">

If a network can be traced in one stroke, then every odd dot is either **where the route starts** or **where it ends**. So a network with **more than two** odd dots can never be traced in one stroke.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Follow the route and look at one dot that is neither the start nor the end. Every time the route arrives at this dot along one line, it must leave along a different line — it cannot stop there, because it does not end there. So the route uses the line ends at this dot **in pairs**: one in, one out. Since the route uses every line exactly once, it uses every line end at the dot, and all of them come in pairs. So the degree of the dot is even.

That means an odd dot must be the start or the end. A route has only one start and one end, so there can be at most two odd dots. Königsberg and the envelope have four odd dots: they can never be traced, however clever we are.

</Proof>

The same argument tells us more. If the route **finishes where it started**, then the start dot also uses its line ends in pairs (the first line out pairs with the last line in). So a route that returns home is possible only if **every** dot is even.

And it tells you where to start: in the house, the odd dots A and B must be the two ends of the route. Starting at the roof top E can never work.

What about the other direction? Here is the full answer, which Leonhard Euler described in his paper of 1736.

<Theorem name="Euler's rule for one-stroke figures">

A **connected** network can be traced in one stroke **exactly when** it has **no** odd dots or **exactly two** odd dots. With two odd dots, the route must start at one of them and end at the other. With no odd dots, you can start anywhere, and you will finish where you started.

</Theorem>

We proved one half: more than two odd dots means it is impossible. The other half — that with 0 or 2 odd dots a connected network *can* always be traced — is also true. Here is the idea, as a sketch rather than a full proof: start at an odd dot (or anywhere, if there are none) and walk along unused lines until you are stuck. By the in-and-out argument you can only get stuck at the other odd dot (or back at the start). If some lines are still unused, they form loops that touch your route somewhere, because the network is connected; walk each loop when you reach it, and splice it into the route. A complete proof was first published by Carl Hierholzer in 1873.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So it isn't the shape of the figure that matters at all — only the numbers on the dots! A huge, complicated-looking figure can be easy, and a simple-looking envelope can be impossible.

</Narrator>

<Example title="Fewest roads or fewest kilometres?">

The route **Home → Well → Market** uses only 2 roads: $7 + 6 = 13$ km.

The route **Home → School → Bridge → Market** uses 3 roads: $2 + 3 + 4 = 9$ km.

The route with more roads is 4 km **shorter**. "Fewest roads" and "shortest" are different questions!

</Example>

How can we be sure 9 km is the shortest? On a small map we can **list every route** in an organised way and add up each one. There are only four routes from Home to Market that never visit a place twice, and 9 km is the smallest total.

On a big map there are far too many routes to list. In 1956 the Dutch computer scientist **Edsger Dijkstra** invented a cleverer method (published in 1959): spread out from home like ripples in a pond, always settling the **nearest place not yet settled**, and keep a note of the best distance found to each place. The School (2 km) is settled first, then the Bridge ($2 + 3 = 5$ km), then the Well (7 km, both ways), and finally the Market ($5 + 4 = 9$ km). Route-finding apps use ideas like this every day.

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon likes statements that are exactly true or exactly false. Decide for each one, and give a reason.

1. "In every network, the degrees add up to an even number." — **True.** They add up to twice the number of lines.
2. "There is a network with exactly three odd dots." — **False.** The number of odd dots is always even.
3. "A route that traces the house can start at the roof top E." — **False.** The odd dots A and B must be the start and the end.
4. "The bridges of Königsberg can be crossed in one walk if you start on the island." — **False.** Four odd dots: no starting place works.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Statement 4 says "no walk works, wherever it starts". The seven bridges could be crossed in many thousands of different orders, and Lean's version of the theorem even covers lists of dots of any length. Trying them one by one would take a very long time — and it would not explain anything. The odd-dot proof covers them all at once, and says *why*.

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"Whether I can trace it depends on how complicated it looks."** It depends only on the degrees of the dots (and on the figure being connected). The envelope looks simple and is impossible; the house looks harder and is possible. Count the odd dots before you start.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If it can be traced, I can start anywhere."** With two odd dots you must start at one odd dot and finish at the other. Only when every dot is even can you start anywhere.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The shortest route is the one with the fewest roads."** The number of roads and the total distance are different things. On Wanjiru's map the 2-road route is 13 km and a 3-road route is 9 km.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A dot's degree is the number of dots it touches."** Degree counts line **ends**. In Königsberg the island touches only three other pieces of land, but it has five bridges, so its degree is 5.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Only the connections matter">

Euler said the bridges problem belonged to the "geometry of position": distances, angles and shapes play no part. You may stretch, bend and move the lines as much as you like, and the answer does not change, as long as you do not cut a line or join new ones. Ideas that survive stretching and bending belong to **topology**. Euler's later formula $V - E + F = 2$ for the corners, edges and faces of a solid is another early example — see <TopicLink id="math.geom.surfaces-euler-characteristic">surfaces and the Euler characteristic</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="Checking a rule versus trying everything">

To decide whether a figure can be traced, a computer *could* try every route — but the number of routes grows enormously as figures get bigger. Counting the odd dots takes just one look at each line. Good mathematics often turns a huge search into a quick check. For shortest routes on real road maps with millions of places, listing routes is hopeless; methods like Dijkstra's are what make route-finding possible (<TopicLink id="math.comp.algorithm-design">algorithm design</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Where networks lead">

In set language, a simple network is a set of dots together with a set of lines, where each line is a pair of dots. Königsberg needs a little more, because two different bridges join the same two pieces of land: we must remember the bridges as separate lines, not just which pairs of dots are joined. That is why the Lean code uses a *list* of lines.

Later, in <TopicLink id="math.discrete.graph-basics">graphs</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.discrete.graph-theory">graph theory</TopicLink>, you will meet trees, colouring, matchings and much more — all built from dots and lines.

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Leonhard Euler**, a Swiss mathematician working in St Petersburg, solved the Königsberg puzzle in a paper dated 1736 (printed in 1741). He showed that a walk crossing every bridge once is impossible and stated when such walks exist. This paper is often called the beginning of graph theory. The complete proof that 0 or 2 odd dots are enough was published by **Carl Hierholzer** in 1873.
- In Angola, Zambia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, storytellers of the **Chokwe** people draw **sona** (singular *lusona*) in the sand: a grid of dots with a line that winds around them, often drawn in one continuous stroke while a story is told. The mathematician **Paulus Gerdes** studied the geometry of these drawings.
- In Tamil Nadu and other parts of South India, many families draw **kolam** at their doorsteps each morning: lines looped around a grid of dots, some made of a single closed line.
- The word *graph* for a network was introduced by the English mathematician **James Joseph Sylvester** in 1878. Today networks describe roads, the internet, friendships, food webs and molecules.
