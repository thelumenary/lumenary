<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Why should complex numbers constrain topology?">

Take $\C^2 \setminus \{0\}$ and identify each point $z$ with $2z$. The quotient $H = (\C^2 \setminus \{0\}) / (z \sim 2z)$ — a fundamental domain is the shell $1 \le |z| \le 2$ with its two boundary spheres glued — is a compact complex surface — the **Hopf surface** — and it is diffeomorphic to $S^1 \times S^3$. Its first Betti number is $b_1 = 1$.

No smooth complex projective surface, and more generally no compact Kähler manifold, can have $b_1 = 1$. The reason is one of the great theorems of twentieth-century geometry: on a compact Kähler manifold every cohomology class splits into pieces of type $(p, q)$, and the pieces of types $(p, q)$ and $(q, p)$ are complex conjugates of each other. So $b_1 = h^{1,0} + h^{0,1} = 2h^{1,0}$ is even.

The same **Hodge decomposition** organises a whole landscape. It explains why algebraic varieties have rigid topology; it gives Calabi–Yau manifolds their Hodge diamonds (the quintic threefold has $h^{2,1} = 101$, a number string theorists and enumerative geometers care about); and it frames the **Hodge conjecture**, one of the seven Millennium Problems — the question of which cohomology classes come from algebraic subvarieties. This lesson is an orientation to that landscape: the definitions, the linear algebra proved in full, the Hodge theorem stated precisely with its consequences proved, worked Hodge diamonds, and a clear line between theorems and open problems.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Three structures that fit together">

On $\C^n = \R^{2n}$ three structures live together:

- the Euclidean **metric** $g = \sum (dx_j^2 + dy_j^2)$;
- the **complex structure** $J$, multiplication by $i$, with $J \partial_{x_j} = \partial_{y_j}$;
- the **symplectic form** $\omega = \sum dx_j \wedge dy_j = \frac{i}{2} \sum dz_j \wedge d\bar z_j$.

They determine each other: $\omega(u, v) = g(Ju, v)$, and $J$ is an isometry. A **Kähler manifold** is a manifold that carries all three, compatibly, with $J$ coming from holomorphic coordinates and $\omega$ closed. The striking fact is that this is equivalent to asking that $J$ be **parallel** for the Levi-Civita connection of $g$ — so the complex structure, the metric and the symplectic form are "locked together" as tightly as possible. A Kähler metric osculates the flat metric to second order in suitable holomorphic coordinates: all its curvature appears at second order, never at first.

Why does this constrain topology? Hodge's insight was to represent each de Rham class by its unique **harmonic** form — the one of least energy in its class. On a Kähler manifold the Laplacian is so well adapted to the complex structure that it does not mix types: the $(p, q)$-parts of a harmonic form are harmonic. Topology (de Rham cohomology) thereby inherits the algebra of types, and complex conjugation exchanges $(p, q)$ with $(q, p)$.

<Analogy>

Think of a harmonic form as a standing wave on a drum. On a generic drum, waves of different "polarisations" interfere and mix; on a Kähler manifold the drum is so symmetric that the polarisations $(p, q)$ vibrate independently. This is only a picture: the actual statement is the identity $\Delta_d = 2\Delta_{\bar\partial}$ proved below from the Kähler identities.

</Analogy>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

The prerequisites are <TopicLink id="math.geom.riemann-surfaces">Riemann surfaces</TopicLink>, <TopicLink id="math.geom.riemannian-geometry">Riemannian geometry</TopicLink>, <TopicLink id="math.geom.vector-bundles-characteristic-classes">vector bundles and Chern classes</TopicLink> and the language of <TopicLink id="math.geom.algebraic-geometry">algebraic geometry</TopicLink>.

<Definition term="complex manifold and almost complex structure">

A **complex manifold** of dimension $n$ is a manifold with an atlas of charts to open subsets of $\C^n$ whose transition maps are holomorphic. Multiplication by $i$ in charts defines an endomorphism $J$ of the tangent bundle with $J^2 = -1$. Any such $J$ on a manifold is an **almost complex structure**; by the Newlander–Nirenberg theorem (1957) it comes from a complex structure exactly when its Nijenhuis tensor vanishes.

</Definition>

<Definition term="forms of type (p, q) and the Dolbeault complex">

On a complex manifold, complex-valued forms split as $\mathcal{A}^k = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} \mathcal{A}^{p,q}$, where locally $\mathcal{A}^{p,q}$ is spanned by $dz_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge dz_{i_p} \wedge d\bar z_{j_1} \wedge \dots \wedge d\bar z_{j_q}$. Integrability gives $d = \partial + \bar\partial$ with $\partial : \mathcal{A}^{p,q} \to \mathcal{A}^{p+1,q}$, $\bar\partial : \mathcal{A}^{p,q} \to \mathcal{A}^{p,q+1}$, and $\partial^2 = \bar\partial^2 = \partial\bar\partial + \bar\partial\partial = 0$. The **Dolbeault cohomology** is $H^{p,q}_{\bar\partial}(X) = \ker \bar\partial / \operatorname{im} \bar\partial$ on $\mathcal{A}^{p,q}$; by Dolbeault's theorem $H^{p,q}_{\bar\partial}(X) \cong H^q(X, \Omega^p)$, sheaf cohomology of holomorphic $p$-forms.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Hermitian metric, fundamental form, Kähler metric">

A **Hermitian metric** on a complex manifold is a Riemannian metric $g$ with $g(Ju, Jv) = g(u, v)$. Its **fundamental form** is $\omega(u, v) = g(Ju, v)$, a real $2$-form of type $(1,1)$; in coordinates $\omega = \frac{i}{2}\sum g_{j\bar k}\, dz_j \wedge d\bar z_k$ with $(g_{j\bar k})$ a positive definite Hermitian matrix of functions. The metric is **Kähler** if $d\omega = 0$; then $\omega$ is the **Kähler form** and $[\omega] \in H^2(X; \R)$ the **Kähler class**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Hodge numbers, canonical bundle, Calabi–Yau">

For a compact complex manifold $X$ the **Hodge numbers** are $h^{p,q} = \dim_\C H^{p,q}_{\bar\partial}(X)$; on a compact Kähler manifold they equal the dimensions of the spaces of harmonic $(p,q)$-forms for any Kähler metric. The **canonical bundle** is $K_X = \Lambda^n T^{*1,0}X$, the line bundle of holomorphic $n$-forms, and $c_1(X) = -c_1(K_X)$. A **Calabi–Yau manifold** is a compact Kähler manifold with trivial canonical bundle; many authors also require it to be simply connected, or that $h^{p,0} = 0$ for $0 \lt p \lt n$, so check the convention in use.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Examples and a non-example">

<Example title="Flat models: ℂⁿ and complex tori">

$\C^n$ with $\omega = \frac{i}{2}\sum dz_j \wedge d\bar z_j$ is Kähler. For a lattice $\Lambda \cong \Z^{2n}$, the torus $X = \C^n / \Lambda$ inherits the flat Kähler metric. Translation-invariant forms $dz_I \wedge d\bar z_J$ with $|I| = p$, $|J| = q$ are harmonic, and they span the harmonic $(p,q)$-forms, so $h^{p,q}(X) = \binom{n}{p}\binom{n}{q}$. Summing over $p + q = k$ gives $\binom{2n}{k}$ — the Betti numbers of the real torus $T^{2n}$, by Vandermonde's identity. Only some complex tori are projective (abelian varieties): exactly those satisfying the Riemann bilinear relations.

</Example>

<Example title="Projective space and the Fubini–Study metric">

On $\mathbb{CP}^n$ with homogeneous coordinates $[Z_0 : \dots : Z_n]$, the form $\omega_{FS} = \frac{i}{2}\partial\bar\partial \log |Z|^2$ is well defined (changing the representative multiplies $|Z|^2$ by $|\lambda|^2$, and $\partial\bar\partial \log |\lambda|^2 = 0$), closed and positive: this is the **Fubini–Study** Kähler metric (normalisations vary by constant factors). In the affine chart $Z_0 = 1$ it has the Kähler potential $\log(1 + |z|^2)$. On $\mathbb{CP}^1$ it is $\frac{dx\, dy}{(1 + |z|^2)^2}$, of total area $\pi$, the round metric on a sphere of radius $\frac12$. The computation section verifies the Kähler condition for it symbolically.

</Example>

<Example title="Projective manifolds are Kähler">

If $Y \subset X$ is a complex submanifold of a Kähler manifold, then $g|_Y$ is Hermitian, its fundamental form is $\omega|_Y$, and $d(\omega|_Y) = (d\omega)|_Y = 0$. So **every smooth complex projective variety is Kähler**, with the restriction of $\omega_{FS}$. This is the main source of examples: smooth hypersurfaces such as the **quartic K3 surface** $\{x_0^4 + x_1^4 + x_2^4 + x_3^4 = 0\} \subset \mathbb{CP}^3$ and the **quintic threefold** $\{\sum_{i=0}^4 x_i^5 = 0\} \subset \mathbb{CP}^4$, both Calabi–Yau.

</Example>

<Example title="Riemann surfaces">

On a complex curve every Hermitian metric is Kähler: $d\omega$ is a $3$-form on a real surface, hence zero. So compact Riemann surfaces are Kähler, with $h^{1,0} = h^{0,1} = g$ (the genus) and $b_1 = 2g$ — the familiar fact that the first Betti number of a closed orientable surface is even appears as Hodge symmetry.

</Example>

<Example title="A non-example: the Hopf surface">

The Hopf surface $H = (\C^2 \setminus 0)/(z \sim 2z)$ is a compact complex manifold diffeomorphic to $S^1 \times S^3$, so $b_1(H) = 1$ and $b_2(H) = 0$. It carries the Hermitian metric $\frac{|dz_1|^2 + |dz_2|^2}{|z|^2}$, invariant under $z \mapsto 2z$, which is not Kähler (computed below). And it carries **no** Kähler metric at all: either because $b_1$ is odd, or because a Kähler class would give $[\omega] \neq 0$ in $H^2(H; \R) = 0$.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="linear" kind="proof" title="Linear algebra and the Kähler condition">

<Theorem name="Proposition 1 — the compatible triple">

Let $(V, J)$ be a real vector space with $J^2 = -1$ and $g$ an inner product with $g(Ju, Jv) = g(u, v)$. Then $\omega(u, v) = g(Ju, v)$ is alternating, $J$-invariant and non-degenerate, with $\omega(u, Ju) = |u|^2$; and $h = g - i\omega$ is a Hermitian inner product on the complex vector space $(V, J)$, linear in the first variable. Conversely $g = \operatorname{Re} h$ and $\omega = -\operatorname{Im} h$. In a unitary basis $e_1, Je_1, \dots, e_n, Je_n$, $\omega = \sum e_j^* \wedge (Je_j)^*$ and $\omega^n / n!$ is the Riemannian volume form.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$\omega(v, u) = g(Jv, u) = g(J^2 v, Ju) = -g(v, Ju) = -\omega(u, v)$, using $J$-invariance of $g$ and symmetry. $\omega(Ju, Jv) = g(J^2 u, Jv) = g(Ju, v) = \omega(u, v)$ after applying $J$-invariance once more. $\omega(u, Ju) = g(Ju, Ju) = |u|^2 \gt 0$ for $u \ne 0$, so $\omega$ is non-degenerate. For $h$: $h(iu, v) = h(Ju, v) = g(Ju, v) - i\omega(Ju, v) = \omega(u, v) + i g(u, v) = i\, h(u, v)$, using $\omega(Ju, v) = g(J^2 u, v) = -g(u, v)$; and $h(v, u) = \overline{h(u, v)}$ by the symmetries just proved. Gram–Schmidt for $h$ gives a unitary basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$; then $e_1, Je_1, \dots$ is $g$-orthonormal, $\omega(e_j, Je_j) = 1$ and all other pairings vanish, which gives the formulas for $\omega$ and $\omega^n / n! = e_1^* \wedge (Je_1)^* \wedge \cdots$.

</Proof>

(Mathlib's inner product is conjugate-linear in the *first* variable, so the formal version uses $\omega = \operatorname{Im}\langle u, v\rangle$; the two conventions differ by a conjugation.)

<Theorem name="Proposition 2 — when is a Hermitian metric Kähler?">

For a Hermitian metric $\omega = \frac{i}{2}\sum g_{j\bar k}\, dz_j \wedge d\bar z_k$ on an open set of $\C^n$, the following are equivalent: (a) $d\omega = 0$; (b) $\partial_l g_{j\bar k} = \partial_j g_{l\bar k}$ for all $j, k, l$; (c) near each point $\omega = \frac{i}{2}\partial\bar\partial\varphi$ for a real function $\varphi$, a **Kähler potential**.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Since $\omega$ is real, $d\omega = 0$ iff $\partial\omega = 0$ (then $\bar\partial\omega = \overline{\partial\omega} = 0$ too). Now $\partial\omega = \frac{i}{2}\sum_{j,k,l} \partial_l g_{j\bar k}\, dz_l \wedge dz_j \wedge d\bar z_k$, and the coefficient of $dz_l \wedge dz_j \wedge d\bar z_k$ for $l \lt j$ is $\frac{i}{2}(\partial_l g_{j\bar k} - \partial_j g_{l\bar k})$, so (a) ⟺ (b). If (c) holds, $g_{j\bar k} = \partial_j \bar\partial_k \varphi$ and $\partial_l g_{j\bar k} = \partial_l\partial_j\bar\partial_k \varphi$ is symmetric in $l, j$, which is (b); more conceptually $d\,\partial\bar\partial = (\partial + \bar\partial)\partial\bar\partial = \partial^2\bar\partial - \partial\bar\partial^2 = 0$. The converse (a) ⟹ (c) is the **local $\partial\bar\partial$-lemma**. On a polydisc, the Poincaré lemma gives a real $1$-form $\alpha$ with $\omega = d\alpha$; write $\alpha = \alpha^{1,0} + \alpha^{0,1}$ with $\alpha^{1,0} = \overline{\alpha^{0,1}}$. Comparing types in $d\alpha = \omega$, the $(0,2)$-part gives $\bar\partial\alpha^{0,1} = 0$, and the $(1,1)$-part gives $\omega = \bar\partial\alpha^{1,0} + \partial\alpha^{0,1}$. By the Dolbeault–Grothendieck lemma (the $\bar\partial$-Poincaré lemma on a polydisc), $\alpha^{0,1} = \bar\partial f$ for a function $f$, and then $\alpha^{1,0} = \partial\bar f$. Hence $\omega = \bar\partial\partial\bar f + \partial\bar\partial f = \partial\bar\partial(f - \bar f) = \frac{i}{2}\partial\bar\partial(4\operatorname{Im} f)$, so $\varphi = 4\operatorname{Im} f$ is a real Kähler potential.

</Proof>

Criterion (b) is what the second experiment tests symbolically: the Fubini–Study metric passes, the Hopf metric $|dz|^2/|z|^2$ fails.

<Theorem name="Proposition 3 — Kähler classes and complex submanifolds are non-trivial">

Let $(X, \omega)$ be a compact Kähler manifold of dimension $n$. Then $[\omega]^k \neq 0$ in $H^{2k}(X; \R)$ for $0 \le k \le n$, so $b_{2k}(X) \ge 1$. If $Y \subset X$ is a compact complex submanifold of dimension $k$, then $\int_Y \omega^k = k!\,\mathrm{vol}(Y) \gt 0$, so the homology class $[Y]$ is non-zero.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By Proposition 1, $\omega^n/n!$ is the volume form, so $\int_X \omega^n \gt 0$. If $\omega^k = d\beta$ then $\omega^n = d(\beta \wedge \omega^{n-k})$ and Stokes gives $\int_X \omega^n = 0$. For $Y$, $\omega|_Y$ is the Kähler form of the induced metric, so $\omega^k|_Y / k!$ is its volume form; if $[Y] = 0$ then $\int_Y \omega^k = \langle [\omega]^k, [Y] \rangle = 0$.

</Proof>

This is the same argument as for symplectic manifolds (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.symplectic-geometry">symplectic geometry</TopicLink>): every Kähler manifold is symplectic. The converse fails — Thurston's example (1976), the Kodaira–Thurston manifold, is a compact symplectic $4$-manifold with $b_1 = 3$, which by the next section cannot be Kähler.

</Section>

<Section id="hodge" kind="proof" title="Hodge theory: the decomposition and its consequences">

Let $(X, g)$ be compact Kähler of complex dimension $n$. With the formal adjoints $d^*, \partial^*, \bar\partial^*$ for the $L^2$ inner product on forms, define the Laplacians $\Delta_d = dd^* + d^*d$ and $\Delta_{\bar\partial} = \bar\partial\bar\partial^* + \bar\partial^*\bar\partial$, and let $L = \omega \wedge \cdot$ with adjoint $\Lambda$.

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 — Hodge theorem (stated)">

On a compact oriented Riemannian manifold every de Rham class contains exactly one harmonic form ($\Delta_d \alpha = 0$), and $\mathcal{H}^k \cong H^k(X; \R)$. On a compact Hermitian manifold, likewise, $\mathcal{H}^{p,q}_{\bar\partial} = \ker \Delta_{\bar\partial} \cap \mathcal{A}^{p,q} \cong H^{p,q}_{\bar\partial}(X)$, and these spaces are finite-dimensional.

</Theorem>

The proof is elliptic PDE: $\Delta$ is an elliptic operator, so it has a Green operator on a compact manifold. See Griffiths–Harris Ch. 0 §6, Wells Ch. IV, or Warner's *Foundations of Differentiable Manifolds and Lie Groups*, Ch. 6.

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 — Kähler identities (stated)">

On a Kähler manifold $[\Lambda, \bar\partial] = -i\partial^*$ and $[\Lambda, \partial] = i\bar\partial^*$. Consequently $\Delta_d = 2\Delta_\partial = 2\Delta_{\bar\partial}$; in particular $\Delta_d$ preserves bidegree and commutes with $L$ and $\Lambda$.

</Theorem>

The identities themselves are first-order and pointwise. Since a Kähler metric agrees with the flat metric to second order at any point in suitable holomorphic coordinates, it suffices to check them on $\C^n$, where they are a computation (Huybrechts, Prop. 3.1.12; Griffiths–Harris, pp. 111–115). The consequences for the Laplacians follow in two lines. First, from $i\bar\partial^* = [\Lambda, \partial]$ and $\partial^2 = 0$,

$$
i(\partial\bar\partial^* + \bar\partial^*\partial) = \partial\Lambda\partial - \partial^2\Lambda + \Lambda\partial^2 - \partial\Lambda\partial = 0,
$$

and its conjugate $\bar\partial\partial^* + \partial^*\bar\partial = 0$ as well, so the mixed terms in $\Delta_d = (\partial + \bar\partial)(\partial^* + \bar\partial^*) + (\partial^* + \bar\partial^*)(\partial + \bar\partial)$ vanish and $\Delta_d = \Delta_\partial + \Delta_{\bar\partial}$. Second, substituting $-i\partial^* = [\Lambda, \bar\partial]$ and $i\bar\partial^* = [\Lambda, \partial]$,

$$
-i\Delta_\partial + i\Delta_{\bar\partial} = \partial\Lambda\bar\partial - \partial\bar\partial\Lambda + \Lambda\bar\partial\partial - \bar\partial\Lambda\partial + \bar\partial\Lambda\partial - \bar\partial\partial\Lambda + \Lambda\partial\bar\partial - \partial\Lambda\bar\partial = -(\partial\bar\partial + \bar\partial\partial)\Lambda + \Lambda(\bar\partial\partial + \partial\bar\partial) = 0,
$$

so $\Delta_\partial = \Delta_{\bar\partial}$ and $\Delta_d = 2\Delta_{\bar\partial}$.

<Theorem name="Theorem 6 — Hodge decomposition and Hodge symmetry">

For a compact Kähler manifold $X$,

$$
H^k(X; \C) \;\cong\; \bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(X), \qquad H^{q,p}(X) = \overline{H^{p,q}(X)},
$$

where $H^{p,q}(X)$ is the space of classes represented by closed forms of type $(p,q)$; it is isomorphic to $H^{p,q}_{\bar\partial}(X)$. The decomposition does not depend on the Kähler metric.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By Theorem 4, $H^k(X; \C) \cong \mathcal{H}^k_{\Delta_d}$. By Theorem 5, $\Delta_d$ preserves bidegree, so if $\alpha = \sum \alpha^{p,q}$ is $\Delta_d$-harmonic then $0 = \Delta_d \alpha = \sum \Delta_d \alpha^{p,q}$ with each summand of a different type, hence each $\alpha^{p,q}$ is $\Delta_d$-harmonic. Thus $\mathcal{H}^k_{\Delta_d} = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} \mathcal{H}^{p,q}_{\Delta_d}$, and $\mathcal{H}^{p,q}_{\Delta_d} = \mathcal{H}^{p,q}_{\Delta_{\bar\partial}} \cong H^{p,q}_{\bar\partial}(X)$ because $\Delta_d = 2\Delta_{\bar\partial}$. Since $\Delta_d$ is a real operator, complex conjugation maps harmonic forms to harmonic forms and $(p,q)$-forms to $(q,p)$-forms, giving $\mathcal{H}^{q,p} = \overline{\mathcal{H}^{p,q}}$. Independence of the metric: the harmonic projection is built from $\Delta_d$, which preserves bidegree, so it commutes with the projection onto type $(p,q)$; hence the harmonic representative of a closed $(p,q)$-form is again of type $(p,q)$, and $H^{p,q}(X)$ is exactly the set of classes having *some* closed representative of type $(p,q)$ — a description that does not mention the metric.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Corollary 7 — topological restrictions">

Let $X$ be compact Kähler of dimension $n$. (i) $b_k = \sum_{p+q=k} h^{p,q}$, $h^{p,q} = h^{q,p}$, and $h^{p,q} = h^{n-p,n-q}$ (Serre duality). (ii) **Odd Betti numbers are even.** (iii) Holomorphic $p$-forms are closed and harmonic, and $h^{p,0} = \dim H^0(X, \Omega^p)$. (iv) The Hopf surface, and every compact complex surface with odd $b_1$, is not Kähler.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

(i) is Theorem 6 plus Serre duality. (ii) For $k = 2m+1$, the involution $p \mapsto k - p$ of $\{0, \dots, k\}$ has no fixed point, so $b_k = \sum_{p=0}^{m} (h^{p,k-p} + h^{k-p,p}) = 2\sum_{p=0}^{m} h^{p, k-p}$ — this arithmetic step is formalised below. (iii) If $\alpha$ is a holomorphic $p$-form then $\bar\partial\alpha = 0$, and $\bar\partial^*\alpha = 0$ because $\bar\partial^*$ maps $\mathcal{A}^{p,0}$ to $\mathcal{A}^{p,-1} = 0$. So $\Delta_{\bar\partial}\alpha = 0$, hence $\Delta_d \alpha = 0$ and $d\alpha = 0$. Conversely a $\bar\partial$-harmonic $(p,0)$-form is $\bar\partial$-closed, i.e. holomorphic. (iv) follows from (ii).

</Proof>

Two further theorems complete the classical picture.

**Hard Lefschetz (stated).** For a compact Kähler manifold, $L^{k} : H^{n-k}(X) \to H^{n+k}(X)$ is an isomorphism for every $k \ge 0$. Consequently $L : H^{j} \to H^{j+2}$ is injective for $j \lt n$, and $b_0 \le b_2 \le b_4 \le \cdots \le b_{2\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$, similarly for odd degrees. The primitive cohomology carries the **Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations**, a definiteness property; for surfaces it gives the Hodge index theorem: the intersection form on $H^{1,1}_\R$ has signature $(1, h^{1,1} - 1)$.

**The $\partial\bar\partial$-lemma (stated).** On a compact Kähler manifold, a $d$-closed form of pure type that is $d$-exact (or $\partial$- or $\bar\partial$-exact) is $\partial\bar\partial$-exact. Deligne, Griffiths, Morgan and Sullivan (1975) deduced that compact Kähler manifolds are **formal**: their real homotopy type is determined by the cohomology ring, and all Massey products vanish. This is a third, independent topological restriction, used to show that certain symplectic manifolds with even Betti numbers are still not Kähler.

<Example title="The Hodge diamond of a K3 surface">

A **K3 surface** is a compact complex surface with trivial canonical bundle and $b_1 = 0$ (it is automatically Kähler, by a theorem of Siu, 1983). Then $h^{1,0} = h^{0,1} = 0$, $h^{2,0} = h^0(K_X) = 1$, and Noether's formula $\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = \frac{1}{12}(c_1^2 + c_2)$ with $c_1 = 0$ and $\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^{0,0} - h^{0,1} + h^{0,2} = 2$ gives $c_2 = \chi_{\mathrm{top}} = 24$. So $b_2 = 24 - 2 = 22$ and $h^{1,1} = 22 - 2 = 20$:

$$
\begin{matrix} & & 1 & & \\ & 0 & & 0 & \\ 1 & & 20 & & 1 \\ & 0 & & 0 & \\ & & 1 & & \end{matrix}
$$

By the Lefschetz $(1,1)$-theorem (below) the Picard number of a K3 surface is at most $h^{1,1} = 20$.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="calabi-yau" kind="human" title="Calabi–Yau manifolds and Yau's theorem">

The **Ricci form** of a Kähler metric, $\rho(u, v) = \operatorname{Ric}(Ju, v)$, is a closed real $(1,1)$-form; locally it is $-i\partial\bar\partial \log\det(g_{j\bar k})$ up to a positive constant fixed by the conventions, and $[\rho] = 2\pi c_1(X)$ in $H^2(X; \R)$ whatever the metric. So $c_1(X)$ is the only obstruction to Ricci-flatness within cohomology — and Calabi asked (1954–57) whether it is the only obstruction at all.

**Theorem (Yau, 1976–78; the Calabi conjecture).** *Let $X$ be compact Kähler with Kähler form $\omega$, and let $\rho'$ be any closed real $(1,1)$-form representing $2\pi c_1(X)$. Then there is a unique Kähler form $\omega'$ in the class $[\omega]$ whose Ricci form is $\rho'$. In particular, if $c_1(X) = 0$ in $H^2(X; \R)$, each Kähler class contains a unique Ricci-flat Kähler metric.*

Writing $\omega' = \omega + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi$ turns the problem into the complex Monge–Ampère equation $(\omega + i\partial\bar\partial\varphi)^n = e^{F}\omega^n$ with $\int e^F\omega^n = \int \omega^n$. Calabi proved uniqueness; Yau proved existence by the continuity method, the decisive step being an a priori $C^0$ estimate for $\varphi$. Ricci-flat Kähler metrics have holonomy in $SU(n)$; K3 surfaces and the quintic threefold carry them, although no explicit formula is known for any compact example other than flat tori and their quotients.

**Kähler–Einstein metrics** ($\rho = \lambda\omega$) exist when $c_1(X) \lt 0$ (Aubin and Yau, 1976–78) and when $c_1(X) = 0$ (Yau). When $c_1(X) \gt 0$ (Fano manifolds) there are obstructions (Matsushima, Futaki), and the Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture — existence if and only if $X$ is K-polystable — was proved for Fano manifolds by Chen, Donaldson and Sun (2015).

**The Hodge diamond of a Calabi–Yau threefold** is determined by two numbers, $h^{1,1}$ and $h^{2,1}$: for the quintic, $h^{1,1} = 1$ (the hyperplane class, by the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem) and $h^{2,1} = 101$ (the number of independent deformations of the complex structure, by the Bogomolov–Tian–Todorov theorem and $H^1(T_X) \cong H^{2,1}$). The experiment computes $101$ by counting monomials. Mirror symmetry predicts a "mirror quintic" with these two numbers exchanged; it exists (Greene–Plesser, 1990).

</Section>

<Section id="hodge-conjecture" kind="human" title="The Hodge conjecture">

Let $X$ be a smooth complex projective variety of dimension $n$. A **Hodge class** of degree $2p$ is an element of

$$
\operatorname{Hdg}^p(X) = H^{2p}(X; \Q) \cap H^{p,p}(X).
$$

If $Z \subset X$ is a subvariety of codimension $p$, its fundamental class $[Z] \in H^{2p}(X; \Q)$ is a Hodge class: integrating a closed $(r, s)$-form with $r + s = 2(n-p)$ over the complex $(n-p)$-dimensional $Z$ gives zero unless $(r, s) = (n-p, n-p)$, so the Poincaré dual of $[Z]$ pairs to zero with every $H^{r,s}$ except $H^{n-p,n-p}$, which places it in $H^{p,p}$.

**The Hodge conjecture** (Hodge, 1950, in the corrected rational form). *Every Hodge class on a smooth complex projective variety is a $\Q$-linear combination of fundamental classes of algebraic subvarieties.* It is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Problems (2000).

What is known:

- **$p = 1$: true.** The **Lefschetz $(1,1)$-theorem** (Lefschetz, 1924; sheaf-theoretic proof by Kodaira and Spencer, 1953): an integral class in $H^2(X; \Z)$ is the first Chern class of a holomorphic line bundle if and only if its image in $H^2(X; \C)$ has type $(1,1)$. Proof sketch: the exponential sequence $0 \to \Z \to \mathcal{O}_X \to \mathcal{O}_X^* \to 0$ gives $H^1(X, \mathcal{O}^*) \xrightarrow{c_1} H^2(X; \Z) \to H^2(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = H^{0,2}$, and a real class maps to zero in $H^{0,2}$ exactly when, by Hodge symmetry, its $(2,0)$ and $(0,2)$ parts vanish. On a projective variety every line bundle is $\mathcal{O}(D)$ for a divisor $D$, so $c_1 = [D]$ is algebraic.
- **$p = n - 1$: true**, by hard Lefschetz from the case $p = 1$.
- Many special cases are known — for instance for abelian varieties of dimension at most $3$, where it follows from the cases $p = 1$ and $p = n - 1$, and for various varieties with large symmetry. The conjecture is open in general, even for abelian varieties of higher dimension.

What is false — the conjecture is stated carefully for good reasons:

- **Integral coefficients.** Atiyah and Hirzebruch (1962) found torsion integral Hodge classes that are not algebraic; Kollár (1992) found non-torsion examples. The integral Hodge conjecture is false.
- **Kähler manifolds.** Voisin (2002) constructed compact Kähler manifolds whose Hodge classes are not even combinations of Chern classes of coherent sheaves; so the natural Kähler generalisation is false, and projectivity is essential.
- **Kodaira's problem.** Kodaira showed (1954) that a compact Kähler manifold with an *integral* Kähler class is projective, and (1963) that every compact Kähler surface deforms to a projective one. Voisin (2004) showed that in dimension at least $4$ there are compact Kähler manifolds not even homotopy equivalent to any projective manifold.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Every complex manifold is Kähler."** The Hopf surface $S^1 \times S^3$ is a compact complex manifold with $b_1 = 1$, so it is not Kähler; Kodaira surfaces (with $b_1 = 3$) and the Iwasawa manifold are other examples. Open subsets of $\C^n$ are Kähler (restrict the flat metric), so the interesting question is about compact manifolds.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The Hodge conjecture is about integral classes."** The integral version is false (Atiyah–Hirzebruch, 1962), and the conjecture is stated for **rational** Hodge classes on smooth **projective** varieties. Its natural extension to compact Kähler manifolds is false as well (Voisin, 2002).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Kähler just means complex plus Riemannian."** Every complex manifold has Hermitian metrics (by a partition of unity); the Kähler condition $d\omega = 0$ is a strong extra requirement, equivalent to $\nabla J = 0$, with topological consequences.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The Hodge decomposition holds on any compact complex manifold."** Dolbeault cohomology is always defined, and the Frölicher spectral sequence relates it to de Rham cohomology, but on non-Kähler manifolds $b_k$ can differ from $\sum_{p+q=k} h^{p,q}$ and $h^{p,q} \neq h^{q,p}$ can happen: on the Hopf surface $h^{0,1} = 1$ but $h^{1,0} = 0$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Symplectic manifolds are basically Kähler."** Every Kähler manifold is symplectic, but the Kodaira–Thurston manifold is a compact symplectic $4$-manifold with $b_1 = 3$, so it admits no Kähler structure. Gompf showed (1995) that every finitely presented group is the fundamental group of a closed symplectic $4$-manifold, while Kähler groups are very restricted (for instance $H_1$ of a Kähler group has even rank).

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Viewpoints">

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Which spaces can be Kähler?">

Hodge theory turns into a list of topological obstructions for a compact manifold to admit a Kähler structure: odd Betti numbers even; $[\omega]^k \neq 0$; hard Lefschetz inequalities $b_{k} \le b_{k+2}$ for $k \lt n$; formality (vanishing Massey products, Deligne–Griffiths–Morgan–Sullivan); and restrictions on the fundamental group — **Kähler groups** — beginning with even rank of $H_1$. Serre showed that every finite group is a Kähler group (indeed the fundamental group of a smooth projective variety), and which infinite groups occur is an active field. The question "does $S^6$ admit a complex structure at all?" is open; it cannot admit a Kähler one, since $b_2(S^6) = 0$.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Holonomy and special geometry">

A Riemannian manifold of real dimension $2n$ is Kähler exactly when its holonomy group lies in $U(n)$, and Ricci-flat Kähler exactly when (for simply connected $X$) it lies in $SU(n)$. In Berger's classification of holonomy groups, $U(n)$, $SU(n)$ and $Sp(n)$ (hyperkähler: K3 surfaces are the four-dimensional case) are the Kähler entries, beside the exceptional $G_2$ and $\mathrm{Spin}(7)$. Yau's theorem makes Calabi–Yau manifolds the main source of compact manifolds with special holonomy; the exceptional cases needed separate constructions by Joyce (1996).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Elliptic operators and Monge–Ampère">

Every major theorem in this lesson is analysis in disguise. The Hodge theorem is elliptic regularity and Fredholm theory for $\Delta$; the Kähler identities are a first-order commutation relation between $\bar\partial$ and the Lefschetz operator; Kodaira's vanishing and embedding theorems rest on the Bochner–Kodaira–Nakano identity and Hörmander's $L^2$ estimates for $\bar\partial$; and Yau's theorem is an a priori estimate for the fully non-linear complex Monge–Ampère equation. The analytic and algebraic sides meet in Serre's GAGA (1956): on a projective variety, analytic and algebraic coherent sheaf cohomology agree.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Hodge structures">

Abstracting the decomposition gives a **pure Hodge structure of weight $k$**: a finitely generated abelian group $V_\Z$ with $V_\C = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} V^{p,q}$ and $V^{q,p} = \overline{V^{p,q}}$ — equivalently a real representation of the Deligne torus $\mathbb{S} = \operatorname{Res}_{\C/\R}\mathbb{G}_m$. Weight-one polarised Hodge structures are the same as abelian varieties (Riemann), and the polarised Hodge structure on $H^1$ determines a curve (Torelli) and on $H^2$ a K3 surface (global Torelli: Piatetski-Shapiro–Shafarevich, 1971; Burns–Rapoport, 1975). Deligne's **mixed Hodge structures** (1971–74) extend the theory to singular and non-compact varieties, and Griffiths' **variations of Hodge structure** describe how the decomposition moves in families.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Point counts and the Weil conjectures">

For a variety defined over $\Z$, the Betti numbers of its complex points control the number of its points over finite fields. Deligne's proof of the Weil conjectures (1974) shows, for smooth projective $X$ with good reduction at $p$, that $\#X(\mathbb{F}_{p^m}) = \sum_k (-1)^k \sum_{i=1}^{b_k} \alpha_{k,i}^m$ with $|\alpha_{k,i}| = p^{k/2}$. For an elliptic curve this is Hasse's bound $|\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) - p - 1| \le 2\sqrt p$ with $b_1 = 2$. Weil computed the point counts of Fermat hypersurfaces with Jacobi sums (1949) — the same Fermat varieties whose Hodge numbers the experiment counts. The **Tate conjecture** is the $\ell$-adic sibling of the Hodge conjecture, and Faltings' Hodge–Tate decomposition (1988) is a $p$-adic analogue of Theorem 6. See <TopicLink id="math.number.arithmetic-geometry">arithmetic geometry</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="String compactification and mirror symmetry">

Candelas, Horowitz, Strominger and Witten (1985) showed that supersymmetric compactifications of the heterotic string on a six-dimensional internal space require a Calabi–Yau threefold; in their simplest models the number of particle generations is $|\chi|/2 = |h^{1,1} - h^{2,1}|$ (one hundred for the quintic, which is why it was not the answer). Mirror symmetry, discovered in physics, pairs Calabi–Yau threefolds with $h^{1,1}$ and $h^{2,1}$ exchanged. Candelas, de la Ossa, Green and Parkes (1991) used it to predict the numbers of rational curves of each degree on the quintic — $2875$ lines (classical) and $609250$ conics (Katz, 1986), and infinitely many further numbers — later proved by Givental (1996) and by Lian, Liu and Yau (1997). The physics supplied conjectures; the proofs are mathematics (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.derived-categories">derived categories</TopicLink> for Kontsevich's homological version).

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **1857–1900: periods.** Bernhard Riemann's theory of abelian integrals (1857) introduced the periods of holomorphic differentials on a curve and the bilinear relations they satisfy — Hodge theory in dimension one. Émile Picard and Henri Poincaré studied integrals on algebraic surfaces.
- **1924–1941: topology meets algebraic geometry.** Solomon Lefschetz's *L'Analysis situs et la géométrie algébrique* (1924) proved the $(1,1)$-theorem and the hyperplane and hard Lefschetz theorems (the last with a gap later repaired by Hodge theory). Georges de Rham proved his theorem (1931). Erich Kähler introduced the metrics now named after him (1933). W. V. D. Hodge developed harmonic integrals in the 1930s, culminating in *The Theory and Applications of Harmonic Integrals* (1941); gaps in its analysis were repaired by Hermann Weyl and Kunihiko Kodaira in the 1940s.
- **1946–1965: sheaves and vanishing.** Shiing-Shen Chern defined Chern classes (1946). Pierre Dolbeault proved his theorem (1953). Kodaira proved the vanishing and embedding theorems (1953–54) and received a Fields Medal in 1954; with Donald Spencer he built deformation theory (1958). Jean-Pierre Serre's GAGA appeared in 1956. In Mumbai, M. S. Narasimhan and C. S. Seshadri related stable bundles on curves to unitary representations (1965), the start of a long line of Hermitian–Einstein correspondences (Donaldson, 1985; Uhlenbeck–Yau, 1986).
- **1962–1975: limits and structures.** Michael Atiyah and Friedrich Hirzebruch disproved the integral Hodge conjecture (1962). Phillip Griffiths created the theory of periods and variations of Hodge structure (1968–70). Pierre Deligne introduced mixed Hodge theory (1971–74) and proved the Weil conjectures (1974). Deligne, Griffiths, Morgan and Sullivan proved formality (1975).
- **1976–today: Calabi–Yau.** Shing-Tung Yau proved the Calabi conjecture (announced 1977, published 1978; Fields Medal 1982). Physicists brought Calabi–Yau threefolds and mirror symmetry into mathematics (1985–91). Claire Voisin settled the Kähler Hodge and Kodaira problems negatively (2002, 2004). Xiuxiong Chen, Simon Donaldson and Song Sun proved the Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture for Fano manifolds (2015).

</Section>
