<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="One thing after another">

To convert a price from Kenyan shillings to Indian rupees, a travel app might first convert shillings to US dollars, then dollars to rupees. To edit a photo, you might rotate it and then crop it. To make chapati, you mix, then knead, then roll, then cook.

Each step is a function, and doing them in sequence produces a new function. This act of chaining is called **composition**. It is one of the simplest ideas in mathematics — and, as we'll see, one of the deepest.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The composite of two functions">

<Definition term="composition">

If $f : A \to B$ and $g : B \to C$, their **composite** $g \circ f : A \to C$ is the function

$$
(g \circ f)(x) = g\big(f(x)\big).
$$

Read $g \circ f$ as "$g$ after $f$": apply $f$ first, then $g$.

</Definition>

The composite only makes sense when the codomain of $f$ matches the domain of $g$ — the output of the first step must be a valid input for the second.

<Definition term="identity function">

For every set $A$, the **identity function** $\mathrm{id}_A : A \to A$ is defined by $\mathrm{id}_A(x) = x$. It does nothing — and that turns out to be important.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Order matters">

Let $f(x) = x + 1$ and $g(x) = 2x$, both from $\Z$ to $\Z$. Then

$$
(g \circ f)(x) = 2(x + 1) = 2x + 2, \qquad (f \circ g)(x) = 2x + 1.
$$

At $x = 0$ these give $2$ and $1$. So $g \circ f \neq f \circ g$: **composition is not commutative**.

</Example>

<Example title="A three-step chain">

Let $h(x) = x^2$ as well. Then $(h \circ g \circ f)(x) = h(g(f(x))) = \big(2(x + 1)\big)^2 = 4(x + 1)^2$. At $x = 2$: $f(2) = 3$, $g(3) = 6$, $h(6) = 36$.

</Example>

<Example title="Undoing">

Let $p(x) = x + 5$ and $q(x) = x - 5$. Then $q \circ p = \mathrm{id}_\Z$ and $p \circ q = \mathrm{id}_\Z$: each undoes the other. Functions like this are called **inverses** of one another.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="The laws of composition">

<Theorem name="associativity">

For all $f : A \to B$, $g : B \to C$, $h : C \to D$: $\quad h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Both sides are functions from $A$ to $D$, so we compare them at an arbitrary $x \in A$:

$$
\big(h \circ (g \circ f)\big)(x) = h\big((g \circ f)(x)\big) = h\big(g(f(x))\big), \qquad \big((h \circ g) \circ f\big)(x) = (h \circ g)\big(f(x)\big) = h\big(g(f(x))\big).
$$

They agree at every $x$, so they are the same function.

</Proof>

Because of associativity we can safely write $h \circ g \circ f$ with no brackets at all.

<Theorem name="identity laws">

For every $f : A \to B$: $\quad f \circ \mathrm{id}_A = f$ and $\mathrm{id}_B \circ f = f$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$(f \circ \mathrm{id}_A)(x) = f(\mathrm{id}_A(x)) = f(x)$ and $(\mathrm{id}_B \circ f)(x) = \mathrm{id}_B(f(x)) = f(x)$ for every $x \in A$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="composites of injections">

If $f : A \to B$ and $g : B \to C$ are both injective, then $g \circ f$ is injective.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose $(g \circ f)(x) = (g \circ f)(y)$, i.e. $g(f(x)) = g(f(y))$. Since $g$ is injective, $f(x) = f(y)$. Since $f$ is injective, $x = y$.

</Proof>

The same pattern of reasoning shows that composites of surjections are surjective, and therefore composites of bijections are bijective.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Look how the proof "peels" the functions in reverse order: first we use that $g$ is injective, then $f$. The outermost function is dealt with first. You'll see this peeling everywhere.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="The first category">

Step back and look at what we have:

- **objects**: sets $A, B, C, \dots$;
- **arrows** between them: functions $f : A \to B$;
- a way to **compose** arrows when they line up: $g \circ f$;
- an **identity** arrow $\mathrm{id}_A$ for every object;

and two laws: composition is **associative**, and identities are **neutral**. Any collection of objects and arrows with these features is called a **category**. Sets and functions form the category usually called $\mathbf{Set}$.

This is not just a new name. Groups with homomorphisms, vector spaces with linear maps, shapes with continuous maps, even the integers ordered by $\le$ — each of these forms a category, and theorems proved about categories in general apply to all of them at once.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Reading $g \circ f$ as "$g$ first".** It means $f$ first, then $g$ — the function written closest to $x$ in $g(f(x))$ acts first.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Composition is multiplication of formulas."** $(g \circ f)(x)$ means substituting $f(x)$ into $g$; it is not $g(x) \cdot f(x)$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If $g \circ f$ is injective, both $f$ and $g$ must be."** Only $f$ must be. Try $f : \{1\} \to \{1, 2\}$ with $f(1) = 1$ and $g : \{1, 2\} \to \{1\}$: $g \circ f$ is injective, but $g$ is not.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Associative means commutative."** Associativity is about *brackets* ($h \circ (g \circ f)$ vs $(h \circ g) \circ f$); commutativity is about *order*. Composition has the first property but not the second.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Set-theoretic connection">

In terms of graphs (sets of ordered pairs), the composite of $f \subseteq A \times B$ and $g \subseteq B \times C$ is

$$
g \circ f = \{\, (a, c) \in A \times C \mid \text{there is } b \in B \text{ with } (a, b) \in f \text{ and } (b, c) \in g \,\}.
$$

The same formula composes arbitrary **relations**, not just functions — and relations with this composition form another category, usually called $\mathbf{Rel}$.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- Repeating and chaining transformations is old: geometers and astronomers combined rotations and reflections long before there was a word for composition.
- In the 19th century, Évariste Galois, Arthur Cayley and others studied **groups** of permutations and transformations, where composition is the basic operation.
- **Samuel Eilenberg** and **Saunders Mac Lane** introduced categories in 1945, in a paper on "natural equivalences" in algebraic topology. Their idea — study objects through the arrows between them — has since spread across mathematics, computer science and logic.

</Section>
