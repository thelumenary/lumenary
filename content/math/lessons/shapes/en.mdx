<Section id="ideas" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="side and corner">

A **side** is a straight edge of a flat shape. A **corner** is where two sides meet. Grown-up mathematicians call a corner a **vertex** (and more than one, **vertices**).

</Definition>

<Definition term="triangle">

A **triangle** is a closed flat shape with **three straight sides**. It has three corners.

</Definition>

<Definition term="rectangle">

A **rectangle** is a closed flat shape with **four straight sides** and **four square corners**, like the corner of a page.

</Definition>

<Definition term="square">

A **square** is a rectangle whose **four sides all have the same length**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="circle">

A **circle** is a round flat shape. Every point on its edge is the same distance from its centre. It has one curved edge and no corners.

</Definition>

<Section id="why" kind="human" title="Why turning does not change a shape">

When you turn a shape, or slide it across the table, **every side keeps its length** and **every corner keeps its size**. A side that was 3 fingers long is still 3 fingers long. A square corner is still a square corner. Nothing was stretched, bent or cut.

The name of a shape only depends on its sides and corners. So if the sides and corners do not change, the name does not change either. A triangle pointing down is still a triangle. A square standing on a corner is still a square.

A solid shape has **faces**: the surfaces on its outside. A face can be **flat** or **curved**.

- A **cube** has 6 flat faces, all squares. It has 12 straight edges and 8 corners. It stacks, but it does not roll.
- A **cylinder** has 2 flat faces (circles) and one curved face. It rolls on its side and stacks on its flat faces.
- A **cone** has 1 flat face (a circle), one curved face and one point at the top. It rolls round in a circle.
- A **sphere** has one curved face and nothing flat at all. It rolls in every direction.

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's yes-or-no game">

Gideon reads a statement. You say **true** or **false**, and why.

1. "A square turned onto its corner is still a square." — **True.** Its sides are still all the same length, and its corners are still square.
2. "Every square is a rectangle." — **True.** A square has four square corners, which is all a rectangle needs.
3. "Every rectangle is a square." — **False!** A long rectangle, 3 steps long and 1 step tall, is a rectangle that is not a square. One example is enough.
4. "Three dots in a straight line make a triangle." — **False.** Joining them up gives a straight line, not a closed shape with three corners.

<Narrator who="gideon">

To show "every rectangle is a square" is false, I don't need to look at every rectangle in the world. I need just *one* rectangle that is not a square. That one example is called a **counterexample**.

</Narrator>

<ForGrownUps title="Margaret's program, and why her pattern always holds">

The program below tries every pair of dots as one side of a square and builds the rest of the square by a quarter turn — the same idea as the Lean file. It counts upright and tilted squares on grids of 2 × 2 up to 6 × 6 dots and compares the totals with the formula $n^2(n^2 - 1)/12$ for an $n \times n$ grid of dots.

<Python id="shapes_hidden_squares" />

The computer's counts are certain for the grids it checked, but no computer can check every grid. Here is why the formula is always right. Every square on the grid, tilted or not, fits snugly inside exactly one *upright* square box with sides of $k$ steps, touching all four sides of the box (an upright square is its own box). Inside such a box there are exactly $k$ snug squares: going $a$ steps along one side of the box to the first corner, for $a = 0, 1, \dots, k - 1$. On an $n \times n$ grid of dots a $k \times k$ box fits in $(n - k)^2$ places. So the number of squares is

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} k\,(n-k)^2 = \frac{n^2(n^2-1)}{12},
$$

where the last step is an exercise in the standard formulas for $\sum k$, $\sum k^2$ and $\sum k^3$. For $n = 3$: $1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 1 = 6$.

</ForGrownUps>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"A square on its corner is a diamond, not a square."** Turning a shape does not change its sides or its corners. A square standing on a corner still has four equal sides and four square corners, so it is still a square. ("Diamond" is an everyday word, not a different shape.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A triangle must sit on a flat bottom" or "a triangle must have equal sides."** Any closed flat shape with three straight sides is a triangle — pointing up, down or sideways, fat or thin, with equal sides or not.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Only red ones are triangles"** or **"a big square is a different shape from a small square."** Colour and size are not part of the definition. A tiny green square and a huge blue square are both squares.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A square is not a rectangle."** A rectangle needs four straight sides and four square corners — and a square has them. A square is a special rectangle, the way a kitten is a special cat.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="sorting" kind="sets" title="Sorting shapes into collections">

Sorting shapes is a great way to *see* their properties. Try sorting a pile of shapes into two collections: "has four sides" and "does not have four sides". Then sort the same pile a different way: "has a square corner" and "does not".

Some shapes belong to **both** "has four sides" and "has a square corner" — rectangles and squares, for example. Drawing two overlapping loops shows this. These loops are called **Venn diagrams**, and you will meet them in <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">Sets</TopicLink>. They grow from the very first lesson on <TopicLink id="math.found.collections">Collections</TopicLink>.

<ForGrownUps title="the square-inside-rectangle picture">

"Every square is a rectangle" says that the collection of squares is a **subset** of the collection of rectangles: $\text{squares} \subseteq \text{rectangles}$. "Not every rectangle is a square" says the subset is not the whole thing. Children who sort shapes are already reasoning about subsets.

</ForGrownUps>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Two ways of looking at shapes">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="The geometer: lengths and corners">

A geometer cares about **lengths and angles**. Two shapes are exactly the same (*congruent*) when one can be moved onto the other by sliding, turning or flipping, without stretching. They have the same shape (*similar*) when one is a zoomed-in copy of the other, like a small square and a big square. But a square and a long rectangle are never similar: zooming in cannot make all the sides of a long rectangle equal. This is the view of this lesson, and of <TopicLink id="math.geom.symmetry-transformations">Symmetry: slides, turns and flips</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="The topologist: the rubber-sheet view">

Imagine shapes drawn on a sheet of rubber that you may stretch and squash, but not tear or glue. A triangle can be stretched into a square, and a square into a circle — so a **topologist** says they are all the same! But a ring with a hole in the middle can never be stretched into a plate without tearing. Topology studies what stays the same under stretching: holes, pieces, and the surprising number $V - E + F = 2$ you met with the solids.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Shapes around the world, long ago">

- **Egypt.** The great pyramids at Giza, built about 4,500 years ago, have square bases and triangular sides. Their builders needed accurate square corners.
- **India.** The *Śulba Sūtras*, written more than 2,000 years ago, give instructions for building fire altars of exact shapes and sizes: squares, rectangles, and a square with the same area as a given rectangle.
- **Greece.** Around 300 BCE, Euclid's *Elements* gave careful definitions of a triangle, a square and a circle, and proved facts about them. It was copied and studied for more than 2,000 years, in Greek, Arabic, Latin and many other languages.
- **The Islamic world.** Artists in places such as Isfahan, Fez and Granada made beautiful tile patterns from stars, polygons and interlacing lines.
- **Japan.** In **origami**, the Japanese art of paper folding, squares, triangles and many other shapes — and whole animals — are folded from a single square of paper.
- **China.** The tangram puzzle became popular in China, and books of tangram puzzles were printed there in the early 1800s, before the puzzle spread around the world.
- **Southern Africa.** Ndebele house painting, like Nomvula's, uses bold triangles, rectangles and lines. The artist Esther Mahlangu has carried this tradition to galleries around the world.

- Edwin A. Abbott, *Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions* (1884) — a story told by a square, for older readers.
