<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Equations that find the best shape">

Dip a wire loop into soapy water and a film forms that, to the eye, has the least area the loop allows. Bend the loop and the film changes; pull two rings apart and the film between them narrows and suddenly bursts into two flat discs. The Belgian physicist Joseph Plateau studied such films experimentally in the nineteenth century, and the mathematical question — does every closed curve in space bound a surface of least area, and what does it look like? — became the **Plateau problem**.

The film is governed by a nonlinear partial differential equation: its mean curvature vanishes. This is the prototype of **geometric analysis**, where nonlinear PDE are used to construct geometric objects, and geometric ideas are used to understand the PDE. Its triumphs include:

- the solution of the Plateau problem (Douglas and Radó, around 1930);
- the **positive mass theorem** of general relativity, proved by Schoen and Yau (1979) with minimal surfaces;
- Yau's solution of the Calabi conjecture (1978), producing the Calabi–Yau metrics of string theory;
- **Ricci flow**, introduced by Richard Hamilton (1982), with which Grigori Perelman proved Thurston's geometrisation conjecture and the Poincaré conjecture (2002–2003).

A unifying theme: many geometric objects are critical points of a natural functional — area for surfaces, energy for maps, total scalar curvature for metrics — and their **gradient flows** are nonlinear heat equations: mean curvature flow, the harmonic map heat flow, Ricci flow. Heat flows smooth things out, but nonlinear ones can also form singularities in finite time. Understanding the singularities is often where the geometry lies.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Critical points and heat flows">

Think of the area of a surface with fixed boundary as a function on an infinite-dimensional space of surfaces. A **minimal surface** is a critical point: every small deformation fixing the boundary changes the area to second order only. Not every critical point is a minimum. The two rings make this visible: for rings close together there are *two* catenoids spanning them, both critical, and only the fatter one is a local minimum; the thinner one is a saddle point — push it and it collapses. Past a critical distance no catenoid exists, and the film jumps to the two discs.

Gradient flows follow the steepest descent of the functional. For curves in the plane, the gradient of length is curvature, and the **curve-shortening flow** moves each point with velocity equal to its curvature vector: bumps flatten, dents fill in, and a closed curve shrinks to a round point. For metrics, Hamilton's **Ricci flow** $\partial_t g = -2\,\mathrm{Ric}(g)$ is (in suitable coordinates) a nonlinear heat equation for the metric itself: positively curved regions shrink, negatively curved ones expand, and the curvature tends to even out. On a round sphere the flow simply shrinks the sphere to a point; on a dumbbell, the neck can pinch off first.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="mean curvature and minimal surface">

For an oriented surface $\Sigma \subset \R^3$ with unit normal $\nu$, the principal curvatures $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ are the eigenvalues of the shape operator $-d\nu$, and the **mean curvature** is $H = \kappa_1 + \kappa_2$ (some authors divide by 2). $\Sigma$ is **minimal** if $H \equiv 0$. Equivalently, for every compactly supported normal variation $\Sigma_t = \{x + t\varphi(x)\nu(x)\}$,
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} \mathrm{Area}(\Sigma_t) = -\int_\Sigma H \varphi \, dA = 0.
$$
A minimal surface is **stable** if moreover the second variation is non-negative for all such $\varphi$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="minimal surface equation">

A graph $z = u(x, y)$ over a domain $\Omega \subset \R^2$ has area $\int_\Omega \sqrt{1 + |\nabla u|^2}\,dx\,dy$; it is minimal if and only if
$$
\operatorname{div}\!\left( \frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1 + |\nabla u|^2}} \right) = 0 \iff (1 + u_y^2)\, u_{xx} - 2 u_x u_y\, u_{xy} + (1 + u_x^2)\, u_{yy} = 0.
$$

</Definition>

<Definition term="harmonic map">

For a smooth map $f : (M, g) \to (N, h)$ of Riemannian manifolds, $M$ compact, the **energy** is $E(f) = \tfrac12 \int_M |df|^2 \, dV_g$. Critical points of $E$ are **harmonic maps**; they are the solutions of $\tau(f) = \operatorname{tr}_g \nabla df = 0$, the **tension field** equation. Examples: geodesics (maps from an interval or circle), harmonic functions ($N = \R$), isometric minimal immersions, and holomorphic maps between Kähler manifolds.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Ricci flow, mean curvature flow, Yamabe problem">

A family of Riemannian metrics $g(t)$ on a manifold $M$ is a **Ricci flow** if $\partial_t g = -2\,\mathrm{Ric}(g)$. A family of hypersurfaces $F(\cdot, t) : \Sigma \to \R^{n+1}$ is a **mean curvature flow** if $\partial_t F = -H \nu$ (for curves in the plane: the **curve-shortening flow**, $\partial_t \gamma = \kappa N$). The **Yamabe problem** asks for a metric of constant scalar curvature in a given conformal class $[g] = \{e^{2w} g\}$ on a compact manifold of dimension $n \ge 3$.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Examples">

<Example title="Classical minimal surfaces">

- The **plane**, and pieces of it.
- The **catenoid**, obtained by rotating the catenary $y = a\cosh(x/a)$ about the $x$-axis (Euler, 1744): together with the plane it is the only minimal surface of revolution.
- The **helicoid** $(s\cos t, s\sin t, ct)$ (Meusnier, 1776), the only ruled minimal surface besides the plane (Catalan, 1842). It is locally isometric to the catenoid, and there is a one-parameter family of minimal surfaces bending one into the other.
- **Scherk's surface** $z = \log\cos y - \log\cos x$ over the square $|x|, |y| < \pi/2$ (1834), a doubly periodic surface when continued by reflection.
- **Enneper's surface**, a complete minimal surface with self-intersections, from the Weierstrass–Enneper representation: every simply connected minimal surface in $\R^3$ is $\mathrm{Re}\int (\tfrac12 f(1 - g^2), \tfrac{i}{2} f(1 + g^2), fg)\,dz$ for a holomorphic $f$ and meromorphic $g$ (with $fg^2$ holomorphic).

Non-example: a hemisphere of radius $r$ has $H = 2/r \ne 0$; it is a critical point of area only among surfaces enclosing a fixed volume (a soap *bubble*, of constant mean curvature).

</Example>

<Example title="Ricci flow on spheres, cylinders and hyperbolic manifolds">

On a round sphere $S^n$ of radius $r$, $\mathrm{Ric} = (n-1)\, g_{S^n}$ where $g_{S^n}$ is the unit round metric, independent of $r$. Seeking $g(t) = \rho(t)\, g_{S^n}$ gives $\rho' = -2(n-1)$, so $\rho(t) = r_0^2 - 2(n-1)t$: the sphere shrinks homothetically and disappears at $T = r_0^2/(2(n-1))$. On a hyperbolic manifold ($\mathrm{Ric} = -(n-1)g_{\mathbb{H}^n}$) the same computation gives $\rho(t) = r_0^2 + 2(n-1)t$: it expands forever. On a cylinder $S^{n-1} \times \R$ the sphere factor shrinks and the line factor stays: this is the model of a **neck pinch**, the singularity Perelman cut out by surgery.

</Example>

<Example title="Curve-shortening flow of a circle">

A circle of radius $r$ has curvature $1/r$, so $r' = -1/r$ and $r(t)^2 = r_0^2 - 2t$: it shrinks to a point at $T = r_0^2/2$. Its area $\pi r^2$ decreases at rate exactly $2\pi$. This is a general fact: for any embedded closed curve, $\frac{dA}{dt} = -\oint \kappa\, ds = -2\pi$ by the theorem of turning tangents (Hopf's Umlaufsatz).

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Central theorems">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (the minimal surface equation; Lagrange 1762)">

A $C^2$ function $u$ on a domain $\Omega \subset \R^2$ is a critical point of $A(u) = \int_\Omega \sqrt{1 + |\nabla u|^2}$ among functions with the same boundary values if and only if it satisfies the minimal surface equation.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $W = \sqrt{1 + |\nabla u|^2}$ and $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$. Differentiating under the integral and integrating by parts,
$$
\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon = 0} A(u + \varepsilon\varphi) = \int_\Omega \frac{\nabla u \cdot \nabla \varphi}{W} = -\int_\Omega \varphi \,\operatorname{div}\!\Big(\frac{\nabla u}{W}\Big).
$$
This vanishes for all $\varphi$ exactly when $\operatorname{div}(\nabla u / W) = 0$, by the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations. Expanding, $\partial_x(u_x / W) = (u_{xx} W^2 - u_x(u_x u_{xx} + u_y u_{xy}))/W^3$ and similarly for $y$; adding and multiplying by $W^3$ gives $(1 + u_y^2) u_{xx} - 2u_xu_yu_{xy} + (1 + u_x^2)u_{yy} = 0$. (Geometrically, $\operatorname{div}(\nabla u/W)$ is the mean curvature of the graph with respect to the upward normal.)

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Proposition 2 (minimal surfaces of revolution)">

The surface obtained by rotating the graph of a positive $C^2$ function $y(x)$ about the $x$-axis is minimal if and only if $y\, y'' = 1 + y'^2$, and the solutions are the catenaries $y = a\cosh((x - b)/a)$, $a > 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The area is $2\pi\int L\,dx$ with $L = yW$, $W = \sqrt{1 + y'^2}$. The Euler–Lagrange equation $\frac{d}{dx}(\partial L/\partial y') = \partial L/\partial y$ reads $\frac{d}{dx}(yy'/W) = W$, that is $(y'^2 + yy'')/W - yy'^2y''/W^3 = W$; multiplying by $W^3$ and using $W^2 - y'^2 = 1$ gives $y'^2W^2 + yy'' = W^4$, so $yy'' = W^2(W^2 - y'^2) = 1 + y'^2$. Since $L$ does not depend on $x$, the Beltrami quantity $L - y'\,\partial L/\partial y' = y/W$ is constant along solutions, say $a > 0$. The catenary $y = a\cosh(x/a)$ satisfies $y' = \sinh(x/a)$, $y'' = \cosh(x/a)/a$ and $yy'' = \cosh^2(x/a) = 1 + y'^2$, with $y/W = a$ (both identities are checked in Lean below). Finally, any initial data $y(x_0) > 0$, $y'(x_0)$ is matched by a catenary $a\cosh((x - b)/a)$ — take $a = y(x_0)/\sqrt{1 + y'(x_0)^2}$ and $b$ with $\sinh((x_0 - b)/a) = y'(x_0)$ — so by uniqueness for the ODE every solution is a catenary.

</Proof>

Scherk's surface: with $u = \log\cos y - \log\cos x$ we have $u_x = \tan x$, $u_y = -\tan y$, $u_{xx} = \sec^2 x$, $u_{yy} = -\sec^2 y$, $u_{xy} = 0$, and the left side of the minimal surface equation is $\sec^2 y \sec^2 x - \sec^2 x \sec^2 y = 0$ (also in Lean).

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (minimal graphs minimise area: a calibration argument)">

Let $u \in C^2(\overline\Omega)$ solve the minimal surface equation on a bounded domain $\Omega \subset \R^2$, with graph $G$. If $S$ is any compact oriented surface in the cylinder $\overline\Omega \times \R$ with $\partial S = \partial G$, then $\mathrm{Area}(S) \ge \mathrm{Area}(G)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

On $\Omega \times \R$ define the unit vector field $\nu(x, y, z) = (-u_x, -u_y, 1)/W$, the upward normal of $G$ translated vertically (it does not depend on $z$). Its divergence is $-\operatorname{div}(\nabla u / W) + \partial_z(1/W) = 0$ by the minimal surface equation. Let $\omega$ be the 2-form $\omega(X, Y) = \det(\nu, X, Y)$, the interior product of $\nu$ with the volume form; then $d\omega = (\operatorname{div}\nu)\, dV = 0$. For any oriented orthonormal pair $X, Y$, $\omega(X, Y) = \nu \cdot (X \times Y) \le 1$, with equality when $X \times Y = \nu$. Hence $\int_S \omega \le \mathrm{Area}(S)$, while $\int_G \omega = \mathrm{Area}(G)$ because $\nu$ is the normal of $G$. Since $\partial S = \partial G$, the cycle $S - G$ bounds a 3-chain $R$ in the cylinder $\overline\Omega \times \R$ (whose second homology vanishes, as $\Omega$ is a planar domain), and Stokes' theorem gives $\int_S \omega - \int_G \omega = \int_R d\omega = 0$. Therefore $\mathrm{Area}(S) \ge \int_S \omega = \int_G \omega = \mathrm{Area}(G)$.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

The whole proof is one inequality used twice. A calibration is a closed form that is at most the area on every tangent plane and exactly the area on $G$: closedness lets us trade $G$ for any competitor $S$ with the same boundary without changing $\int\omega$, and the pointwise inequality does the rest. Finding the calibration is the hard part — once you have one, area-minimisation comes for free.

</Narrator>

The form $\omega$ is a **calibration** (Harvey–Lawson, 1982): a closed form bounded by the area on every tangent plane. The catenoid between two rings is *not* a graph over a planar domain, and Theorem 3 does not apply to it; the computation below shows that for rings far enough apart the catenoid does not minimise area.

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (the Plateau problem; Douglas 1931, Radó 1930)">

Every Jordan curve $\Gamma \subset \R^3$ bounds a disc-type minimal surface: there is a map $f : \overline{D} \to \R^3$, continuous, harmonic and conformal on the open disc $D$, mapping $\partial D$ monotonically onto $\Gamma$, whose image has least area among all disc-type surfaces spanning $\Gamma$ (assuming some spanning disc has finite area).

</Theorem>

*Proof sketch.* Area is invariant under reparametrisation, so minimising it directly has no compactness. Douglas and Radó instead minimise the Dirichlet energy $E(f) = \tfrac12\int_D |\nabla f|^2 \ge \mathrm{Area}(f)$, with equality exactly for conformal maps, over maps whose boundary values sweep out $\Gamma$, normalised so that three fixed points of $\partial D$ go to three fixed points of $\Gamma$. The three-point condition restores compactness (the Courant–Lebesgue lemma), a minimiser is harmonic, and minimising also over reparametrisations of the disc makes it conformal. The solution may a priori have branch points; Osserman (1970), Alt (1973) and Gulliver (1973) showed an area-minimising solution in $\R^3$ has none in the interior. See Colding–Minicozzi, Chapter 4, or Dierkes–Hildebrandt–Sauvigny.

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (Bernstein's theorem and its limits)">

An entire solution $u : \R^2 \to \R$ of the minimal surface equation is affine (Bernstein, 1915–17). The same holds for entire minimal graphs over $\R^n$ with $n \le 7$ (Fleming, De Giorgi, Almgren, Simons, 1962–68) and fails for $n \ge 8$ (Bombieri, De Giorgi and Giusti, 1969, using the Simons cone).

</Theorem>

<Theorem name="Theorem 6 (harmonic maps; Eells–Sampson 1964)">

If $M$ and $N$ are compact Riemannian manifolds and $N$ has non-positive sectional curvature, then every continuous map $M \to N$ is homotopic to a harmonic map, obtained as the limit of the harmonic map heat flow $\partial_t f = \tau(f)$.

</Theorem>

Without the curvature hypothesis the flow can blow up: for maps from surfaces, Sacks and Uhlenbeck (1981) showed that energy concentrates at points and **bubbles off** harmonic 2-spheres, and used this to produce minimal 2-spheres in every compact manifold with $\pi_2 \ne 0$. And harmonic representatives need not exist: there is no harmonic map of degree $\pm1$ from the torus to the round 2-sphere (Eells–Wood, 1976).

<Theorem name="Theorem 7 (the Yamabe problem; Yamabe 1960, Trudinger 1968, Aubin 1976, Schoen 1984)">

Every compact Riemannian manifold of dimension $n \ge 3$ admits a conformal metric of constant scalar curvature.

</Theorem>

Writing the conformal metric as $\tilde g = \phi^{4/(n-2)} g$, the problem becomes the semilinear equation $-\frac{4(n-1)}{n-2}\Delta\phi + R_g\phi = \lambda\phi^{(n+2)/(n-2)}$, whose exponent is critical for the Sobolev embedding, so compactness fails. Yamabe's proof had a gap; Trudinger repaired it when the Yamabe invariant of the conformal class is non-positive, Aubin proved the case $n \ge 6$ (non-locally-conformally-flat), and Schoen completed the proof using the positive mass theorem. See Lee–Parker.

<Theorem name="Theorem 8 (Ricci flow; Hamilton 1982)">

1. On a closed manifold, the Ricci flow has a unique smooth solution for a short time from any initial metric (Hamilton 1982; a simpler proof by DeTurck, 1983).
2. If a closed 3-manifold admits a metric of positive Ricci curvature, the normalised Ricci flow from that metric converges to a metric of constant positive sectional curvature; hence the manifold is diffeomorphic to a spherical space form $S^3/\Gamma$.

</Theorem>

The engine is the **maximum principle**. Under Ricci flow the scalar curvature evolves by $\partial_t R = \Delta R + 2|\mathrm{Ric}|^2$.

<Theorem name="Proposition 9 (scalar maximum principle)">

Along a Ricci flow on a closed manifold, $R_{\min}(t) = \min_M R(\cdot, t)$ is non-decreasing. In particular positive scalar curvature is preserved.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Given $\varepsilon > 0$, let $\tilde R = R + \varepsilon(1 + t)$; then $\partial_t\tilde R = \Delta \tilde R + 2|\mathrm{Ric}|^2 + \varepsilon > \Delta\tilde R$. Suppose $\tilde R$ first reaches a value below $m = \min_M \tilde R(\cdot, 0)$ — more precisely, let $t_0$ be the first time with $\min \tilde R(\cdot, t_0) = m - \delta$ for some small $\delta > 0$, attained at $x_0$. At $(x_0, t_0)$ we have $\Delta\tilde R \ge 0$ (spatial minimum) and $\partial_t\tilde R \le 0$ (the minimum has just decreased to this value), contradicting $\partial_t\tilde R > \Delta\tilde R$. So $\tilde R \ge m - \delta$ for every $\delta$, hence $R(\cdot, t) + \varepsilon(1 + t) \ge R_{\min}(0) + \varepsilon$; let $\varepsilon \to 0$.

</Proof>

The discrete analogue — an explicit finite-difference heat scheme never exceeds its initial bounds when the time step is small enough — is formalised below. Hamilton extended the principle to tensors (curvature pinching), which is how Theorem 8(2) is proved.

<Theorem name="Theorem 10 (Perelman 2002–2003; Ricci flow with surgery)">

For every closed orientable 3-manifold there is a Ricci flow with surgery, defined for all time: singularities form only in regions modelled on shrinking necks $S^2 \times \R$, caps, or round components, and are removed by cutting along 2-spheres and gluing in caps. The long-time behaviour yields the geometric decomposition; hence Thurston's geometrisation conjecture and the Poincaré conjecture hold.

</Theorem>

The key new tools were Perelman's **entropy** functional, monotone along the flow, which gives a **no local collapsing** theorem, and his classification of ancient solutions (κ-solutions) that model singularities. Complete accounts: Morgan–Tian (2007), Kleiner–Lott (2008), Cao–Zhu (2006). For simply connected manifolds, finite extinction (Perelman; Colding–Minicozzi) gives a shorter route to the Poincaré conjecture. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.low-dimensional-topology">low-dimensional topology</TopicLink>.

<Theorem name="Theorem 11 (mean curvature and curve-shortening flow)">

1. (Huisken 1984.) A closed convex hypersurface in $\R^{n+1}$, $n \ge 2$, shrinks under mean curvature flow to a point in finite time, becoming asymptotically round.
2. (Gage–Hamilton 1986, Grayson 1987.) A smooth embedded closed curve in the plane stays embedded, becomes convex, and shrinks to a point, asymptotically round, at time $A(0)/2\pi$.

</Theorem>

In higher dimensions embeddedness does not prevent singularities before extinction: Grayson's dumbbell (1989) pinches at its neck. The experiment below follows Theorem 11(2) numerically.

<Section id="landscape" kind="human" title="The landscape: what is known and what is open">

**Established.** Beyond the theorems above: the positive mass theorem (Schoen–Yau 1979 by minimal surfaces; Witten 1981 by spinors); Yau's solution of the Calabi conjecture (1978) via the complex Monge–Ampère equation; the Willmore conjecture, proved by Marques and Neves (2014) with min-max theory for minimal surfaces; Yau's conjecture that every closed 3-manifold contains infinitely many minimal surfaces (Irie–Marques–Neves 2018 for generic metrics, Song 2018 for all metrics); Lawson's conjecture that the Clifford torus is the only embedded minimal torus in the round 3-sphere (Brendle 2013); the differentiable sphere theorem (Brendle–Schoen 2009) via Ricci flow; and the existence of Kähler–Einstein metrics on K-stable Fano manifolds (Chen–Donaldson–Sun 2015).

**Open problems.**

- Ricci flow in dimension 4 and higher: there is no analogue of Perelman's classification of singularities, and no general theory of surgery.
- Singularities of mean curvature flow: for surfaces in $\R^3$ the generic singularities are spheres and cylinders (Colding–Minicozzi, 2012), and flows through neck-pinches are now well understood (Bamler–Kleiner and others), but a complete theory in higher dimensions is open.
- **Chern's conjecture** (around 1970): a closed minimal hypersurface in the round sphere $S^{n+1}$ with constant scalar curvature has scalar curvature in a discrete set; open in general.
- **Yau's first eigenvalue conjecture** (1982): for a closed embedded minimal hypersurface in $S^{n+1}$, the first non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian equals $n$; known in special cases.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A minimal surface minimises area among surfaces with the same boundary."** Minimal means *stationary*: the first variation of area vanishes. The thin catenoid between two rings is minimal but unstable — a saddle point of area — and for rings with $0.5277 < h/R < 0.6627$ even the stable fat catenoid has more area than two flat discs. Minimal graphs over planar domains do minimise (Theorem 3), and area-minimising surfaces are minimal, but not conversely.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Ricci flow always smooths a metric out to a round one."** Only under strong hypotheses, such as positive Ricci curvature in dimension 3 (Hamilton). In general singularities form in finite time — even a round sphere shrinks to a point — and a dumbbell-shaped sphere can develop a neck pinch first. Perelman's achievement was to understand the singularities well enough to cut them out (surgery) and continue the flow.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Every homotopy class of maps contains a harmonic map."** True when the target has non-positive curvature (Eells–Sampson), false in general: no harmonic map of degree $\pm1$ from the torus to the round 2-sphere exists (Eells–Wood). The harmonic map heat flow may form bubbles instead of converging.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A PDE with a natural variational structure automatically has a solution."** Existence needs compactness, and geometric problems often lack it: area is invariant under reparametrisation (the Plateau problem), the Yamabe equation has critical Sobolev exponent, and gradient flows can blow up. Much of geometric analysis is the art of recovering compactness — by normalisation, by choosing better functionals, or by analysing exactly how it fails.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Curvature as the unknown">

For a geometer, these equations prescribe curvature: zero mean curvature (minimal surfaces), constant scalar curvature (Yamabe), Ricci curvature proportional to the metric (Einstein metrics, Calabi–Yau metrics with zero Ricci curvature), and flows that move curvature towards these ideals. The Gauss–Bonnet theorem and the comparison geometry of <TopicLink id="math.geom.riemannian-geometry">Riemannian geometry</TopicLink> constrain what solutions can look like; the uniformisation theorem for surfaces — every metric on a closed surface is conformal to one of constant curvature — is the two-dimensional model, and it can itself be proved by Ricci flow (Hamilton; Chow).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Existence from topology, topology from existence">

Topology produces solutions: Morse theory on the space of surfaces (Almgren–Pitts min-max theory) produces minimal surfaces in every closed 3-manifold, and $\pi_2 \ne 0$ produces minimal 2-spheres (Sacks–Uhlenbeck). Solutions in turn prove topological theorems: Schoen–Yau and Gromov–Lawson used minimal surfaces and spinors to show that the torus $T^n$ carries no metric of positive scalar curvature, and Ricci flow proved geometrisation. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.differential-topology">differential topology</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.geom.low-dimensional-topology">low-dimensional topology</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Elliptic and parabolic machinery">

For an analyst, the subject is quasilinear and fully nonlinear elliptic and parabolic PDE (see <TopicLink id="math.analysis.pde-theory">partial differential equations</TopicLink>): Schauder and De Giorgi–Nash–Moser regularity, maximum principles (Proposition 9 and its discrete shadow in Lean), monotonicity formulas (for minimal surfaces, the area ratio $|\Sigma \cap B_r|/\pi r^2$ is non-decreasing in $r$; Perelman's entropy and Huisken's monotonicity for mean curvature flow play the same role), and blow-up analysis at singularities. The minimal surface equation is the model case where geometry explains the analysis: Bernstein's theorem fails for $n \ge 8$ because of the area-minimising Simons cone.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Soap films, mass and the renormalisation group">

Soap films realise minimal surfaces to within the thickness of the film, and Plateau's empirical laws for how films meet (three sheets at 120° along curves, four curves at the tetrahedral angle at points) were proved by Jean Taylor (1976) for area-minimising soap-film-like surfaces. In general relativity, the positive mass theorem says an isolated gravitating system with non-negative local energy density has non-negative total mass; Schoen and Yau proved it by showing that negative mass would force a stable minimal surface that cannot exist. In quantum field theory, the renormalisation group flow of the nonlinear sigma model is, to first order, Ricci flow (Friedan, 1980) — a heuristic connection that predated Hamilton's work. See <TopicLink id="math.applied.relativity">relativity</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="Brownian motion and harmonic maps">

Harmonic functions are exactly the functions $u$ for which $u(B_t)$ is a martingale along Brownian motion $B_t$, which is the probabilistic form of the mean value property (formalised below from Mathlib). The same holds for maps: $f : M \to N$ is harmonic if and only if it sends Brownian motion on $M$ to a martingale on $N$ (with respect to the Levi-Civita connection). Probabilistic methods (coupling, stochastic flows) give proofs of gradient estimates and Liouville theorems, and Perelman's entropy has a heuristic interpretation as a Boltzmann–Nash entropy of the heat kernel. See <TopicLink id="math.prob.stochastic-processes">stochastic processes</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="Discrete differential geometry">

Computers find minimal surfaces and run geometric flows by discretising them: triangulated surfaces with the cotangent Laplacian (Pinkall–Polthier, 1993), Brakke's Surface Evolver for soap films, and finite-element schemes for mean curvature flow (Dziuk). Good discretisations keep structure — a discrete maximum principle needs a time step $\lambda = \Delta t/\Delta x^2 \le \tfrac12$ (formalised in Lean), and redistributing vertices keeps polygons well shaped, as in the curve-shortening experiment. Computer images also guide proofs: they led Hoffman and Meeks to prove (1985) that Costa's surface is embedded, the first new complete embedded minimal surface of finite topology since the plane, the catenoid and the helicoid.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **1740s–1880s: from variational calculus to soap films.** Leonhard Euler (Switzerland) found the catenoid as the minimal surface of revolution (1744); Joseph-Louis Lagrange (Italy–France) derived the minimal surface equation (1762); Jean Baptiste Meusnier (France) found the helicoid and interpreted minimality as vanishing mean curvature (1776); Heinrich Scherk (Germany) found his surfaces (1834); Joseph Plateau (Belgium) published his experiments on soap films (1873); Bernhard Riemann, Karl Weierstrass and Alfred Enneper (Germany) gave representation formulas by complex analysis.
- **1910s–1960s: existence and regularity.** Sergei Bernstein (Russia–Ukraine) proved his theorem (1915–17); Jesse Douglas (USA) and Tibor Radó (Hungary–USA) solved the Plateau problem (1930–31), and Douglas received one of the first two Fields Medals (1936); Ennio De Giorgi (Italy) and John Nash (USA) proved regularity of weak solutions of elliptic equations (1957–58); James Eells and Joseph Sampson (USA) introduced the harmonic map heat flow (1964); Hidehiko Yamabe (Japan) posed his problem (1960), solved by Neil Trudinger (Australia), Thierry Aubin (France) and Richard Schoen (USA).
- **1970s–1990s: geometric analysis comes of age.** Shing-Tung Yau (China–USA) and Schoen proved the positive mass theorem (1979), and Yau the Calabi conjecture (1978); Jonathan Sacks and Karen Uhlenbeck (USA) discovered bubbling (1981) — Uhlenbeck received the Abel Prize in 2019 for this circle of ideas; Richard Hamilton (USA) introduced Ricci flow (1982); Gerhard Huisken (Germany) proved convex hypersurfaces shrink to round points (1984); Michael Gage, Hamilton and Matthew Grayson settled the curve-shortening flow (1986–87).
- **2000s–present.** Grigori Perelman (Russia) proved geometrisation with Ricci flow (2002–03); Simon Brendle (Germany–USA) and Schoen proved the differentiable sphere theorem (2009); Fernando Codá Marques (Brazil) and André Neves (Portugal) proved the Willmore conjecture (2014); Tobias Colding and William Minicozzi (Denmark, USA) classified generic singularities of mean curvature flow (2012); Antoine Song (France) completed the proof of Yau's conjecture on minimal surfaces (2018).

</Section>
