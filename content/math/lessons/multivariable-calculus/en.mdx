<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Many inputs, many outputs">

A hiker on the slopes of Kilimanjaro stands at a point $(x, y)$ of a map; her altitude is a function $h(x, y)$. Which way is steepest? How steep is it? A cooperative in Bangladesh wants the largest yield from fields of fixed total perimeter. A hydrologist in the Netherlands wants the total water flowing out through the surface of a region of soil. A physicist wants to know whether a force field can be described by a potential energy.

Each question involves a function of **several** variables — and each needs a new idea. With one variable, the derivative is a number and the integral is over an interval. With several, the derivative becomes a **linear map** (a matrix), optimisation must handle **constraints**, integrals run over **regions, curves and surfaces**, and the fundamental theorem of calculus grows into a family of theorems — Green's, Stokes's and Gauss's — relating the inside of a region to its boundary.

This lesson assumes <TopicLink id="math.analysis.single-variable-calculus">calculus of one variable</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">linear algebra</TopicLink> (matrices, determinants, linear maps). We write $|x|$ for the Euclidean length of $x \in \R^n$ and $x \cdot y$ for the dot product.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Slices, tangent planes and boundaries">

A function $f : \R^2 \to \R$ has a graph, a surface $z = f(x, y)$ in space, and **level curves** $f(x, y) = c$, like the contour lines on a map. Freeze $y$ and you get a function of $x$ alone, whose slope is the **partial derivative** $\partial f/\partial x$; freezing $x$ gives $\partial f/\partial y$. But slices along two directions do not see the whole surface. The right notion is that, near a point, the surface looks like a **plane**: the derivative is the best *linear* approximation,

$$
f(a + h) \approx f(a) + Df(a)\,h ,
$$

where $Df(a)$ is a linear map — for $f : \R^n \to \R^m$, an $m \times n$ matrix of partial derivatives.

For integrals, the picture is "chop into small pieces and add". The new difficulty is that the natural pieces (polar rectangles, small patches on a curved surface) are distorted copies of squares, and the distortion factor — a **Jacobian determinant** — must be included. Finally, the one-variable theorem $\int_a^b F' = F(b) - F(a)$ says the integral of a derivative over an interval is determined by the **boundary** points $a, b$. In higher dimensions the same is true for regions, with "derivative" meaning curl or divergence and the boundary a curve or a surface.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

Let $U \subseteq \R^n$ be open (every point of $U$ has a ball around it inside $U$), $f : U \to \R^m$ and $a \in U$. Limits and continuity are defined as in one variable, with $|\cdot|$ the Euclidean length.

<Definition term="partial and directional derivatives">

The **partial derivative** of $f$ with respect to $x_j$ at $a$ is $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}(a) = \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + t e_j) - f(a)}{t}$, where $e_j$ is the $j$-th standard basis vector. More generally, for $v \in \R^n$ the **directional derivative** is $D_v f(a) = \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + t v) - f(a)}{t}$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="derivative (total or Fréchet derivative)">

$f$ is **differentiable at** $a$ if there is a linear map $L : \R^n \to \R^m$ such that

$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|f(a + h) - f(a) - L h|}{|h|} = 0 ,
$$

that is, $f(a + h) = f(a) + Lh + o(|h|)$. Then $L = Df(a)$ is **the derivative** of $f$ at $a$ (it is unique). Its matrix is the **Jacobian matrix** $J_f(a) = \big(\partial f_i/\partial x_j(a)\big)$, an $m \times n$ matrix; when $m = n$, $\det J_f(a)$ is the **Jacobian determinant**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="gradient">

For a real-valued differentiable $f$, the **gradient** $\nabla f(a) = \big(\partial f/\partial x_1(a), \dots, \partial f/\partial x_n(a)\big)$ is the vector with $Df(a)h = \nabla f(a) \cdot h$ for all $h$.

</Definition>

$f$ is **of class $C^1$** on $U$ if all partial derivatives exist and are continuous on $U$; $C^2$ if the second partials $\partial^2 f/\partial x_i \partial x_j$ are too. The **Hessian** of a $C^2$ function $f : U \to \R$ is the symmetric matrix $H_f(a) = \big(\partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j(a)\big)$, and $a$ is a **critical point** if $\nabla f(a) = 0$.

<Definition term="line integral">

Let $\gamma : [a, b] \to \R^n$ be a $C^1$ path and $F$ a continuous vector field on its image. The **line integral** (work, or circulation if $\gamma$ is closed) is

$$
\int_\gamma F \cdot d\mathbf r = \int_a^b F(\gamma(t)) \cdot \gamma'(t)\,dt .
$$

$F$ is **conservative** on $U$ if $F = \nabla \varphi$ for some $C^1$ function $\varphi : U \to \R$ (a **potential**; physicists often use $V = -\varphi$).

</Definition>

<Definition term="divergence and curl">

For a $C^1$ field $F = (F_1, \dots, F_n)$, the **divergence** is $\operatorname{div} F = \nabla \cdot F = \sum_i \partial F_i / \partial x_i$. In $\R^3$ the **curl** is

$$
\operatorname{curl} F = \nabla \times F = \Big(\frac{\partial F_3}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial z},\; \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_3}{\partial x},\; \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y}\Big),
$$

and in $\R^2$, for $F = (P, Q)$, the scalar curl is $\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="surface integral and flux">

A parametrised surface is a $C^1$ map $\Phi : D \to \R^3$, $D \subseteq \R^2$, with $\Phi_u \times \Phi_v \ne 0$ in the interior of $D$. The **flux** of a continuous field $F$ through it, in the direction of the normal $\Phi_u \times \Phi_v$, is

$$
\iint_S F \cdot \mathbf n\,dS = \iint_D F(\Phi(u, v)) \cdot \big(\Phi_u \times \Phi_v\big)\,du\,dv ,
$$

and the **area** of $S$ is $\iint_D |\Phi_u \times \Phi_v|\,du\,dv$.

</Definition>

Multiple integrals $\iint_R f\,dA$ over rectangles are defined by Riemann sums exactly as in one variable, and over a bounded region $D$ by integrating $f \cdot \mathbf 1_D$ over a rectangle containing $D$; a continuous function on a closed bounded region with a reasonable boundary (for example, a piecewise $C^1$ curve) is integrable.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples and a warning">

<Example title="A Jacobian matrix and a directional derivative">

Let $f(x, y) = (x^2 y,\; x + \sin y)$. Then

$$
J_f(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} 2xy & x^2 \\ 1 & \cos y \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J_f(1, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

For the real-valued $g(x, y) = x^2 y$ at $(1, 2)$: $\nabla g = (2xy, x^2) = (4, 1)$. In the unit direction $u = (3/5, 4/5)$, $D_u g = 4 \cdot \tfrac35 + 1 \cdot \tfrac45 = \tfrac{16}{5}$, while the steepest rate is $|\nabla g| = \sqrt{17} \approx 4.12$, in the direction $(4, 1)/\sqrt{17}$.

</Example>

<Example title="Partial derivatives are not enough">

Let $f(x, y) = \dfrac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ for $(x, y) \ne 0$ and $f(0, 0) = 0$. On both axes $f = 0$, so $\partial f/\partial x(0, 0) = \partial f/\partial y(0, 0) = 0$. But on the diagonal $f(t, t) = \tfrac12$ for every $t \ne 0$. So $f$ is not continuous at the origin, and therefore not differentiable there (a differentiable function is continuous, by the same argument as in one variable). The partial derivatives see only two directions; differentiability is a statement about all of them at once, uniformly.

Even existence of *every* directional derivative is not enough: $g(x, y) = \dfrac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}$ (with $g(0) = 0$) has a directional derivative $D_v g(0)$ in every direction $v$, but $g(t, t^2) = \tfrac12$, so $g$ is discontinuous at $0$.

</Example>

<Example title="Classifying critical points">

$f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$ has $\nabla f = (3x^2 - 3,\, 2y)$, so the critical points are $(\pm 1, 0)$. The Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix} 6x & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{pmatrix}$: at $(1, 0)$ it is positive definite (a local minimum, $f = -2$); at $(-1, 0)$ it has eigenvalues $-6$ and $2$ (a saddle point). There is no global minimum: $f(x, 0) \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="derivative" kind="proof" title="The derivative and the chain rule">

<Theorem name="Proposition (what differentiability gives)">

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$, every directional derivative exists and $D_v f(a) = Df(a)\,v$, and the matrix of $Df(a)$ is the Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $f(a + h) = f(a) + Lh + r(h)$ with $|r(h)|/|h| \to 0$. Then $|f(a + h) - f(a)| \le \|L\|\,|h| + |r(h)| \to 0$, where $\|L\|$ is the operator norm. For $v \ne 0$ and $t \to 0$,

$$
\frac{f(a + tv) - f(a)}{t} = Lv + \frac{r(tv)}{t}, \qquad \left|\frac{r(tv)}{t}\right| = \frac{|r(tv)|}{|tv|}\,|v| \to 0 .
$$

Taking $v = e_j$ shows that the $j$-th column of the matrix of $L$ is $\partial f/\partial x_j(a)$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem (continuous partials imply differentiability)">

If all partial derivatives of $f : U \to \R$ exist near $a$ and are continuous at $a$, then $f$ is differentiable at $a$. (Apply this to each component when $m > 1$.)

</Theorem>

<Proof>

We give the proof for $n = 2$; the general case adds one coordinate at a time in the same way. Write $a = (a_1, a_2)$ and $h = (h_1, h_2)$ small. Change one coordinate at a time:

$$
f(a + h) - f(a) = \big[f(a_1 + h_1, a_2 + h_2) - f(a_1, a_2 + h_2)\big] + \big[f(a_1, a_2 + h_2) - f(a_1, a_2)\big].
$$

By the one-variable mean value theorem, the first bracket is $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(\xi)\,h_1$ for a point $\xi$ on the segment from $(a_1, a_2 + h_2)$ to $(a_1 + h_1, a_2 + h_2)$, and the second is $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(\eta)\,h_2$ for a point $\eta$ between $(a_1, a_2)$ and $(a_1, a_2 + h_2)$. Hence

$$
\frac{\big|f(a + h) - f(a) - \nabla f(a) \cdot h\big|}{|h|} \le \Big|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(\xi) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a)\Big| + \Big|\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(\eta) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a)\Big|,
$$

using $|h_1|, |h_2| \le |h|$. As $h \to 0$, both $\xi$ and $\eta$ tend to $a$, and the right side tends to $0$ by continuity of the partials at $a$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem (chain rule)">

If $f : U \to \R^m$ is differentiable at $a$ and $g : V \to \R^p$ is differentiable at $b = f(a)$ (with $f(U) \subseteq V$, $V$ open), then $g \circ f$ is differentiable at $a$ and

$$
D(g \circ f)(a) = Dg(f(a)) \circ Df(a), \qquad J_{g \circ f}(a) = J_g(f(a))\,J_f(a).
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $A = Df(a)$ and $B = Dg(b)$, and write $f(a + h) = b + Ah + r(h)$ and $g(b + k) = g(b) + Bk + s(k)$ with $r(h) = o(|h|)$, $s(k) = o(|k|)$ and $s(0) = 0$. Put $k(h) = f(a + h) - b = Ah + r(h)$. Then

$$
g(f(a + h)) = g(b) + BAh + \underbrace{B\,r(h) + s(k(h))}_{\text{error}} .
$$

First, $|B\,r(h)| \le \|B\|\,|r(h)| = o(|h|)$. Second, for small $h$ we have $|r(h)| \le |h|$, so $|k(h)| \le (\|A\| + 1)|h|$. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $\delta$ with $|s(k)| \le \varepsilon |k|$ for $|k| < \delta$ (this holds for $k = 0$ too). For $h$ small enough that $|k(h)| < \delta$, $|s(k(h))| \le \varepsilon(\|A\| + 1)|h|$. So the error is $o(|h|)$, and $D(g \circ f)(a) = BA$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Proposition (the gradient)">

Let $f : U \to \R$ be differentiable at $a$ with $\nabla f(a) \ne 0$. Among unit vectors $u$, the directional derivative $D_u f(a) = \nabla f(a) \cdot u$ is largest, with value $|\nabla f(a)|$, exactly for $u = \nabla f(a)/|\nabla f(a)|$. If $\gamma$ is a differentiable curve in the level set $\{f = f(a)\}$ with $\gamma(0) = a$, then $\nabla f(a) \cdot \gamma'(0) = 0$: the gradient is perpendicular to level sets.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By Cauchy–Schwarz, $\nabla f(a) \cdot u \le |\nabla f(a)|\,|u| = |\nabla f(a)|$, with equality if and only if $u$ is a positive multiple of $\nabla f(a)$. For the second claim, $f(\gamma(t)) = f(a)$ for all $t$; differentiating with the chain rule gives $\nabla f(a) \cdot \gamma'(0) = 0$.

</Proof>

For $C^2$ functions, the order of differentiation does not matter: $\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}$ (the theorem of **Schwarz**, or **Clairaut**; the hypothesis is needed — the function $xy(x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)$ has unequal mixed partials at the origin). Hence the Hessian is symmetric, and second-order Taylor expansion gives the **second derivative test**: at a critical point of a $C^2$ function, a positive definite Hessian means a strict local minimum, a negative definite one a strict local maximum, and one with eigenvalues of both signs a saddle point.

</Section>

<Section id="lagrange" kind="proof" title="Constrained optimisation: Lagrange multipliers">

To find the extreme values of $f$ on a constraint set $\{g = c\}$, we cannot simply set $\nabla f = 0$: the extremum is taken *along* the constraint. The idea, due to Lagrange (1788), is that at a constrained extremum the level curve of $f$ must be **tangent** to the constraint curve.

<FunctionPlot fns={["c/x", "sqrt(2 - x^2)", "-sqrt(2 - x^2)"]} labels={["level curve xy = c", "constraint x² + y² = 2", "constraint (lower half)"]} params={[{ name: "c", min: -1.5, max: 1.5, step: 0.05, value: 0.5 }]} points={[[1, 1, "(1, 1)"], [-1, -1, "(−1, −1)"]]} x={[-2.5, 2.5]} y={[-2.5, 2.5]} aspect="equal" title="Maximise xy on the circle x² + y² = 2" subtitle="Increase c until the hyperbola last touches the circle. At that moment the two curves are tangent." />

<Theorem name="Theorem (Lagrange multipliers)">

Let $f, g : U \to \R$ be $C^1$, let $a$ satisfy $g(a) = c$ and $\nabla g(a) \ne 0$, and suppose $f$, restricted to $\{x \in U : g(x) = c\}$, has a local maximum or minimum at $a$. Then there is a number $\lambda$ (a **Lagrange multiplier**) with

$$
\nabla f(a) = \lambda\,\nabla g(a).
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

We use the **implicit function theorem** (proved in real analysis, from the inverse function theorem): since $\nabla g(a) \ne 0$, near $a$ the set $\{g = c\}$ is a $C^1$ hypersurface, and for every vector $v$ with $\nabla g(a) \cdot v = 0$ there is a $C^1$ curve $\gamma$ in $\{g = c\}$ with $\gamma(0) = a$ and $\gamma'(0) = v$.

Take such $v$ and $\gamma$. The function $t \mapsto f(\gamma(t))$ has a local extremum at $t = 0$, so its derivative vanishes there: by the chain rule, $\nabla f(a) \cdot v = 0$. Thus $\nabla f(a)$ is orthogonal to every vector orthogonal to $\nabla g(a)$; that is, $\nabla f(a) \in \big(\nabla g(a)^\perp\big)^\perp = \operatorname{span}\{\nabla g(a)\}$.

</Proof>

With several constraints $g_1 = c_1, \dots, g_k = c_k$ whose gradients at $a$ are linearly independent, the same argument gives $\nabla f(a) = \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i(a)$.

<Example title="xy on a circle, and the best box">

**On the circle.** Maximise $f = xy$ on $x^2 + y^2 = 2$. The equations $(y, x) = \lambda(2x, 2y)$ give $y = 2\lambda x$ and $x = 2\lambda y = 4\lambda^2 x$. If $x = 0$ then $y = 0$, which is not on the circle; so $\lambda = \pm\tfrac12$ and $y = \pm x$, giving $(1, 1), (-1, -1)$ (value $1$) and $(1, -1), (-1, 1)$ (value $-1$). The circle is closed and bounded, so by the extreme value theorem $f$ has a maximum and a minimum there, and they must be among these candidates: **max 1, min −1**. (Independently: $2xy \le x^2 + y^2 = 2$ since $(x - y)^2 \ge 0$.)

**The best box.** Maximise the volume $xyz$ of a box with surface area $2(xy + yz + zx) = 6$ and $x, y, z > 0$. The multiplier equations $yz = 2\lambda(y + z)$, $xz = 2\lambda(x + z)$, $xy = 2\lambda(x + y)$ force $x = y = z = 1$ (multiply the first by $x$, the second by $y$, and compare). Here the constraint set is not compact, so existence needs a separate argument: by the AM–GM inequality, $(xy \cdot yz \cdot zx)^{1/3} \le \frac{xy + yz + zx}{3} = 1$, so $xyz \le 1$, with equality for the cube. The multiplier method found the candidate; the inequality proved it optimal.

</Example>

The multiplier itself has a meaning: if $M(c)$ is the optimal value as a function of the constraint level $c$, then (under mild hypotheses) $M'(c) = \lambda$ — the "shadow price" of the constraint in economics.

</Section>

<Section id="integrals" kind="proof" title="Multiple integrals and change of variables">

<Theorem name="Theorem (Fubini, for continuous functions)">

If $f$ is continuous on the rectangle $[a, b] \times [c, d]$, then

$$
\iint_{[a,b]\times[c,d]} f\,dA = \int_a^b \left(\int_c^d f(x, y)\,dy\right) dx = \int_c^d \left(\int_a^b f(x, y)\,dx\right) dy.
$$

</Theorem>

The proof (via uniform continuity and Riemann sums) belongs to <TopicLink id="math.analysis.real-analysis">real analysis</TopicLink>; the general version for Lebesgue integrals is a cornerstone of <TopicLink id="math.analysis.measure-theory">measure theory</TopicLink>. Without integrability the conclusion can fail: for $f(x, y) = \frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$ on $(0, 1]^2$ the two iterated integrals are $\frac\pi4$ and $-\frac\pi4$.

<Theorem name="Theorem (change of variables)">

Let $T : U \to \R^n$ be a $C^1$ map that is one-to-one on an open set $U$ with $\det J_T \ne 0$ there, and let $D \subseteq U$ be a closed bounded region with a reasonable boundary. For $f$ continuous on $T(D)$,

$$
\int_{T(D)} f(x)\,dx = \int_D f(T(u))\,\big|\det J_T(u)\big|\,du .
$$

</Theorem>

**Why the determinant?** A linear map $A$ multiplies all volumes by $|\det A|$ (from linear algebra: true for elementary matrices, hence for products). Near a point $u$, $T$ is approximately the affine map $u + h \mapsto T(u) + J_T(u)h$, so a tiny box of volume $\Delta V$ at $u$ is carried to a region of volume about $|\det J_T(u)|\,\Delta V$. Summing over a fine partition gives the formula; making "about" precise is the real work of the proof, done in analysis texts. Points where $\det J_T = 0$ or $T$ fails to be injective may be ignored if they form a set of volume zero — this is what allows polar coordinates, whose Jacobian vanishes at $r = 0$.

**Polar coordinates.** $T(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ has

$$
J_T = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det J_T = r(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = r ,
$$

so $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$. Below, the widget shows $J_T$ at $(r, \theta) = (2, \pi/6)$ acting on the plane: the unit square becomes a parallelogram of area $2 = r$. A tiny polar rectangle of sides $\Delta r$ and $\Delta\theta$ is nearly a rectangle with sides $\Delta r$ and $r\,\Delta\theta$.

<Theorem name="Corollary (the Gaussian integral)">

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi}.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $I(R) = \int_{-R}^{R} e^{-x^2}dx$, which increases to a finite limit $I$ (the integrand is at most $e^{-|x|}$ for $|x| \ge 1$). By Fubini, $I(R)^2 = \iint_{Q_R} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\,dA$ over the square $Q_R = [-R, R]^2$. The integrand is positive and the square lies between the discs of radius $R$ and $R\sqrt2$, so with $D_\rho$ the disc of radius $\rho$,

$$
\iint_{D_R} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}dA \;\le\; I(R)^2 \;\le\; \iint_{D_{R\sqrt2}} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}dA .
$$

In polar coordinates, $\iint_{D_\rho} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\rho e^{-r^2} r\,dr\,d\theta = \pi\big(1 - e^{-\rho^2}\big)$ — the factor $r$ is exactly what makes $e^{-r^2}$ integrable in closed form. Both bounds tend to $\pi$, so $I^2 = \pi$.

</Proof>

This is why the normal density $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}$ has total probability $1$.

</Section>

<Section id="vector-calculus" kind="proof" title="Line integrals and the theorems of Green, Stokes and Gauss">

<Theorem name="Theorem (gradient theorem)">

If $\varphi$ is $C^1$ on an open set $U$ and $\gamma : [a, b] \to U$ is a $C^1$ path, then

$$
\int_\gamma \nabla\varphi \cdot d\mathbf r = \varphi(\gamma(b)) - \varphi(\gamma(a)).
$$

In particular the line integral of a conservative field depends only on the endpoints, and is $0$ around every closed path.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By the chain rule, $(\varphi \circ \gamma)'(t) = \nabla\varphi(\gamma(t)) \cdot \gamma'(t)$, which is continuous. The one-variable fundamental theorem of calculus gives $\int_a^b (\varphi \circ \gamma)'(t)\,dt = \varphi(\gamma(b)) - \varphi(\gamma(a))$.

</Proof>

**A test for conservative fields.** If $F = (P, Q) = \nabla\varphi$ with $\varphi$ of class $C^2$, then $\partial P/\partial y = \partial^2\varphi/\partial y\,\partial x = \partial^2\varphi/\partial x\,\partial y = \partial Q/\partial x$ by Schwarz's theorem: **a conservative $C^1$ field has zero curl.** The converse holds on a disc, or any star-shaped open set (Poincaré's lemma), but **not** in general:

<Example title="The vortex: curl zero, but not conservative">

On $U = \R^2 \setminus \{0\}$ let $F = \left(\dfrac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}, \dfrac{x}{x^2 + y^2}\right)$. A quotient-rule computation gives $\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \dfrac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}$, so $\operatorname{curl} F = 0$ on $U$. But on the unit circle $\gamma(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$ we have $F(\gamma(t)) = (-\sin t, \cos t) = \gamma'(t)$, so

$$
\oint_\gamma F \cdot d\mathbf r = \int_0^{2\pi} (\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t)\,dt = 2\pi \ne 0 .
$$

By the gradient theorem, $F$ is not $\nabla\varphi$ for any $C^1$ function $\varphi$ on $U$. Locally it is: $F = \nabla\theta$ for the polar angle $\theta$, but $\theta$ cannot be defined continuously all the way round the origin. The circulation $2\pi$ detects the hole.

</Example>

<Theorem name="Theorem (Green's theorem)">

Let $D \subset \R^2$ be a closed bounded region whose boundary $\partial D$ consists of finitely many piecewise $C^1$ simple closed curves, oriented so that $D$ lies to the left. If $P, Q$ are $C^1$ on an open set containing $D$, then

$$
\oint_{\partial D} P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA .
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

We prove it for a rectangle $D = [a, b] \times [c, d]$, which already contains the idea; general regions are handled by cutting them into pieces of this kind (or of the form $\{a \le x \le b,\ \varphi_1(x) \le y \le \varphi_2(x)\}$, where the same computation works), since the line integrals over interior cuts cancel in pairs.

Treat the two terms separately. By Fubini and the one-variable fundamental theorem,

$$
-\iint_D \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\,dA = -\int_a^b \big(P(x, d) - P(x, c)\big)\,dx = \int_a^b P(x, c)\,dx - \int_a^b P(x, d)\,dx .
$$

On the other hand, $\oint_{\partial D} P\,dx$ runs along the bottom edge from $(a, c)$ to $(b, c)$, contributing $\int_a^b P(x, c)\,dx$; along the top edge from right to left, contributing $-\int_a^b P(x, d)\,dx$; and along the vertical edges, where $dx = 0$, contributing nothing. The two expressions agree. In the same way $\iint_D \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\,dA = \int_c^d \big(Q(b, y) - Q(a, y)\big)dy = \oint_{\partial D} Q\,dy$. Add.

</Proof>

The vortex shows why the hypothesis "$C^1$ on all of $D$" matters: for the unit disc the left side is $2\pi$, the right side would be $0$, and indeed $F$ is undefined at the origin. Green's theorem with $P = -y/2$, $Q = x/2$ gives a formula for area, $\operatorname{Area}(D) = \tfrac12 \oint_{\partial D} x\,dy - y\,dx$ — the principle of the planimeter, a mechanical instrument that measures areas by tracing boundaries. For the ellipse $(a\cos t, b\sin t)$ it gives $\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi} ab\,dt = \pi ab$.

Applied to the field $(-Q, P)$ rotated by a right angle, Green's theorem becomes its **flux form**: the outward flux $\oint_{\partial D} F \cdot \mathbf n\,ds$ of $F = (P, Q)$ equals $\iint_D \operatorname{div} F\,dA$. In three dimensions both forms have analogues.

<Theorem name="Theorem (divergence theorem of Gauss)">

Let $V \subset \R^3$ be a closed bounded region whose boundary $\partial V$ is a piecewise smooth closed surface with outward unit normal $\mathbf n$, and let $F$ be $C^1$ on an open set containing $V$. Then

$$
\iint_{\partial V} F \cdot \mathbf n\,dS = \iiint_V \operatorname{div} F\,dV .
$$

</Theorem>

<Theorem name="Theorem (Stokes's theorem)">

Let $S$ be a piecewise smooth oriented surface with boundary curve $\partial S$, oriented compatibly (walking along $\partial S$ with your head in the direction of $\mathbf n$, the surface is on your left), and let $F$ be $C^1$ near $S$. Then

$$
\oint_{\partial S} F \cdot d\mathbf r = \iint_S (\operatorname{curl} F) \cdot \mathbf n\,dS .
$$

</Theorem>

For a box $V = [a_1, b_1] \times [a_2, b_2] \times [a_3, b_3]$ the divergence theorem is proved exactly like Green's theorem: $\iiint_V \partial F_3/\partial z\,dV$ equals, by Fubini and the fundamental theorem, the integral of $F_3$ over the top face minus that over the bottom face — the flux of $(0, 0, F_3)$ — and similarly for the other components. The general versions (and Stokes's theorem, which for a graph $z = h(x, y)$ reduces to Green's theorem by the chain rule) are proved in texts such as Hubbard and Hubbard, or Marsden and Tromba, and most cleanly as the single theorem $\int_{\partial M} \omega = \int_M d\omega$ of <TopicLink id="math.geom.differential-forms">differential forms</TopicLink> (see the geometry viewpoint).

<Example title="Flux out of a sphere, two ways">

Let $F(x, y, z) = (x, y, z)$ and let $V$ be the ball of radius $R$. Directly: on the sphere $\mathbf n = (x, y, z)/R$, so $F \cdot \mathbf n = R$, and the flux is $R \cdot 4\pi R^2 = 4\pi R^3$. By the divergence theorem: $\operatorname{div} F = 3$, so the flux is $3 \cdot \frac43\pi R^3 = 4\pi R^3$.

</Example>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"If the partial derivatives exist, the function is differentiable."** Partial derivatives only look along the coordinate axes. $f(x, y) = xy/(x^2 + y^2)$ (with $f(0) = 0$) has both partials at the origin but is not even continuous there. What is true: if the partials exist near $a$ and are **continuous** at $a$, then $f$ is differentiable at $a$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Forgetting the Jacobian factor.** "$dx\,dy = dr\,d\theta$" is wrong; $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$. Without the $r$, the area of the disc of radius $R$ would come out as $2\pi R$, and the experiment above shows the size of the error for a Gaussian. The factor is $|\det J_T|$ — the absolute value, because volumes are positive even when $T$ reverses orientation.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Zero curl means conservative."** Only on simply connected domains (for instance discs, or any star-shaped set). The vortex field has zero curl on the punctured plane but circulation $2\pi$ around the origin.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ always finds the maximum."** It finds candidates, and only at points where $\nabla g \ne 0$. One must also check points where $\nabla g = 0$ on the constraint, and make sure a maximum exists at all — by compactness, or by a separate inequality, as for the box.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The gradient is just a list of partial derivatives, independent of geometry."** The *derivative* $Df(a)$ is defined without any inner product; the *gradient* is the vector representing it via the dot product. With a different inner product (in curvilinear coordinates, or on a curved surface), the gradient vector changes: in polar coordinates $\nabla f = f_r\,\mathbf e_r + \frac1r f_\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta$, not $(f_r, f_\theta)$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="One theorem: ∫ over the boundary of ω equals ∫ of dω">

Green's, Stokes's and Gauss's theorems, and the fundamental theorem of calculus itself, are one theorem in the language of **differential forms**. A $k$-form is something that can be integrated over oriented $k$-dimensional surfaces: $P\,dx + Q\,dy$ is a 1-form, $f\,dx \wedge dy$ a 2-form. The exterior derivative $d$ turns $k$-forms into $(k+1)$-forms; in $\R^3$, on 0-, 1- and 2-forms, it is gradient, curl and divergence. The **generalised Stokes theorem** says $\int_{\partial M} \omega = \int_M d\omega$ for a compact oriented manifold $M$ with boundary. The wedge product is antisymmetric, $dx \wedge dy = -dy \wedge dx$, which is the geometric reason the Jacobian *determinant* appears in change of variables: $dx \wedge dy = \det J_T\, dr \wedge d\theta$. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.differential-forms">differential forms</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.geom.smooth-manifolds">smooth manifolds</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Fields that detect holes">

The vortex shows that the difference between "curl-free" and "conservative" is topological. Curl-free fields on an open set $U \subseteq \R^2$, modulo gradients, form a vector space: the first **de Rham cohomology** $H^1_{dR}(U)$. Poincaré's lemma says it is zero for a disc; for the punctured plane it is one-dimensional, spanned by the vortex, and the linear map "circulation around the origin, divided by $2\pi$" identifies it with $\R$. For a curl-free field the circulation around a loop depends only on the loop's homotopy class, and for the vortex it is $2\pi$ times the **winding number** of the loop around the origin. De Rham's theorem (1931) says these analytic invariants agree with the topological ones computed by counting holes. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.fundamental-group">the fundamental group</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.geom.algebraic-topology">algebraic topology</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Linearisation, and d² = 0">

Multivariable calculus is linear algebra applied locally: the derivative is a linear map, the chain rule is matrix multiplication, the second derivative test is the classification of a symmetric matrix by the signs of its eigenvalues, and the change of variables formula is the multiplicativity of the determinant. The identities $\operatorname{curl}(\nabla \varphi) = 0$ and $\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl} F) = 0$ are both consequences of the symmetry of second derivatives, and in the language of forms they are the single identity $d \circ d = 0$: the sequence "functions $\xrightarrow{\nabla}$ fields $\xrightarrow{\text{curl}}$ fields $\xrightarrow{\text{div}}$ functions" is a **cochain complex**, and its failure to be exact measures topology, as in the topological viewpoint.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Gauss's law, potentials and constraint forces">

The theorems of this lesson were developed largely for physics. Gauss's law for the electric field, $\iint_{\partial V} \mathbf E \cdot \mathbf n\,dS = Q_{\text{inside}}/\varepsilon_0$, becomes the local equation $\operatorname{div}\mathbf E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ by the divergence theorem applied to small balls; Faraday's law becomes $\operatorname{curl}\mathbf E = -\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ by Stokes's theorem — Maxwell's equations in their two equivalent forms. A force field is conservative exactly when work is path-independent, and then $\mathbf F = -\nabla V$ for a potential energy $V$ (see <TopicLink id="math.applied.classical-mechanics">classical mechanics</TopicLink>). Lagrange multipliers have a physical reading too: a bead on a wire feels a constraint force $\lambda\nabla g$ normal to the wire, and the multiplier measures its strength.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="Densities transform with the Jacobian">

If a random point $X$ in $\R^n$ has density $p_X$ and $Y = T(X)$ for a $C^1$ bijection $T$ with non-vanishing Jacobian, then $Y$ has density $p_Y(y) = p_X(T^{-1}(y))\,|\det J_{T^{-1}}(y)|$ — the change of variables formula read backwards. The Gaussian integral makes the normal distribution a probability distribution, and polar coordinates explain the **Box–Muller method** for simulating it: if $U_1, U_2$ are independent and uniform on $(0, 1)$, then $R = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}$, $\Theta = 2\pi U_2$ give independent standard normal variables $R\cos\Theta$ and $R\sin\Theta$ — the factor $r$ in $r\,dr\,d\theta$ is exactly absorbed by the density of $R$. See <TopicLink id="math.prob.probability-theory">probability theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="categories" title="The chain rule is functoriality">

Consider pointed open sets $(U, a)$ and differentiable maps $f : (U, a) \to (V, b)$ with $f(a) = b$. Sending $(U, a)$ to the vector space $\R^n$ of directions at $a$, and $f$ to the linear map $Df(a)$, respects identities ($D(\mathrm{id})(a) = \mathrm{id}$) and composition ($D(g \circ f)(a) = Dg(b) \circ Df(a)$). So the chain rule says precisely that differentiation at a point is a **functor** from this category to the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces. On manifolds this becomes the tangent functor $T$, sending $M$ to its tangent bundle. See <TopicLink id="math.cat.functors">functors</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Partial derivatives and multiple integrals (18th century).** Leonhard Euler and Alexis Clairaut used partial derivatives for problems in mechanics and the shape of the Earth (Clairaut's *Théorie de la figure de la Terre*, 1743, and Euler's work on the symmetry of mixed partials in the 1730s–40s). Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange evaluated double and triple integrals by changing variables, and Lagrange introduced multipliers in his *Mécanique analytique* (1788) for mechanical constraints.
- **Potential theory and the divergence theorem.** Lagrange (1762) and Carl Friedrich Gauss (1813, and his 1839–40 papers on potential theory) proved special cases of the divergence theorem; Mikhail Ostrogradsky in St Petersburg gave a general proof in 1826 (published 1831), which is why the theorem is also called the Ostrogradsky–Gauss theorem.
- **Green and Stokes.** George Green, a self-taught miller's son from Nottingham, published *An Essay on the Application of Mathematical Analysis to the Theories of Electricity and Magnetism* privately in 1828; it was rediscovered by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) in 1845. The theorem now called Stokes's appeared in a letter from Thomson to George Gabriel Stokes in 1850, and Stokes set it as an examination question in Cambridge in 1854.
- **Vector notation and forms.** Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside created vector analysis in the 1880s, in part to write James Clerk Maxwell's equations compactly. Élie Cartan developed differential forms (around 1899–1922), and Vito Volterra, Henri Poincaré and Cartan made the generalised Stokes theorem a single statement; Georges de Rham connected it with topology in 1931.
- **Rigour.** Cauchy and later Weierstrass, Jordan and Peano clarified the notion of differentiability in several variables; the definition via the best linear approximation is due to Otto Stolz (1893), William Henry Young (1910) and Maurice Fréchet (1911), the last in infinite-dimensional spaces.

</Section>
