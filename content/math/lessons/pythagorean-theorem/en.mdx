<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Three, four, five">

A builder in Accra wants the corner of a new wall to be exactly square. She has no protractor, only a tape measure. So she marks a point 3 metres along one wall and a point 4 metres along the other, and measures the distance between the marks. If it is exactly 5 metres, the corner is a right angle. If it is more, the corner is too wide; if less, too narrow.

Why should the numbers 3, 4 and 5 know anything about right angles? And why does the same trick work with 5, 12 and 13, or with 6, 8 and 10 — but not with 4, 5 and 6? The answer is one of the oldest and most famous theorems in mathematics. It was known, in some form, to scribes in ancient Mesopotamia, to the authors of the Indian *Śulbasūtras*, and to Chinese astronomers, long before it became attached to the name of Pythagoras. It is also the reason a computer can measure the distance between two points on a map.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Squares on the sides">

Draw a right triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 on squared paper, and build a square outward on each side. The square on the side 3 contains $9$ unit squares, the square on the side 4 contains $16$, and the square on the side 5 contains $25$. And $9 + 16 = 25$.

So the theorem is really about **areas**: the two smaller squares together have exactly the same area as the big one. You can try other right triangles in the explorer further down — for legs 1 and 1, the tilted square has area $2$ — and the areas always balance.

Now try a triangle *without* a right angle, such as an equilateral triangle with all sides 1. The squares on its sides have areas $1$, $1$ and $1$, and $1 + 1 \neq 1$. The right angle is essential.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Squared paper can check a few triangles. But there are infinitely many right triangles, most of them with sides that are not whole numbers. How can we be sure it works for *all* of them? We need a proof.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="right triangle">

A **right triangle** (or right-angled triangle) is a triangle with one angle of $90^\circ$. The two sides that form the right angle are the **legs**; the side opposite the right angle is the **hypotenuse**. The hypotenuse is always the longest side.

</Definition>

<Definition term="square root">

For a number $x \ge 0$, the **square root** $\sqrt{x}$ is the number $r \ge 0$ with $r^2 = x$. For example $\sqrt{25} = 5$ and $\sqrt{2.25} = 1.5$. Some square roots, such as $\sqrt{2} = 1.41421\dots$, are not whole numbers or even fractions; we can still approximate them as closely as we like.

</Definition>

We will use two facts from earlier lessons: the angles of a triangle add up to $180^\circ$, and the area of a right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ is $\tfrac12 ab$ (half of an $a \times b$ rectangle).

</Section>

<Section id="proof" kind="proof" title="The theorem and two proofs">

<Theorem name="Pythagorean theorem">

In a right triangle with legs $a$, $b$ and hypotenuse $c$:
$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

**First proof: rearrangement.** Take four copies of the right triangle, with legs $a$, $b$, hypotenuse $c$, and acute angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Since the angles of a triangle add up to $180^\circ$ and one of them is $90^\circ$, we have $\alpha + \beta = 90^\circ$.

*Left picture.* Place the four triangles in the corners of a square of side $a + b$, as shown, so that along each side of the big square a leg $a$ is followed by a leg $b$. The shape left in the middle has four sides, each a hypotenuse, so each of length $c$. At each of its corners, the angle of the middle shape sits on a straight side of the big square between an angle $\alpha$ of one triangle and an angle $\beta$ of the next, so it measures $180^\circ - (\alpha + \beta) = 180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$. A shape with four equal sides and four right angles is a square: its area is $c^2$.

*Right picture.* Rearrange the same four triangles into two $a \times b$ rectangles, placed in opposite corners of a second square of side $a + b$. What is left is a square of side $b$ and a square of side $a$: total area $a^2 + b^2$.

Both big squares have the same area, $(a + b)^2$, and both contain four triangles of the same total area. So the uncovered areas are equal:
$$
c^2 = a^2 + b^2.
$$

</Proof>

The left picture also gives an algebraic proof. Its total area is
$$
(a + b)^2 = c^2 + 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} a b, \quad\text{that is,}\quad a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = c^2 + 2ab,
$$
and subtracting $2ab$ from both sides leaves $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

<Proof>

**Second proof: the square in the middle.** Suppose $a \le b$. Arrange the four triangles inside the square on the hypotenuse, each with its hypotenuse along one side, turning a quarter turn from one to the next. Along each side of the middle shape, a long leg $b$ overlaps a short leg $a$, so the middle is a square of side $b - a$ (if $a = b$ it disappears). Adding up areas:
$$
c^2 = (b - a)^2 + 4 \cdot \tfrac12 ab = b^2 - 2ab + a^2 + 2ab = a^2 + b^2.
$$

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Two proofs, the same idea: compute one area in two different ways. And notice where the right angle was used — to show that the middle shape really is a square. Without the right angle, $\alpha + \beta$ would not be $90^\circ$, and the proof would collapse. That is why the theorem is false for other triangles.

</Narrator>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Finding the hypotenuse">

A right triangle has legs 5 cm and 12 cm. Then $c^2 = 5^2 + 12^2 = 25 + 144 = 169$, so $c = \sqrt{169} = 13$ cm.

</Example>

<Example title="Finding a leg: the ladder">

A ladder 2.5 m long leans against a wall with its foot 0.7 m from the wall. How high does it reach? The ladder is the hypotenuse: $h^2 + 0.7^2 = 2.5^2$, so $h^2 = 6.25 - 0.49 = 5.76$ and $h = \sqrt{5.76} = 2.4$ m.

</Example>

<Example title="A length that is not a whole number">

A square tile has side 1. Its diagonal splits it into two right triangles with legs 1 and 1, so the diagonal $d$ has $d^2 = 1 + 1 = 2$ and $d = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$. No whole number squares to 2 ($1^2 = 1$, $2^2 = 4$), and in fact $\sqrt{2}$ is not a fraction either — a discovery explored in <TopicLink id="math.number.real-numbers">real numbers</TopicLink>.

</Example>

<Example title="Is it a right triangle?">

Sides 6, 8, 10: $6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^2$. By the converse (below), the triangle has a right angle, opposite the side 10. Sides 5, 6, 8: $5^2 + 6^2 = 61$ but $8^2 = 64$. No right angle — in fact the angle opposite 8 is slightly more than $90^\circ$.

</Example>

<Example title="Screen sizes">

A screen is advertised by its diagonal. A screen 48 cm wide and 27 cm high has a diagonal of $\sqrt{48^2 + 27^2} = \sqrt{2304 + 729} = \sqrt{3033} \approx 55.1$ cm, about 21.7 inches.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="converse" kind="proof" title="The converse: a test for right angles">

The builder's 3-4-5 trick uses the theorem *backwards*: from the side lengths she concludes there is a right angle.

<Theorem name="Converse of the Pythagorean theorem">

If the sides of a triangle satisfy $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, then the triangle has a right angle, opposite the side $c$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let the triangle be $T$, with sides $a$, $b$, $c$ and $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. Build a second triangle $T'$ with a right angle between two legs of lengths $a$ and $b$. By the Pythagorean theorem, its hypotenuse $c'$ satisfies $c'^2 = a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. Since $c$ and $c'$ are both positive, $c' = c$. Now $T$ and $T'$ have the same three side lengths, so they are congruent: one can be moved exactly onto the other (a triangle is fixed by its three sides — the SSS rule). Congruent triangles have equal angles, so the angle of $T$ opposite $c$ equals the angle of $T'$ opposite $c'$, which is $90^\circ$.

</Proof>

The same comparison tells more. If $a^2 + b^2 > c^2$, the angle opposite $c$ is **acute** (less than $90^\circ$); if $a^2 + b^2 \lt c^2$, it is **obtuse** (more than $90^\circ$). The computation section checks this on hundreds of triangles; a proof comes later with the law of cosines in <TopicLink id="math.geom.trigonometry">trigonometry</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="distance" kind="proof" title="Distance in the coordinate plane">

In the <TopicLink id="math.geom.coordinate-plane">coordinate plane</TopicLink> we could measure distances along horizontal and vertical lines. Now we can measure them in any direction.

<Theorem name="Distance formula">

The distance between $P = (x_1, y_1)$ and $Q = (x_2, y_2)$ is
$$
PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $P$ and $Q$ lie on a horizontal or vertical line, this is the distance $|x_2 - x_1|$ or $|y_2 - y_1|$ from before (note that $\sqrt{u^2} = |u|$). Otherwise let $R = (x_2, y_1)$. The segment $PR$ is horizontal with length $|x_2 - x_1|$, the segment $RQ$ is vertical with length $|y_2 - y_1|$, and they meet at a right angle at $R$. So $PQ$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle, and $PQ^2 = |x_2 - x_1|^2 + |y_2 - y_1|^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2$.

</Proof>

<Example title="Two distances between the same points">

From $(1, 2)$ to $(4, 6)$: legs $3$ and $4$, distance $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$. From $(-3, 2)$ to $(4, -1)$: legs $7$ and $3$, distance $\sqrt{49 + 9} = \sqrt{58} \approx 7.62$ — shorter than the grid distance $7 + 3 = 10$. A bird can fly straight there, but a taxi in a grid city such as Manhattan, in New York, must drive along the streets: $7$ blocks one way and $3$ the other.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="triples" kind="human" title="Pythagorean triples">

A **Pythagorean triple** is a set of three positive whole numbers $(a, b, c)$ with $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, such as $(3, 4, 5)$, $(5, 12, 13)$ and $(8, 15, 17)$. Multiplying a triple by any whole number $k$ gives another, because $(ka)^2 + (kb)^2 = k^2(a^2 + b^2) = k^2 c^2 = (kc)^2$. So $(6, 8, 10)$, $(9, 12, 15)$, $(12, 16, 20)$, … are triples too, and there are **infinitely many** triples.

A triple whose three numbers have no common factor, like $(3, 4, 5)$, is **primitive**. Here is a recipe that produces them, recorded in Euclid's *Elements* (Book X).

<Theorem name="Euclid's formula">

For any whole numbers $m > n > 0$, the numbers $a = m^2 - n^2$, $b = 2mn$, $c = m^2 + n^2$ form a Pythagorean triple.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Expand both sides:
$$
(m^2 - n^2)^2 + (2mn)^2 = m^4 - 2m^2n^2 + n^4 + 4m^2n^2 = m^4 + 2m^2n^2 + n^4 = (m^2 + n^2)^2.
$$

</Proof>

$m = 2, n = 1$ gives $(3, 4, 5)$; $m = 3, n = 2$ gives $(5, 12, 13)$; $m = 4, n = 1$ gives $(15, 8, 17)$. In fact, when $m$ and $n$ have no common factor and are not both odd, the formula gives a primitive triple, and *every* primitive triple arises this way (with the two legs possibly swapped). That second statement is a theorem of number theory whose proof we postpone to <TopicLink id="math.number.elementary-number-theory">elementary number theory</TopicLink>; the computation below checks it for all triples up to 100.

What about cubes? Are there positive whole numbers with $a^3 + b^3 = c^3$, or $a^n + b^n = c^n$ for some $n \ge 3$? Pierre de Fermat claimed around 1637 that there are none. His claim, **Fermat's Last Theorem**, was proved only in 1995, by Andrew Wiles (with a final step completed together with Richard Taylor), using some of the deepest mathematics of the twentieth century.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Using $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ for triangles without a right angle.** The proof used the right angle to show that the middle shape is a square. For an equilateral triangle with sides 1, $1^2 + 1^2 = 2 \neq 1 = 1^2$. Before using the theorem, check that there is a right angle — or, if you only know the sides, use the converse.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Taking $c$ to be any side.** $c$ must be the **hypotenuse**, the side opposite the right angle, which is always the longest side. In the 3-4-5 triangle, $3^2 + 5^2 = 34 \neq 16 = 4^2$. When finding a missing leg, *subtract*: $a^2 = c^2 - b^2$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Thinking $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = a + b$.** For legs 3 and 4, $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$, not $7$. Squaring a sum gives an extra term: $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, which is bigger than $a^2 + b^2$ whenever $a, b > 0$. Geometrically: the hypotenuse is a short cut, shorter than walking along both legs.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“Pythagoras discovered it.”** The relation was known and used in Mesopotamia, India and China, in some cases more than a thousand years before Pythagoras (6th century BCE). No writing by Pythagoras survives, and we do not know whether he proved it. The oldest surviving complete proof is in Euclid's *Elements* (about 300 BCE).

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="On a sphere, Pythagoras fails">

The proof used the fact that the angles of a triangle add up to $180^\circ$ — true on a flat plane, false on a curved surface. On a globe, walk from the North Pole down to the equator, turn $90^\circ$, walk a quarter of the way round the equator, turn $90^\circ$ and walk back to the pole. Your path is a triangle with **three** right angles and three equal sides, so $a^2 + b^2 = 2c^2 \neq c^2$. For small triangles on a large sphere, $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ holds approximately, and the size of the error measures how curved the surface is. Try it below. <Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Whole-number solutions">

Asking for whole-number solutions of $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ turns a geometry theorem into a problem about integers. Euclid's formula answers it completely. Dividing by $c^2$ gives $(a/c)^2 + (b/c)^2 = 1$: each triple is a point with fraction coordinates on the circle of radius 1, such as $(\tfrac35, \tfrac45)$. Drawing lines through $(-1, 0)$ with fraction slopes finds every such point — a geometric derivation of Euclid's formula. Changing the exponent from 2 to 3 or more leads to Fermat's Last Theorem. See <TopicLink id="math.number.elementary-number-theory">elementary number theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="More dimensions">

Applying the theorem twice gives distances in space: the diagonal of a box with edges $a$, $b$, $h$ has length $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + h^2}$ (first the diagonal of the floor, then up). In coordinates, the length of a vector $(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is $\sqrt{x_1^2 + \dots + x_n^2}$ in any number of dimensions, and “perpendicular” becomes the algebraic condition $x_1 y_1 + \dots + x_n y_n = 0$ that appeared in our Lean proof of the converse. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.vectors">vectors</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Mesopotamia.** The Old Babylonian tablet YBC 7289 (about 1800–1600 BCE) shows a square with its diagonals and gives $\sqrt{2}$ in base 60 as $1;24,51,10 \approx 1.4142130$, correct to about six decimal places. The tablet Plimpton 322, from the same period, lists numbers closely related to Pythagorean triples such as $(119, 120, 169)$; its exact purpose is still debated.
- **India.** The *Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra* (dates uncertain, perhaps 800–500 BCE), a manual for building fire altars, states that the diagonal of a rectangle produces both areas that its length and breadth produce separately — the theorem in words — and lists triples.
- **China.** The *Zhoubi Suanjing* (compiled by about the 1st century CE) discusses the *gougu* rule with the 3-4-5 triangle. In the 3rd century CE, Zhao Shuang's commentary gave the “hypotenuse diagram” used in our second proof, and Liu Hui gave a dissection proof in his commentary on the *Nine Chapters*.
- **Greece.** Euclid's *Elements* (about 300 BCE) proves the theorem as Book I, Proposition 47, and its converse as Proposition 48. The traditional link with Pythagoras comes from much later writers.
- **The Islamic world and beyond.** Thābit ibn Qurra in 9th-century Baghdad generalised the theorem to all triangles. In 12th-century India, Bhāskara II gave a dissection proof with the square-in-the-middle figure. In 1876 James Garfield, later President of the United States, published a proof using a trapezium. Elisha Loomis's *The Pythagorean Proposition* (1927; 2nd edition 1940) collected 367 proofs.
- **1995.** Andrew Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem settled the question Fermat asked about higher powers.
