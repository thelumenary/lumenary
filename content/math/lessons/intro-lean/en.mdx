<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Can a computer check a proof?">

In 1998 Thomas Hales announced a proof of the **Kepler conjecture**: no way of stacking equal spheres fills space more densely than the familiar pyramid of oranges on a market stall. The proof combined hundreds of pages of argument with enormous computer calculations. After years of work, the referees reported that they were "99% certain" it was correct, but could not check every detail. So Hales started a new project, *Flyspeck*: to write the entire proof in a language so precise that a computer program could check every single step. It took a team of mathematicians in several countries more than a decade; the check was completed in 2014.

Programs that check proofs are called **proof assistants**. In 2020 the mathematician Peter Scholze challenged the community to check a difficult new theorem of his and Dustin Clausen's in the proof assistant **Lean**; a team led by Johan Commelin finished in 2022, and Scholze wrote that the project had clarified parts of the argument for him. Today Lean's community library, **Mathlib**, contains well over a hundred thousand theorems, from school algebra to research mathematics, every one of them machine-checked.

In this lesson you will write your first Lean proofs. You have seen Lean code throughout the Lumenary; now you will learn to read it, to write it — and to judge precisely what a green checkmark means.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="A very strict reader">

When you write a proof for a person, you can say "clearly" and skip a step. A proof assistant cannot be persuaded that way. It keeps track of two things:

- the **goal**: what remains to be proved, and
- the **hypotheses**: what you are allowed to use.

Each step you write changes the goal, and Lean checks that the step is legal. When no goals remain, the proof is complete. If a step is wrong — or missing — Lean refuses and tells you which goal is still open.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Precise instructions are my favourite thing. A proof assistant is the ultimate precise reader: it accepts *only* what follows from the rules. That is annoying for an afternoon and wonderful for a lifetime — once it says yes, you never have to wonder whether you fooled yourself.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The vocabulary">

<Definition term="proof assistant">

A **proof assistant** is a program that checks proofs written in a formal language. Its core, the **kernel**, is a small program that checks each step against the rules of the logic.

</Definition>

<Definition term="propositions as types">

In Lean, a statement (a **proposition**) is a **type**, and a proof of it is a **term** of that type. Writing `h : p` means "`h` is a proof of `p`". Checking a proof means checking that the term really has the claimed type.

</Definition>

<Definition term="tactic">

A **tactic** is a command that builds part of a proof by transforming the goal, such as `intro` (assume a hypothesis), `exact` (supply a proof), `rw` (rewrite with an equation) or `induction`. A proof written with tactics starts with `by`.

</Definition>

The connectives of logic each have a meaning in this language, closely matching the truth tables of <TopicLink id="math.found.propositional-logic">propositional logic</TopicLink>:

| Statement | How to prove it in Lean | How to use a proof of it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `p ∧ q` ("and") | give a pair `⟨hp, hq⟩` (tactic `constructor`) | take it apart (`obtain ⟨hp, hq⟩ := h`) |
| `p ∨ q` ("or") | `Or.inl hp` or `Or.inr hq` | consider both cases (`cases h`) |
| `p → q` ("implies") | a function from proofs of `p` to proofs of `q` (`intro hp`) | apply it: `h hp` is a proof of `q` |
| `¬p` ("not") | means `p → False` | apply it to a proof of `p` to get `False` |
| `a = b` | `rfl` if both sides compute to the same thing | rewrite with it (`rw [h]`) |

</Section>

<Section id="induction" kind="proof" title="Natural numbers and induction">

Lean's natural numbers are built from two constructors: `0`, and the successor `n + 1` of any natural number $n$. Addition is defined by **recursion on the second argument**:

$$
n + 0 = n, \qquad n + (k + 1) = (n + k) + 1.
$$

The first rule is literally the statement $n + 0 = n$, so Lean accepts it by `rfl` for every $n$:

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.add_zero_rfl" />

But $0 + n = n$ is different. With $n$ an unknown number, neither rule applies to $0 + n$ — the second argument $n$ is neither $0$ nor visibly of the form $k + 1$ — so the computation is stuck and `rfl` fails. We need a proof by **induction**.

<Theorem name="Theorem">

For every natural number $n$, $\;0 + n = n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By induction on $n$. *Base case:* $0 + 0 = 0$ by the first rule. *Inductive step:* assume $0 + k = k$. Then $0 + (k + 1) = (0 + k) + 1$ by the second rule, and this equals $k + 1$ by the assumption.

</Proof>

In Lean, each line of the human proof becomes one step:

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.zero_add_induction" />

Lean's library already contains this fact as `Nat.zero_add`, so we could simply apply it. Both are machine-checked, but they say different things: the first is proved **here, from the definitions**; the second **uses** a theorem someone else proved. The Lumenary labels every declaration as *independent* or *library* to keep this distinction honest.

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.zero_add_library" />

Here is a bigger induction: **Gauss's formula** $0 + 1 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$, stated without division as $2 \cdot (0 + 1 + \dots + n) = n(n + 1)$. We define the sum by recursion and prove the formula by induction; in the inductive step, the tactic `ring` does the algebra $k(k+1) + 2(k+1) = (k+1)(k+2)$.

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.sumUpTo" />

<Section id="mathlib" kind="formal" title="Reading Mathlib">

Much of using Lean is *reading* statements. Here is Euclid's theorem as Mathlib states it:

```lean
theorem Nat.exists_infinite_primes (n : ℕ) : ∃ p, n ≤ p ∧ Nat.Prime p
```

Read it piece by piece: the name; then the **arguments** in brackets (for any natural number `n`); then, after the colon, the **statement**: there exists `p` with `n ≤ p` and `p` prime. So: *for every $n$ there is a prime at least as large as $n$* — that is, there are infinitely many primes. Applying it is one line:

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.infinitely_many_primes" />

Statements about general structures have two more kinds of bracket. For instance

```lean
theorem add_comm {G : Type*} [AddCommMagma G] (a b : G) : a + b = b + a
```

says: for any type `G` (in **curly braces**: Lean will work out `G` by itself from `a` and `b`), carrying an addition that is commutative (in **square brackets**: a *structure* Lean finds automatically — for ℕ, ℤ, ℝ…), and any `a b : G`, we have `a + b = b + a`.

Two more library results. The first is certified by `norm_num`, an automated procedure that checks the arithmetic and hands the kernel a proof to re-check; the second is a famous theorem used as a black box:

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.prime_97" />

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.sqrt_two_irrational" />

Notice that `prime_97` lists three axioms — `propext`, `Classical.choice` and `Quot.sound`. These are the standard axioms of Mathlib's logic, the same for almost every theorem in the library. What you must *never* see in a finished proof is `sorryAx`.

<Section id="guarantees" kind="human" title="What a machine check guarantees — and what it does not">

When Lean accepts a theorem, the kernel has checked that the proof follows from Lean's rules and the axioms listed. That is a very strong guarantee — far stronger than "I read it carefully". But it guarantees exactly that, and nothing more.

**1. `sorry` means "not proved".** Writing `sorry` tells Lean "trust me, I'll fill this in later". Lean accepts the file with a warning, and `#print axioms` reports that the theorem depends on `sorryAx`. A proof containing `sorry` is a plan, not a proof. (The Lean exercises of this lesson are statements with `sorry` for you to replace.)

**2. The check is relative to the axioms and the kernel.** Lean's kernel is small and has been studied carefully, and its axioms are standard; but a proof is checked *relative to* them. That is why the Lumenary shows the axiom list under every declaration.

**3. A correct proof of the wrong statement is still a correct proof.** This is the most important lesson about formal mathematics. The theorem below is proved correctly:

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.no_nat_sqrt_two" />

It says that no *natural number* squares to $2$. It does **not** say that $\sqrt 2$ is irrational — that would need "no fraction squares to $2$", which is Mathlib's `irrational_sqrt_two` above. If someone announced "Lean has verified that $\sqrt 2$ is irrational" on the strength of `no_nat_sqrt_two`, the checkmark would be genuine and the claim false.

**4. Definitions have conventions you must know.** Lean's functions are defined for *every* input, so some familiar operations get unfamiliar conventions:

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.nat_sub_truncates" />

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.nat_div_rounds_down" />

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.real_div_zero" />

So the statement `∀ n : ℕ, n - 1 + 1 = n` is *false* in Lean (it fails at $n = 0$), and "$x / x = 1$" needs the hypothesis $x \ne 0$. These conventions are harmless once you know them — and dangerous if you don't.

**5. Impossible hypotheses prove anything.** A theorem whose hypotheses can never all hold is true and useless:

<Lean decl="Lumenary.IntroLean.vacuous" />

So when you read a formal theorem, ask: *Is this statement what was claimed? Can its hypotheses actually happen? Which axioms does it use?* The machine checks the proof; checking the statement is the human's job.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Machine-checked means it says what was intended."** Lean checks that the proof proves the *formal* statement. Whether the formal statement matches the informal claim — natural numbers versus fractions, missing hypotheses, conventions like $1/0 = 0$ — must be checked by people.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"It compiles, so it's finished."** A file with `sorry` compiles with a warning. Only a proof whose axiom list is free of `sorryAx` is complete.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Using a Mathlib lemma is the same as proving the result."** Applying `Nat.zero_add` gives a valid, checked proof, but the mathematical work was done elsewhere. Both are honest — as long as you say which one you did.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"`rfl` proves every true equation."** `rfl` works only when both sides *compute* to the same thing. $n + 0 = n$ does; $0 + n = n$ does not, though it is just as true.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The proof assistant finds the proof for you."** Tactics like `simp`, `ring` and `norm_num` automate routine steps, and modern tools can search for short proofs; but the ideas — which induction, which invariant, which lemma — usually still come from the mathematician.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="The same ideas, seen by others">

<Viewpoint lens="logic" title="Proofs are programs: the Curry–Howard correspondence">

Haskell Curry (1934, 1958) and William Howard (1969) noticed that the rules for proofs and the rules for typed programs are the same rules: a proof of $p \to q$ is a function, a proof of $p \wedge q$ is a pair, a proof of $p \vee q$ is a tagged choice. Under this correspondence, `and_swap` is literally the program that swaps the two parts of a pair. The correspondence is with **constructive** logic, where a proof of $p \vee q$ must say which side holds; principles such as $\neg\neg p \to p$ or "$p \vee \neg p$" are added as axioms, which is exactly what `double_negation`'s axiom list shows. See <TopicLink id="math.found.type-theory">type theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="A small trusted core">

The Dutch mathematician N. G. de Bruijn insisted that a proof checker's trusted core be small enough to check by hand — the **de Bruijn criterion**. Lean follows it: tactics, `simp` and `norm_num` may be large and clever, but everything they produce is re-checked by the small kernel, so a bug in a tactic can make it fail but cannot make a false theorem pass. Checking is computation: our toy checker in Python does on a small scale what the kernel does in general. Compare <TopicLink id="math.comp.algorithms-and-efficiency">algorithms and correctness</TopicLink>, where Lean checked loop invariants.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="sets" title="Types are not sets">

In set theory, everything is a set, and $\N \subseteq \Z \subseteq \R$ literally. In Lean every term has exactly one type: `(2 : ℕ)` and `(2 : ℝ)` are different terms, and Lean inserts a **coercion** `↑` when a natural number is used as a real number. Statements like "$x \in \N$" for a real $x$ must be phrased differently. Both foundations can express ordinary mathematics; they organise it differently. See <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">sets</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.found.zfc">ZFC</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="categories" title="Logic as a category">

Types and functions form a category, and in it "and" is the **product**: a proof of $r \to p \wedge q$ is the same as a pair of proofs of $r \to p$ and $r \to q$ — the universal property of <TopicLink id="math.cat.intro-universal-properties">products by maps</TopicLink>. Implication is the "exponential" object, and Joachim Lambek showed in the 1970s and 1980s that typed λ-calculi correspond precisely to **cartesian closed categories**. Logic, programming and category theory are three views of one structure.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Equality as a path">

In **homotopy type theory**, developed from the mid-2000s by Vladimir Voevodsky, Steve Awodey, Michael Warren and others, a proof of $a = b$ is pictured as a *path* from $a$ to $b$ in a space, and different proofs can be genuinely different paths — like two non-homotopic loops around a hole. Lean deliberately does not adopt this view: its propositions are *proof-irrelevant* (any two proofs of the same proposition are equal), which is incompatible with Voevodsky's univalence axiom. Homotopy type theory lives in other proof assistants. It is an honest example of a topological picture of logic — and of a design choice with consequences. See <TopicLink id="math.found.homotopy-type-theory">homotopy type theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Deciding identities by normal forms">

The tactic `ring` decides whether an equation holds in every commutative ring: it expands both sides into a **normal form** — a sum of monomials with coefficients, in a fixed order — and compares them. That is the algebraist's theorem that two polynomials are equal as polynomials exactly when their coefficients agree, turned into an algorithm that produces a checkable proof. The same strategy — compute normal forms, compare — is behind `simp` and `norm_num`.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Gottlob Frege**'s *Begriffsschrift* (Germany, 1879) created a fully formal language for logic. **Alfred North Whitehead** and **Bertrand Russell**'s *Principia Mathematica* (England, 1910–13) attempted to derive mathematics in such a language by hand — famously taking hundreds of pages to reach $1 + 1 = 2$.
- The first computer proof checkers appeared around 1970: **Automath**, by N. G. de Bruijn in Eindhoven (Netherlands, from 1967), and **Mizar**, by Andrzej Trybulec in Poland (from 1973), whose library of formalised mathematics is still growing. **Robin Milner**'s LCF (Stanford and Edinburgh, 1970s) introduced tactics and the small trusted kernel.
- **Per Martin-Löf** (Sweden) developed the type theory of the 1970s on which many systems build; **Thierry Coquand** and **Gérard Huet** created the Calculus of Constructions in France in the 1980s, the basis of Coq (renamed Rocq in 2025) and an ancestor of Lean's logic.
- Landmarks: the **four colour theorem** checked in Coq by Georges Gonthier and Benjamin Werner (2005); the **odd order theorem** of group theory (Gonthier and a team, 2012); the **Kepler conjecture** (Flyspeck, 2014, in HOL Light and Isabelle); the **Liquid Tensor Experiment** in Lean (2022).
- **Lean** was started by Leonardo de Moura at Microsoft Research in 2013; Lean 4 appeared in 2021. Mathlib is written by a worldwide volunteer community, and since 2024 Kevin Buzzard has led a project to formalise the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean.

</Section>
