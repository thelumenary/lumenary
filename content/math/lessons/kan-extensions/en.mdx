<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Extending as well as possible">

Here are three problems that look unrelated.

- You know a function only on the integers, say $n \mapsto n$, and you want a monotone function on all of $\mathbb{R}$ that is “as close as possible” to it. From below, the answer is $\lfloor x \rfloor$; from above, $\lceil x \rceil$.
- A subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ acts on a set or a vector space $V$. You want a $G$-action that is “freely generated” by the $H$-action. The answer is the induced representation $\mathrm{Ind}_H^G V$.
- You know a topological space $\Delta^n$ for each standard simplex $[n]$, and you want to turn *every* simplicial set into a space, gluing simplices the way the simplicial set says. The answer is geometric realisation.

In each case there is a functor $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{E}$ defined on a “small piece” $\mathcal{C}$, a functor $K : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ embedding (or merely mapping) that piece into a larger world, and we want the best functor $\mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$ that extends $F$. In general no functor extends $F$ on the nose — but there is a best approximation from the left and a best approximation from the right. These are the **left and right Kan extensions**, and they turn out to be so universal that Saunders Mac Lane ended the first edition of *Categories for the Working Mathematician* (1971) with a section titled **“All concepts are Kan extensions”**.

This lesson assumes <TopicLink id="math.cat.adjunctions">adjunctions</TopicLink>, <TopicLink id="math.cat.limits-colimits">limits and colimits</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.cat.yoneda">the Yoneda lemma</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="The picture: a triangle that need not commute">

Draw $F$ and $K$ as two sides of a triangle. We want to fill in the third side.

Asking for $L \circ K = F$ is usually hopeless: if $K$ sends two objects $a, b$ to the same object $d$, then $L(d)$ would have to equal both $F(a)$ and $F(b)$. Category theory's answer is to ask instead for a **natural transformation** $F \Rightarrow L \circ K$ — “$L$ is at least as big as $F$ along $K$” — and to take the *smallest* such $L$. Dually, a right Kan extension is the *largest* $R$ with $R \circ K \Rightarrow F$.

The poset case makes this concrete. A poset is a category with at most one arrow between two objects, a functor between posets is a monotone map, and a natural transformation $f \Rightarrow g$ exists exactly when $f \le g$ pointwise. The smallest monotone $L$ with $f(c) \le L(k(c))$ is forced: $L(d)$ must be at least $f(c)$ whenever $k(c) \le d$, so the best choice is

$$
L(d) = \bigvee \{\, f(c) \mid k(c) \le d \,\}.
$$

That supremum is the poset shadow of the general formula: **a left Kan extension at $d$ is the colimit of everything that maps to $d$.**

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

Throughout, $K : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{E}$ are functors. For a functor $G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$, write $GK = G \circ K$, and for a natural transformation $\sigma : G \Rightarrow G'$ write $\sigma K : GK \Rightarrow G'K$ for the whiskered transformation with components $(\sigma K)_c = \sigma_{Kc}$.

<Definition term="left Kan extension">

A **left Kan extension** of $F$ along $K$ is a functor $L : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$ together with a natural transformation $\eta : F \Rightarrow LK$ (the **unit**) such that for every functor $G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$ and every natural transformation $\alpha : F \Rightarrow GK$ there is a **unique** natural transformation $\sigma : L \Rightarrow G$ with

$$
\alpha = (\sigma K) \circ \eta, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \alpha_c = \sigma_{Kc} \circ \eta_c \ \text{ for all } c.
$$

Equivalently, $\sigma \mapsto (\sigma K) \circ \eta$ is a bijection $\mathrm{Nat}(L, G) \cong \mathrm{Nat}(F, GK)$ for every $G$. We write $L = \mathrm{Lan}_K F$; it is unique up to unique isomorphism, as any universal object is.

</Definition>

  objects={[

  ]}

  ]}
  caption="The universal property, drawn in the functor category $[\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{E}]$: every $\alpha$ factors uniquely through the unit $\eta$."
/>

<Definition term="right Kan extension">

A **right Kan extension** of $F$ along $K$ is a functor $R = \mathrm{Ran}_K F : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$ with a natural transformation $\varepsilon : RK \Rightarrow F$ (the **counit**) such that for every $G$ and every $\beta : GK \Rightarrow F$ there is a unique $\sigma : G \Rightarrow R$ with $\beta = \varepsilon \circ (\sigma K)$. Equivalently, $\mathrm{Nat}(G, R) \cong \mathrm{Nat}(GK, F)$.

</Definition>

If every $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{E}$ has a left (respectively right) Kan extension along $K$, the bijections above say precisely that $\mathrm{Lan}_K$ and $\mathrm{Ran}_K$ are the left and right adjoints of **restriction along $K$**, the functor $K^* : [\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{E}] \to [\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{E}]$, $G \mapsto GK$:

$$
\mathrm{Lan}_K \;\dashv\; K^* \;\dashv\; \mathrm{Ran}_K .
$$

To compute Kan extensions we need comma categories.

<Definition term="comma categories over and under an object">

For an object $d \in \mathcal{D}$, the comma category $(K \downarrow d)$ has as objects the pairs $(c, f)$ with $c \in \mathcal{C}$ and $f : Kc \to d$; a morphism $(c, f) \to (c', f')$ is a morphism $u : c \to c'$ with $f' \circ K(u) = f$. It comes with the projection $\pi_d : (K \downarrow d) \to \mathcal{C}$, $(c, f) \mapsto c$. Dually, $(d \downarrow K)$ has objects $(c, f : d \to Kc)$ and morphisms $u$ with $K(u) \circ f = f'$. (Mathlib calls these `CostructuredArrow K d` and `StructuredArrow d K`.)

</Definition>

<Definition term="pointwise Kan extension">

The left Kan extension is **pointwise** when, for every $d$,

$$
(\mathrm{Lan}_K F)(d) = \operatorname*{colim}_{(c,\, f) \in (K \downarrow d)} F(c),
$$

the colimit of $F \circ \pi_d$, and dually $(\mathrm{Ran}_K F)(d) = \lim_{(c, f) \in (d \downarrow K)} F(c)$. In words: the left Kan extension at $d$ glues together the values $F(c)$ of everything that maps to $d$, identified along the arrows between them.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Examples, non-examples and boundary cases">

<Example title="Floor and ceiling">

Let $K : \mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be the inclusion and $F : \mathbb{Z} \to \overline{\mathbb{Z}} = \mathbb{Z} \cup \{\pm\infty\}$ the inclusion into the complete lattice of extended integers. By the poset formula,

$$
(\mathrm{Lan}_K F)(x) = \sup\{\, n \in \mathbb{Z} \mid n \le x \,\} = \lfloor x \rfloor, \qquad (\mathrm{Ran}_K F)(x) = \inf\{\, n \in \mathbb{Z} \mid x \le n \,\} = \lceil x \rceil.
$$

Every monotone $g : \mathbb{R} \to \overline{\mathbb{Z}}$ that agrees with $F$ on the integers satisfies $\lfloor x \rfloor \le g(x) \le \lceil x \rceil$: the Kan extensions are the extreme extensions. Since $K$ is an order embedding, both restrict back to $F$ on $\mathbb{Z}$.

</Example>

<Example title="Limits and colimits">

Let $\mathbf{1}$ be the terminal category and $! : J \to \mathbf{1}$ the unique functor. A functor $c : \mathbf{1} \to \mathcal{E}$ is an object $c$, and a natural transformation $c \circ {!} \Rightarrow F$ is a family of maps $c \to F(j)$ compatible with all $F(u)$ — a **cone** over $F$ with vertex $c$. The universal property of $\mathrm{Ran}_! F$ says that there is a universal cone. Hence

$$
\mathrm{Ran}_! F = \lim F, \qquad \mathrm{Lan}_! F = \operatorname{colim} F,
$$

each existing exactly when the (co)limit does. (A full proof is in the next section.) For $J$ the discrete category on two objects this gives products and coproducts: the right Kan extension of $(A, B)$ to $\mathbf{1}$ is $A \times B$ — the universal property explored in the trail *The product, by its maps*.

</Example>

<Example title="Extending along a non-injective functor: no strict extension">

Let $K : \{a, b\} \to \mathbf{1}$ (discrete source) and $F : \{a, b\} \to \mathbf{Set}$ with $|F(a)| = 2$, $|F(b)| = 3$. Then $(\mathrm{Lan}_K F)(\ast) = F(a) \sqcup F(b)$ has $5$ elements and $(\mathrm{Ran}_K F)(\ast) = F(a) \times F(b)$ has $6$. Neither restricts back to $F$: $(\mathrm{Lan}_K F)K(a)$ has $5$ elements, not $2$. The unit $\eta_a : F(a) \to F(a) \sqcup F(b)$ is the coproduct inclusion — a genuine map, not an identity.

</Example>

<Example title="Induced and coinduced actions">

A group $G$ is a category $\mathbf{B}G$ with one object $\ast$ and one arrow for each $g \in G$; a functor $\mathbf{B}G \to \mathbf{Set}$ is a $G$-set. For a subgroup $H \le G$ with inclusion $i : \mathbf{B}H \to \mathbf{B}G$ and an $H$-set $X$:

- The comma category $(i \downarrow \ast)$ has one object for each $g \in G$ (an arrow $i(\ast) \to \ast$), and an arrow $g'h \to g'$ for each $h \in H$. Its colimit glues $(g'h, x)$ to $(g', h x)$, so $(\mathrm{Lan}_i X)(\ast) = G \times_H X$, the **induced** $G$-set, with $[G : H]\,|X|$ elements when $G$ is finite.
- Dually $(\mathrm{Ran}_i X)(\ast) = \mathrm{Hom}_H(G, X)$, the **coinduced** $G$-set of $H$-equivariant maps, with $|X|^{[G:H]}$ elements.

For $H = 1$ and $X = \{\ast\}$, $\mathrm{Lan}_i X = G$ acting on itself: the free $G$-set on one generator. The experiment below computes all of these as colimits and limits.

</Example>

<Example title="Non-example: a Kan extension that does not exist">

Let $k$ be the inclusion of the discrete poset $\{a, b\}$ into $\{a, b, \top\}$ with $a, b \le \top$, and let $f$ send $a \mapsto x$, $b \mapsto y$ in the *discrete* two-element poset $\{x, y\}$. A monotone $g$ with $f \le g \circ k$ would need $x \le g(a) \le g(\top)$ and $y \le g(b) \le g(\top)$, which is impossible. So there is no extension at all, and certainly no Kan extension. The pointwise formula fails precisely because the join $x \vee y$ does not exist.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="The main theorems">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (the pointwise formula)">

Suppose that for every $d \in \mathcal{D}$ the colimit $L(d) = \operatorname{colim}(F \circ \pi_d)$ over $(K \downarrow d)$ exists, with coprojections $\iota_{(c, f)} : F(c) \to L(d)$. Then $d \mapsto L(d)$ extends uniquely to a functor $L : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$ with $L(g) \circ \iota_{(c, f)} = \iota_{(c,\, g f)}$, and with $\eta_c = \iota_{(c,\, \mathrm{id}_{Kc})}$ the pair $(L, \eta)$ is a left Kan extension of $F$ along $K$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Functoriality.* An arrow $g : d \to d'$ induces a functor $g_* : (K \downarrow d) \to (K \downarrow d')$, $(c, f) \mapsto (c, gf)$, with $\pi_{d'} \circ g_* = \pi_d$. The maps $\iota_{(c, gf)} : F(c) \to L(d')$ therefore form a cocone over $F \circ \pi_d$, so there is a unique $L(g) : L(d) \to L(d')$ with $L(g) \circ \iota_{(c, f)} = \iota_{(c, gf)}$. Both $L(\mathrm{id})$ and $\mathrm{id}$, and both $L(g'g)$ and $L(g') L(g)$, satisfy the same equations on coprojections, so they agree by the uniqueness clause of the colimit.

*The unit is natural.* For $u : c \to c'$ in $\mathcal{C}$ we must show $L(Ku) \circ \eta_c = \eta_{c'} \circ F(u)$. The left side is $\iota_{(c, Ku)}$ by definition of $L(Ku)$. On the right, $u$ is a morphism $(c, Ku) \to (c', \mathrm{id}_{Kc'})$ of $(K \downarrow Kc')$, so the cocone condition gives $\iota_{(c', \mathrm{id})} \circ F(u) = \iota_{(c, Ku)}$.

*Existence of factorisations.* Let $G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$ and $\alpha : F \Rightarrow GK$. For fixed $d$, the maps $G(f) \circ \alpha_c : F(c) \to G(d)$ form a cocone over $F \circ \pi_d$: if $u : (c, f) \to (c', f')$, then $f' \circ K(u) = f$, and naturality of $\alpha$ gives

$$
G(f') \circ \alpha_{c'} \circ F(u) = G(f') \circ G(Ku) \circ \alpha_c = G(f) \circ \alpha_c .
$$

So there is a unique $\sigma_d : L(d) \to G(d)$ with $\sigma_d \circ \iota_{(c, f)} = G(f) \circ \alpha_c$. It is natural in $d$, because for $g : d \to d'$ both $\sigma_{d'} \circ L(g)$ and $G(g) \circ \sigma_d$ send $\iota_{(c,f)}$ to $G(gf) \circ \alpha_c$. Finally $\sigma_{Kc} \circ \eta_c = \sigma_{Kc} \circ \iota_{(c, \mathrm{id})} = G(\mathrm{id}) \circ \alpha_c = \alpha_c$.

*Uniqueness.* If $\sigma' : L \Rightarrow G$ also satisfies $\sigma'_{Kc} \circ \eta_c = \alpha_c$, then, using $\iota_{(c, f)} = L(f) \circ \iota_{(c, \mathrm{id})} = L(f) \circ \eta_c$ and naturality of $\sigma'$,

$$
\sigma'_d \circ \iota_{(c, f)} = \sigma'_d \circ L(f) \circ \eta_c = G(f) \circ \sigma'_{Kc} \circ \eta_c = G(f) \circ \alpha_c = \sigma_d \circ \iota_{(c, f)} ,
$$

so $\sigma'_d = \sigma_d$ by the uniqueness of maps out of a colimit.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Corollary (existence)">

If $\mathcal{C}$ is small, $\mathcal{D}$ is locally small and $\mathcal{E}$ has all small colimits, then every $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{E}$ has a pointwise left Kan extension along every $K$, so $\mathrm{Lan}_K \dashv K^*$. Dually, if $\mathcal{E}$ has all small limits, $K^* \dashv \mathrm{Ran}_K$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Each $(K \downarrow d)$ is small: its objects form the set $\coprod_{c} \mathcal{D}(Kc, d)$ (a set because $\mathcal{C}$ is small and $\mathcal{D}$ locally small), and its morphisms are among those of $\mathcal{C}$. So the colimits in Theorem 1 exist. The dual statement is Theorem 1 applied to $K^{\mathrm{op}}$ and $F^{\mathrm{op}}$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Proposition 2 (extension along a fully faithful functor)">

If $K$ is fully faithful and $\mathrm{Lan}_K F$ is pointwise, then the unit $\eta : F \Rightarrow (\mathrm{Lan}_K F) K$ is a natural isomorphism: the Kan extension really extends $F$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

In $(K \downarrow Kc)$ the object $(c, \mathrm{id}_{Kc})$ is terminal: given $(c', f : Kc' \to Kc)$, full faithfulness gives a unique $u : c' \to c$ with $K(u) = f$, which is exactly a unique morphism $(c', f) \to (c, \mathrm{id})$. The colimit of a diagram over a category with a terminal object $t$ is its value at $t$, with coprojection at $t$ an isomorphism. Here that coprojection is $\iota_{(c, \mathrm{id})} = \eta_c$.

</Proof>

The hypothesis matters: the two-point example above shows what happens for a $K$ that is not injective on objects.

<Theorem name="Proposition 3 (limits are right Kan extensions)">

For $F : J \to \mathcal{E}$, an object $\ell$ with a cone $\lambda : \ell \circ {!} \Rightarrow F$ is a right Kan extension of $F$ along $! : J \to \mathbf{1}$ if and only if $(\ell, \lambda)$ is a limit of $F$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Functors $\mathbf{1} \to \mathcal{E}$ are objects, and natural transformations between them are morphisms. A transformation $\beta : c \circ {!} \Rightarrow F$ is a cone with vertex $c$, and for $\sigma : c \to \ell$ the composite $\lambda \circ (\sigma\, {!})$ is the cone $(\lambda_j \circ \sigma)_j$. So the universal property of $\mathrm{Ran}_!$ — every $\beta$ equals $\lambda \circ (\sigma\, !)$ for a unique $\sigma$ — is word for word the universal property of a limit.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (adjoints are Kan extensions; Mac Lane)">

A functor $G : \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{X}$ has a left adjoint if and only if the right Kan extension $(R, \varepsilon) = \mathrm{Ran}_G \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{A}}$ exists and is **preserved by $G$**, meaning that $(GR, G\varepsilon)$ is a right Kan extension of $G$ along $G$. In that case $R$ is the left adjoint, with counit $\varepsilon$. In fact, if $F \dashv G$ then $(F, \varepsilon)$ is preserved by *every* functor out of $\mathcal{A}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

($\Rightarrow$) Let $F \dashv G$ with unit $\eta : \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}} \Rightarrow GF$, counit $\varepsilon : FG \Rightarrow \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{A}}$, and triangle identities $G\varepsilon \circ \eta G = 1_G$ and $\varepsilon F \circ F\eta = 1_F$. Let $P : \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{B}$ be any functor; we show $(PF, P\varepsilon)$ is a right Kan extension of $P$ along $G$. Given $H : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{B}$ and $\beta : HG \Rightarrow P$, put $\sigma_x = \beta_{Fx} \circ H(\eta_x) : H(x) \to PF(x)$. Then, by naturality of $\beta$ at $\varepsilon_a$ and the first triangle identity,

$$
P(\varepsilon_a) \circ \sigma_{Ga} = P(\varepsilon_a) \circ \beta_{FGa} \circ H(\eta_{Ga}) = \beta_a \circ HG(\varepsilon_a) \circ H(\eta_{Ga}) = \beta_a \circ H\big(G(\varepsilon_a) \circ \eta_{Ga}\big) = \beta_a .
$$

If $\sigma'$ is another solution, the second triangle identity and naturality of $\sigma'$ at $\eta_x$ give

$$
\sigma'_x = P(\varepsilon_{Fx}) \circ PF(\eta_x) \circ \sigma'_x = P(\varepsilon_{Fx}) \circ \sigma'_{GFx} \circ H(\eta_x) = \beta_{Fx} \circ H(\eta_x) = \sigma_x .
$$

Taking $P = \mathrm{id}$ shows $(F, \varepsilon) = \mathrm{Ran}_G \mathrm{id}$; taking $P = G$ shows that $G$ preserves it.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $(R, \varepsilon : RG \Rightarrow \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{A}})$ is $\mathrm{Ran}_G \mathrm{id}$ and $(GR, G\varepsilon)$ is $\mathrm{Ran}_G G$. Apply the universal property of $(GR, G\varepsilon)$ to the identity transformation $1_G : \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}} G \Rightarrow G$: there is a unique $\eta : \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}} \Rightarrow GR$ with $G\varepsilon \circ \eta G = 1_G$, which is one triangle identity. For the other, both $1_R$ and $\tau = \varepsilon R \circ R\eta$ are transformations $R \Rightarrow R$, and the universal property of $(R, \varepsilon)$ says there is only one $\tau$ with $\varepsilon \circ \tau G = \varepsilon$. The identity qualifies, and so does $\tau$: by naturality of $\varepsilon$ at the arrow $\varepsilon_a$,

$$
\varepsilon_a \circ \varepsilon_{RGa} \circ R(\eta_{Ga}) = \varepsilon_a \circ RG(\varepsilon_a) \circ R(\eta_{Ga}) = \varepsilon_a \circ R\big(G(\varepsilon_a) \circ \eta_{Ga}\big) = \varepsilon_a .
$$

Hence $\varepsilon R \circ R\eta = 1_R$. Both triangle identities hold, so $R \dashv G$ with unit $\eta$ and counit $\varepsilon$.

</Proof>

Note the twist: a **left** adjoint is a **right** Kan extension, $F = \mathrm{Ran}_G \mathrm{id}$, and dually a right adjoint is a left Kan extension, $G = \mathrm{Lan}_F \mathrm{id}$. In posets: the ceiling function, left adjoint to $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$, is an infimum; the floor, right adjoint, is a supremum.

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (extending along the Yoneda embedding)">

Let $\mathcal{C}$ be small, $\mathcal{E}$ cocomplete and locally small, $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{E}$, and $y : \mathcal{C} \to \widehat{\mathcal{C}} = [\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}, \mathbf{Set}]$ the Yoneda embedding. Then $L = \mathrm{Lan}_y F$ exists and is given by

$$
L(P) = \operatorname*{colim}_{(c,\, x) \in \int P} F(c),
$$

the colimit over the category of elements of $P$ (objects: pairs $c \in \mathcal{C}$, $x \in P(c)$). Moreover $L \circ y \cong F$; $L$ is left adjoint to the “nerve” $N_F : \mathcal{E} \to \widehat{\mathcal{C}}$, $N_F(e) = \mathcal{E}(F(-), e)$; and $L$ is, up to isomorphism, the unique colimit-preserving functor $\widehat{\mathcal{C}} \to \mathcal{E}$ whose composite with $y$ is isomorphic to $F$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Sketch.* By the Yoneda lemma an arrow $y(c) \to P$ is the same as an element $x \in P(c)$, and this identifies the comma category $(y \downarrow P)$ with $\int P$ (up to size: $\widehat{\mathcal{C}}$ is locally small because $\mathcal{C}$ is small, so $\int P$ is small). Theorem 1 gives the formula. The Yoneda embedding is fully faithful, so Proposition 2 gives $Ly \cong F$. For the adjunction, maps out of a colimit are compatible families:

$$
\mathcal{E}(L P, e) \cong \lim_{(c, x) \in \int P} \mathcal{E}(F c, e) \cong \mathrm{Nat}\big(P, \mathcal{E}(F(-), e)\big),
$$

the second step because a natural transformation $P \Rightarrow N_F(e)$ is exactly an assignment $x \mapsto (Fc \to e)$ compatible with restriction. Being a left adjoint, $L$ preserves colimits. Uniqueness: every presheaf is canonically the colimit of representables over its category of elements (the density theorem, which is the case $F = y$), so a colimit-preserving functor is determined by its values on representables. Full details: Riehl (2016), Chapter 6, or Mac Lane and Moerdijk (1992), Chapter I.

</Proof>

This “nerve and realisation” pattern appears all over mathematics: with $F : \Delta \to \mathbf{Top}$, $[n] \mapsto \Delta^n$, it gives geometric realisation $|{-}| \dashv \mathrm{Sing}$; with $F : \Delta \to \mathbf{Cat}$, $[n] \mapsto$ the poset $\{0 < \dots < n\}$, it gives the nerve of a category and its left adjoint.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**“Kan extensions always exist.”** They do not: the two-point example above has no extension at all, because the target lacks a join. The standard sufficient condition is: $\mathcal{C}$ small, $\mathcal{D}$ locally small, and $\mathcal{E}$ cocomplete (for $\mathrm{Lan}$) or complete (for $\mathrm{Ran}$). The local smallness of $\mathcal{D}$ is easy to forget: it is what makes the comma categories $(K \downarrow d)$ small. Not every Kan extension that exists is pointwise either; in practice the pointwise ones are the ones that behave well.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Confusing left and right.** Left Kan extensions are computed by **colimits** over $(K \downarrow d)$ — “everything that maps *to* $d$” — and right Kan extensions by **limits** over $(d \downarrow K)$. Mnemonic: floor is $\mathrm{Lan}$, ceiling is $\mathrm{Ran}$. But beware the twist: a *left adjoint* is a *right* Kan extension, $F = \mathrm{Ran}_G \mathrm{id}$, because its counit $FG \Rightarrow \mathrm{id}$ has the shape of a Kan extension counit.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“A Kan extension restricts back to the original functor.”** Only when the unit is an isomorphism, which holds for pointwise extensions along fully faithful $K$ (Proposition 2). Along $K : \{a, b\} \to \mathbf{1}$, $(\mathrm{Lan}_K F)K(a) = F(a) \sqcup F(b) \neq F(a)$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“A Kan extension is just some extension.”** The universal property is the point: $\mathrm{Lan}_K F$ is the *initial* functor equipped with $F \Rightarrow (-)K$. In the floor example infinitely many monotone functions extend the integers; the Kan extensions are the two extreme ones.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Viewpoints">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Induction, Frobenius reciprocity and extension of scalars">

For a subgroup $H \le G$ and the inclusion $i : \mathbf{B}H \to \mathbf{B}G$, restriction $i^*$ is restriction of representations from $G$ to $H$. For functors into $\mathrm{Vect}_k$, the pointwise formula gives

$$
\mathrm{Lan}_i V = k[G] \otimes_{k[H]} V = \mathrm{Ind}_H^G V, \qquad \mathrm{Ran}_i V = \mathrm{Hom}_{k[H]}(k[G], V) = \mathrm{CoInd}_H^G V,
$$

and the adjunctions $\mathrm{Lan}_i \dashv i^* \dashv \mathrm{Ran}_i$ are **Frobenius reciprocity**: $\mathrm{Hom}_G(\mathrm{Ind}_H^G V, W) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_H(V, \mathrm{Res}^G_H W)$, and its dual. For linear representations and $[G:H] < \infty$, induction and coinduction are isomorphic; for $G$-sets they are not ($[G:H]\,|X|$ versus $|X|^{[G:H]}$ elements), which the experiment shows. More generally, a ring homomorphism $R \to S$ is a functor between one-object $\mathbf{Ab}$-enriched categories, and the *enriched* left Kan extension of a module along it is extension of scalars $S \otimes_R M$, with coextension $\mathrm{Hom}_R(S, M)$ on the right; this needs the enriched theory of <TopicLink id="math.cat.enriched-categories">enriched categories</TopicLink>, where Kan extensions are coends.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Inverse images of presheaves and geometric realisation">

Let $f : X \to Y$ be continuous and write $\mathcal{O}(X)$ for the poset of open sets. Preimage is a monotone map $f^{-1} : \mathcal{O}(Y) \to \mathcal{O}(X)$. For a presheaf $\mathcal{F}$ on $Y$, the pushforward is just precomposition, $f_*\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{F} \circ (f^{-1})^{\mathrm{op}}$, so its left adjoint is a left Kan extension along $(f^{-1})^{\mathrm{op}}$. The pointwise formula computes it: the comma category at an open $U \subseteq X$ consists of the opens $V \subseteq Y$ with $U \subseteq f^{-1}(V)$, i.e. $f(U) \subseteq V$, so

$$
(f^{-1}_{\mathrm{pre}} \mathcal{F})(U) = \operatorname*{colim}_{V \supseteq f(U)} \mathcal{F}(V),
$$

the familiar formula for the presheaf inverse image (the sheaf inverse image is its sheafification; see <TopicLink id="math.geom.sheaves">sheaves</TopicLink>). Geometric realisation of simplicial sets is the left Kan extension of $[n] \mapsto \Delta^n$ along the Yoneda embedding $\Delta \to \mathbf{sSet}$ (Theorem 5), with right adjoint the singular complex. Beyond this lesson: in <TopicLink id="math.geom.homotopy-theory">homotopy theory</TopicLink> one needs *homotopy* Kan extensions (derived functors of $\mathrm{Lan}$, such as homotopy colimits), and in <TopicLink id="math.cat.infinity-categories">∞-categories</TopicLink> Kan extensions are defined up to coherent homotopy.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Best approximations from below and above">

Let $f : \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{R}$ be monotone and $k : \mathbb{Q} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ the inclusion, with values in the complete lattice $[-\infty, \infty]$. Then

$$
(\mathrm{Lan}_k f)(x) = \sup_{q \le x} f(q), \qquad (\mathrm{Ran}_k f)(x) = \inf_{q \ge x} f(q).
$$

Both extend $f$ (since $k$ is an order embedding), every monotone extension $g$ of $f$ satisfies $\mathrm{Lan}_k f \le g \le \mathrm{Ran}_k f$, and at an irrational $x$ the two are the one-sided limits $f(x^-)$ and $f(x^+)$. They differ exactly at the jumps of $f$, and a monotone real-valued function has at most countably many jumps (disjoint open intervals each contain a rational). So the monotone extension of $f$ to $\mathbb{R}$ is unique if and only if $f$ has no jumps at irrational points. The Python exercise computes these envelopes on finitely many sample points.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="logic" title="Quantifiers are Kan extensions">

Regard a function $f : X \to Y$ as a functor between discrete categories, and a predicate on $X$ as a functor $P : X \to \mathbf{2} = \{\bot \le \top\}$. Then the pointwise formulas give

$$
(\mathrm{Lan}_f P)(y) = \exists x\, \big(f(x) = y \wedge P(x)\big), \qquad (\mathrm{Ran}_f P)(y) = \forall x\, \big(f(x) = y \Rightarrow P(x)\big),
$$

and $\mathrm{Lan}_f \dashv f^* \dashv \mathrm{Ran}_f$ is Lawvere's discovery that $\exists_f \dashv f^* \dashv \forall_f$: quantifiers are adjoint to substitution. For a projection $X \times Y \to X$ these are the ordinary quantifiers over $Y$. See <TopicLink id="math.cat.categorical-logic">categorical logic</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="Codensity monads and faster programs">

For any functor $G : \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{X}$, if the right Kan extension $T = \mathrm{Ran}_G G$ exists it carries a canonical <TopicLink id="math.cat.monads">monad</TopicLink> structure, the **codensity monad** of $G$; when $G$ has a left adjoint $F$, $T \cong GF$ is the monad of the adjunction (by Theorem 4, applied with $P = G$). A classical example (Kennison and Gildenhuys, 1971): the codensity monad of the inclusion $\mathbf{FinSet} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Set}$ is the ultrafilter monad. In functional programming, Haskell's `Codensity m a = forall b. (a -> m b) -> m b` is $\mathrm{Ran}_m m$ written as an end, and Voigtländer (2008) used it to turn left-nested binds of free monads from quadratic into linear time.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="All concepts are Kan extensions">

Mac Lane's slogan collects the results of this lesson:

- **limits and colimits** are Kan extensions along $J \to \mathbf{1}$ (Proposition 3);
- **adjoints** are Kan extensions of identities, $F = \mathrm{Ran}_G \mathrm{id}$ and $G = \mathrm{Lan}_F \mathrm{id}$ (Theorem 4);
- **density and Yoneda**: the density theorem says $\mathrm{Lan}_y\, y \cong \mathrm{id}_{\widehat{\mathcal{C}}}$ — every presheaf is canonically a colimit of representables (Theorem 5 with $F = y$);
- **monads** arise as codensity monads $\mathrm{Ran}_G G$.

Even Kan extensions are Kan extensions: the definition uses only functors, natural transformations and their composites, so it makes sense in any 2-category, and the pointwise formula becomes a weighted colimit (a coend, $\int^{c} \mathcal{D}(Kc, d) \cdot F(c)$) in the enriched setting of Kelly (1982). The slogan is an exaggeration — not every concept is usefully a Kan extension — but it records how often a construction turns out to be “the best approximation along a functor”.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Daniel M. Kan** (born in the Netherlands, later at MIT) introduced adjoint functors in his paper *Adjoint functors* (Transactions of the American Mathematical Society, 1958), working in homotopy theory and simplicial sets. The same paper constructs what are now called Kan extensions, by the colimit and limit formulas.
- In the 1960s, the Grothendieck school's work on topos theory (SGA 4, 1963–64) made systematic use of extending functors from a site to all presheaves by colimits, and **F. William Lawvere** showed that quantifiers are adjoint to substitution (1969).
- **Saunders Mac Lane**'s *Categories for the Working Mathematician* (1971) ends its chapter on Kan extensions with the section “All concepts are Kan extensions”.
- **Eduardo Dubuc** (Argentina) developed *Kan extensions in enriched category theory* (Springer Lecture Notes 145, 1970), and **G. M. Kelly** (Sydney) made pointwise enriched Kan extensions central in *Basic Concepts of Enriched Category Theory* (1982).
- Today Kan extensions are basic tools in homotopy theory (homotopy Kan extensions, ∞-categorical Kan extensions in Lurie's *Higher Topos Theory*, 2009), in representation theory, in algebraic geometry, and in functional programming.

</Section>
