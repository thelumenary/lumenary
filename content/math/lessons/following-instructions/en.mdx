<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Put the jam on the bread">

In a classroom in Nairobi, Mr Otieno has a loaf of bread, a jar of jam and a spoon. "I am a robot," he says. "I will make a jam sandwich, but I will do **exactly** what you tell me — nothing more."

"Put the jam on the bread!" calls Wanjiru.

Mr Otieno picks up the whole jar and puts it on top of the loaf. Everybody laughs. That is not what Wanjiru meant! But it *is* what she said.

"Open the jar," tries Baraka. "Take a slice of bread out of the bag. Put a spoonful of jam on the slice. Spread it with the spoon…" Step by step, the sandwich gets made.

Hello! I'm Chloe. Why did the first try go wrong? Wanjiru knew what she meant — but the "robot" couldn't read her mind. Good instructions say *exactly* what to do, one step at a time.

<Section id="ideas" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="instruction">

An **instruction** tells you to do one thing, such as "move one square up" or "turn left".

</Definition>

<Definition term="algorithm">

An **algorithm** (or **program**) is a precise list of steps that can be followed to complete a task. You do the steps **one after another, in order**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="bug">

A **bug** is a mistake in an algorithm that makes it do something we did not want. Finding and fixing bugs is called **debugging**. Every programmer in the world does it, every day!

</Definition>

<Narrator who="gideon">

I'm Gideon. I like precision. "Go over there a bit" is not an instruction I can follow. Where is "there"? How much is "a bit"? "Move 2 squares right" — now *that* I can do.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Example title="Finding a bug">

Juma wants the robot to go **2 squares up and 1 square to the right**. He writes: *forward, forward, turn left, forward*. He tests it, and the robot ends up one square to the **left**. There is a bug!

Which step is wrong? The first two steps go up correctly. The third step turns the wrong way. Changing **turn left** to **turn right** fixes the program: *forward, forward, turn right, forward*. Testing it again shows that it works.

<Section id="precise" kind="human" title="Why instructions must be precise">

A robot or a computer **does not know what you mean**. It only does what you **say**. That is why the jam went on the loaf.

- **Each step must be clear.** "Turn" is not enough: turn left or right? "Go forward" — how many squares?
- **The steps must be in the right order.** Spreading the jam before opening the jar does not work.
- **The same program always does the same thing.** If you follow a program twice from the same start, you end up in the same place twice. That is what makes programs so useful — and it is why, when a program has a bug, it does the wrong thing *every* time until you fix it.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why is it good that a robot never guesses? Because then you can *trust* it. If the program is right, it works every single time. And if it is wrong, you can find exactly which step to change.

</Narrator>

<Note>

Testing is part of writing a program. Write your steps, follow them carefully (or let a friend follow them), and see what happens. If something goes wrong, that is not a failure — it is a bug to find, and finding it is a little puzzle.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's yes-or-no game">

Gideon reads a statement. Is it **true** or **false**?

1. "The program → → ↑ takes you to the square 2 across and 1 up." — **True.**
2. "There is only one correct program to reach a square." — **False!** ↑ → → and → → ↑ both reach the same square.
3. "For the robot, *forward then turn right* does the same as *turn right then forward*." — **False.** The robot ends on different squares.
4. "*Turn left, then turn right* leaves the robot exactly as it was." — **True.** The second turn undoes the first.
5. "You can reach the square 3 across and 2 up with only 4 arrow cards." — **False.** Each card moves you only one square, and you need 3 across and 2 up: at least 5 cards.

<Narrator who="margaret">

My computer is a very fast, very obedient instruction-follower. I asked it to run **every** arrow program with up to 6 cards — there are 5,460 of them! — and to find the shortest programs to each square. Margaret's computer found that the shortest program to a square always has exactly "squares across plus squares up" cards, and that there are usually **many** shortest programs. To reach the square 2 across and 2 up there are 6 different shortest programs; to reach 3 across and 3 up there are 20!

<ForGrownUps title="Margaret's program">

The number of shortest programs to the square $a$ across and $b$ up is the binomial coefficient $\binom{a+b}{a}$: a shortest program has $a + b$ cards, and choosing which $a$ of them are "across" cards decides the whole program. The program checks this, and the distance rule, for every square reached by up to 6 cards; the Lean file proves the distance rule for all programs. On the town map above, H to L is 2 across and 2 down, so there are $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ shortest routes.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"The robot knows what I mean."** It doesn't. A robot or a computer follows the instructions exactly as they are written, with no guessing. If the program says "turn left" when you meant "turn right", it turns left.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"There is only one right answer."** Usually there are many correct programs for the same task, like the 6 different shortest routes from Home to the Library. Some may be shorter or easier to read, but they all work.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The order doesn't matter, as long as all the steps are there."** Sometimes it doesn't (→ ↑ and ↑ → reach the same square), but often it does: *forward, turn right* and *turn right, forward* end in different places, and socks must go on before shoes.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A bug means I'm bad at this."** Every programmer writes bugs. Testing and debugging are a normal, important part of writing any algorithm.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Two ways of looking at programs">

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="The computer scientist: programs are built from steps">

A computer scientist builds big programs out of small ones: follow one program, then another. Instead of writing *forward, forward, forward, forward*, you can say "repeat 4 times: forward" — that is a **loop**, and "if there is a wall, turn right" is a **decision**. You will meet both in <TopicLink id="math.comp.loops-and-conditions">Repetition and decisions</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="The geometer: distance on a grid">

On a grid where you can only move along the lines, the distance between two squares is not "as the crow flies". It is **squares across plus squares up**, because no program can be shorter than that. Grown-ups call this the *taxicab distance*, after taxis driving on a grid of streets. It is a real distance, with its own geometry: the squares that are 2 steps from the start make a diamond, not a circle!

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Instructions in history">

- **The word "algorithm".** Around 820 CE, in Baghdad, Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī wrote a book explaining how to calculate with the Hindu–Arabic numerals 0 to 9. When it was translated into Latin, his name was written *Algoritmi* — and that gave us the word **algorithm**.
- **A loom that follows instructions.** In 1804, Joseph-Marie Jacquard in France built a weaving loom controlled by a chain of punched cards. The holes in the cards told the loom which threads to lift, so the same pattern could be woven again and again.
- **The first published computer program.** In 1843, Ada Lovelace published notes on Charles Babbage's planned Analytical Engine, including a step-by-step method for the machine to calculate a sequence of numbers called the Bernoulli numbers.
- **The first "actual bug".** In 1947, the team working on the Harvard Mark II computer found a moth stuck in one of its switches, and taped it into their logbook as the "first actual case of bug being found". (Engineers had already called mistakes "bugs" for decades.) Grace Hopper, a member of the team, loved to tell the story.
- **Turtles for children.** In the late 1960s, Seymour Papert, Cynthia Solomon and Wally Feurzeig created the Logo language, in which children give instructions like "forward" and "right" to a "turtle" — sometimes a little floor robot — to draw shapes.

</Section>
