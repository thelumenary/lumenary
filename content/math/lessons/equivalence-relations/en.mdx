<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Sorting by sameness">

A teacher in Nairobi wants to celebrate birthdays, so she groups her class by **birth month**. A librarian in Montevideo shelves books by the **first letter** of the author's surname. A train timetable in Delhi treats 14:00 and 2 p.m. as **the same time**, and a recipe treats $\tfrac{1}{2}$ cup and $\tfrac{2}{4}$ cup as **the same amount**.

Each time, a rule says when two things are “the same in some respect” — and then everything can be sorted into boxes, with every item in exactly one box.

But not every rule of the form “these two are alike” sorts so neatly. Try grouping people by “lives within 1 km of each other”: Amara lives 1 km east of Bongani, and Bongani 1 km east of Chen, so Amara and Bongani belong together, and Bongani and Chen belong together — yet Amara and Chen are 2 km apart. Which box does Bongani go in?

This lesson finds the three properties that make a sameness rule sort cleanly, and proves that such rules and ways of splitting a set into boxes are really one idea, seen from two sides.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="A relation is a yes-or-no question about pairs">

A **relation** on a set answers a yes-or-no question for every pair of its elements: *is $a$ related to $b$?* Take the set $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and the question “does $a$ divide $b$?”. The pairs that answer *yes* are

$$
(1,1),\ (1,2),\ (1,3),\ (1,4),\ (2,2),\ (2,4),\ (3,3),\ (4,4).
$$

We can show the same information in three ways: as this **set of ordered pairs**, as a **table** with a mark in row $a$, column $b$ whenever $a$ is related to $b$, or as an **arrow diagram** with an arrow from $a$ to $b$ (a little loop when $a$ is related to itself).

Three questions about any relation turn out to matter most, and each has a picture in the arrow diagram:

- Does every element have a **loop**? (Is everything related to itself?)
- Does every arrow have a **return arrow**? (If $a$ is related to $b$, is $b$ related to $a$?)
- Does every two-step path have a **shortcut**? (If $a \to b \to c$, is there an arrow $a \to c$?)

<Narrator who="chloe">

In the divides diagram every dot has a loop, and every two-step path has a shortcut: $1 \to 2 \to 4$ comes with $1 \to 4$. But the arrow $1 \to 2$ has no return arrow — $2$ does not divide $1$. Keep these three questions in your pocket. A rule that passes all three can sort a set into boxes.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="relation">

A **relation** $R$ on a set $A$ is a subset of the Cartesian product $A \times A$, that is, a set of ordered pairs of elements of $A$. We write $a \mathrel{R} b$, read “$a$ is related to $b$”, when $(a, b) \in R$.

</Definition>

Order matters in a pair: $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$ are different pairs (see <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">sets</TopicLink>), so a relation can contain one without the other.

<Definition term="reflexive, symmetric, transitive">

A relation $R$ on $A$ is

- **reflexive** if $a \mathrel{R} a$ for every $a \in A$;
- **symmetric** if whenever $a \mathrel{R} b$, also $b \mathrel{R} a$;
- **transitive** if whenever $a \mathrel{R} b$ and $b \mathrel{R} c$, also $a \mathrel{R} c$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="equivalence relation">

An **equivalence relation** is a relation that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Equivalence relations are often written $\sim$ (“tilde”), and $a \sim b$ is read “$a$ is equivalent to $b$”.

</Definition>

<Definition term="equivalence class">

If $\sim$ is an equivalence relation on $A$ and $a \in A$, the **equivalence class** of $a$ is the set of everything equivalent to $a$:

$$
[a] = \{\, x \in A \mid x \sim a \,\}.
$$

</Definition>

<Definition term="partition">

A **partition** of a set $A$ is a collection of subsets of $A$, called **blocks**, such that

1. every block is non-empty;
2. every element of $A$ lies in some block;
3. different blocks are **disjoint**: they have no element in common.

</Definition>

Conditions 2 and 3 together say: *every element of $A$ lies in exactly one block.* That is precisely what a teacher's birthday boxes achieve.

<Note>

To show that a relation **has** one of the three properties, we must check *every* case. To show that it **fails** a property, one **counterexample** is enough: a single element without a loop, a single arrow without its return, or a single two-step path without its shortcut.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Examples and non-examples">

<Example title="Testing six relations">

| Relation | Set | Reflexive? | Symmetric? | Transitive? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| same remainder on division by 3 | $\Z$ | yes | yes | yes |
| same birth month | people | yes | yes | yes |
| $a$ divides $b$ | $\{1, \dots, 6\}$ | yes | no: $1 \mid 2$, but $2 \nmid 1$ | yes |
| $a < b$ | $\Z$ | no: $0 \not< 0$ | no: $0 < 1$, but $1 \not< 0$ | yes |
| differ by at most 1 | $\Z$ | yes | yes | no: $0, 1$ and $1, 2$, but not $0, 2$ |
| is a sibling of | people | no | yes | no |

Only the first two are equivalence relations. The last row has a subtle failure of transitivity: if Ana and Ben are siblings, then Ana is a sibling of Ben and Ben is a sibling of Ana — but Ana is not her own sibling.

</Example>

<Example title="Finding the classes">

On $\Z$, let $a \sim b$ mean “$a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder on division by $3$”. Remainders on division by $3$ are $0$, $1$ or $2$, also for negative numbers: $-1 = 3 \cdot (-1) + 2$ has remainder $2$. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
[0] &= \{\dots, -6, -3, 0, 3, 6, \dots\},\\
[1] &= \{\dots, -5, -2, 1, 4, 7, \dots\},\\
[2] &= \{\dots, -4, -1, 2, 5, 8, \dots\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every class has many names: $[2] = [5] = [-1]$, because $2 \sim 5 \sim -1$. A class is a **set**, and any of its members can be used to name it.

</Example>

<Example title="From a table to a partition">

The relation $\{(0,0), (1,1), (2,2), (0,1), (1,0)\}$ on $\{0, 1, 2\}$ is an equivalence relation (check the three properties — there are only $3 + 9 + 27$ cases). Its classes are $[0] = [1] = \{0, 1\}$ and $[2] = \{2\}$, so it splits the set into the partition $\{\{0, 1\}, \{2\}\}$.

</Example>

<Example title="From a partition to a relation">

The partition $\{\{1, 4\}, \{2\}, \{3, 5\}\}$ of $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ defines the relation “lies in the same block as”. Its pairs are all pairs from the same block:

$$
(1,1), (1,4), (4,1), (4,4),\quad (2,2),\quad (3,3), (3,5), (5,3), (5,5).
$$

A block with $k$ elements contributes $k \cdot k$ ordered pairs, so there are $4 + 1 + 4 = 9$ pairs in all.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proof" kind="proof" title="Classes, partitions and remainders">

Throughout, $\sim$ is an equivalence relation on a set $A$.

<Theorem name="Lemma 1 (every element is in its own class)">

For every $a \in A$, $a \in [a]$. In particular, every class is non-empty.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$a \sim a$ by reflexivity, so $a \in [a]$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Lemma 2 (equivalent elements have equal classes)">

If $a \sim b$, then $[a] = [b]$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Two sets are equal when they have the same elements, so we show each is a subset of the other.

If $x \in [a]$, then $x \sim a$; together with $a \sim b$, transitivity gives $x \sim b$, so $x \in [b]$.

If $x \in [b]$, then $x \sim b$. By symmetry $b \sim a$, and transitivity gives $x \sim a$, so $x \in [a]$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (equal or disjoint)">

Any two equivalence classes $[a]$ and $[b]$ are either equal or disjoint.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose $[a]$ and $[b]$ are not disjoint: some $x$ lies in both. Then $x \sim a$ and $x \sim b$. By symmetry $a \sim x$, and by transitivity $a \sim b$. By Lemma 2, $[a] = [b]$. So if two classes share even one element, they are equal.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (the classes form a partition)">

The equivalence classes of $\sim$ form a partition of $A$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Check the three conditions. Every class is non-empty and every element $a$ lies in a class, namely $[a]$ (Lemma 1). Different classes are disjoint by Theorem 1.

</Proof>

Now go the other way, starting from boxes instead of a rule.

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (every partition comes from exactly one equivalence relation)">

Let $P$ be a partition of $A$, and say $a \approx b$ when $a$ and $b$ lie in the same block. Then $\approx$ is an equivalence relation whose classes are exactly the blocks of $P$, and it is the only equivalence relation with those classes.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Reflexive:* every $a$ lies in some block $B$, so $a$ and $a$ lie in the same block. *Symmetric:* “$a$ and $b$ lie in a common block” says the same as “$b$ and $a$ lie in a common block”. *Transitive:* suppose $a, b \in B$ and $b, c \in C$ for blocks $B$ and $C$. The blocks $B$ and $C$ share the element $b$, so they are not disjoint, and therefore $B = C$. So $a$ and $c$ both lie in $B$.

*The classes are the blocks:* if $a$ lies in the block $B$, then $[a]$ is the set of $x$ that share a block with $a$. Since $a$ lies in exactly one block, that block is $B$, so $[a] = B$.

*Only one:* if $\sim$ is any equivalence relation whose classes are the blocks of $P$, then $a \sim b$ means $a \in [b]$, which means $a$ and $b$ lie in the same class — the same block. So $\sim$ is $\approx$.

</Proof>

So **equivalence relations on $A$ and partitions of $A$ are two descriptions of the same thing**: a rule for “the same” gives boxes, boxes give a rule for “the same”, and doing one after the other brings you back where you started.

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (remainders)">

Let $n \ge 1$. On $\Z$, the relation “$a$ and $b$ have the same remainder on division by $n$” is an equivalence relation with exactly $n$ classes, namely $[0], [1], \dots, [n-1]$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $r(a)$ for the remainder of $a$ on division by $n$: the unique number $r$ with $0 \le r \le n - 1$ and $a = qn + r$ for some integer $q$. The relation is “$r(a) = r(b)$”. Equality of remainders is reflexive ($r(a) = r(a)$), symmetric (if $r(a) = r(b)$ then $r(b) = r(a)$) and transitive (if $r(a) = r(b)$ and $r(b) = r(c)$ then $r(a) = r(c)$).

*At most $n$ classes:* every integer $a$ has some remainder $r$ with $0 \le r \le n - 1$, and $r(r) = r$ (because $r = 0 \cdot n + r$), so $a \sim r$ and $a \in [r]$. So every class is one of $[0], [1], \dots, [n-1]$.

*At least $n$ classes:* if $0 \le r, s \le n - 1$ and $[r] = [s]$, then $r \sim s$, so $r = r(r) = r(s) = s$. So these $n$ classes are all different.

</Proof>

The same proof works for **any** rule of the form “$f(a) = f(b)$”, where $f$ is a <TopicLink id="math.found.functions">function</TopicLink> on $A$: same birth month, same first letter, same last digit, same remainder. And every equivalence relation is of this form — take $f(a) = [a]$, the function that sends each element to its own class.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**“Symmetric and transitive together imply reflexive.”** The argument “$a \sim b$, so $b \sim a$, so $a \sim a$” assumes there is some $b$ related to $a$. If an element is related to nothing, the argument never starts. On $\{0, 1\}$, the relation $\{(0, 0)\}$ is symmetric and transitive, but $1$ is not related to itself. Reflexivity has to be checked separately.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“An equivalence class is a single element.”** The class $[2]$ for remainders on division by $3$ is the whole set $\{\dots, -4, -1, 2, 5, 8, \dots\}$. The number $2$ is just one convenient name for it; $[5]$ and $[-1]$ name the same set.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“Every relation is symmetric.”** Many everyday relations are not: “is less than”, “divides”, “is a parent of”, “is taller than”. In a symmetric relation every arrow has a return arrow; check for one before assuming it.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“A relation that is true for nearby things is transitive.”** “Differ by at most 1” and “lives within 1 km of” feel like sameness, but small differences add up: $0$ is close to $1$, and $1$ to $2$, yet $0$ is not close to $2$. Such relations do not sort a set into boxes.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“Transitivity only matters for three different elements.”** The definition covers *all* $a, b, c$, including $c = a$. That is exactly why “is a sibling of” fails: Ana is Ben's sibling and Ben is Ana's, but Ana is not her own sibling.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="What is a shape?">

Two triangles are **congruent** if a rigid motion — a slide, turn or flip — carries one exactly onto the other, and **similar** if this is possible after also scaling (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">rigid motions, congruence and similarity</TopicLink>). Both are equivalence relations: every triangle is congruent to itself (do nothing), a motion can be undone (symmetry), and two motions can be done one after another (transitivity). The classes of “similar” are what we mean by *a shape*: “the shape of an equilateral triangle” is a whole class of triangles of every size, position and orientation. This viewpoint — study what stays the same under a family of transformations — grew into Felix Klein's Erlangen programme (1872; see <TopicLink id="math.geom.transformation-geometry">transformation geometry</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Gluing">

Take a line segment from $0$ to $1$ and declare its two endpoints equivalent (and every other point equivalent only to itself). The classes are the points of a new space, and it is a **circle**: walking off the end at $1$ brings you back in at $0$. Do the same with a square, gluing each point of the left edge to the point at the same height on the right edge, and each point of the top edge to the point below it on the bottom edge, and you get a **torus**, the surface of a doughnut. A video game in which leaving the screen on the right brings you back on the left, and leaving at the top brings you back at the bottom, is played on such a torus. Mathematicians build many spaces this way, as *quotient spaces* (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.point-set-topology">point-set topology</TopicLink>); the extra work at that level is to say what “nearby” means for the new points.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Arithmetic with classes">

The remainder classes on division by $n$ can be added and multiplied: $[a] + [b] = [a + b]$ and $[a] \cdot [b] = [a b]$. This only makes sense because the answer does not depend on which member names each class — if $a \sim a'$ and $b \sim b'$, then $a - a'$ and $b - b'$ are multiples of $n$, so $(a + b) - (a' + b')$ is one too. The result is a number system with exactly $n$ elements, written $\Z/n\Z$: the arithmetic of clocks and calendars (see <TopicLink id="math.number.modular-arithmetic">clock arithmetic</TopicLink> and, later, <TopicLink id="math.number.congruences">congruences</TopicLink>). The Remainder families trail below explores it.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Set-theoretic connection: numbers as classes">

Equivalence relations let mathematicians **build** number systems. A fraction $\tfrac{a}{b}$ can be thought of as a pair $(a, b)$ of integers with $b \neq 0$, where two pairs count as the same number when $a d = b c$: so $(1, 2) \sim (2, 4) \sim (3, 6)$. This is an equivalence relation, and a <TopicLink id="math.number.rational-numbers">rational number</TopicLink> *is* a class of such pairs. Writing $\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{2}{4}$ means that the two pairs lie in the same class.

The integers can be built the same way from pairs of natural numbers, with $(m, n)$ standing for “$m - n$” and $(m, n) \sim (m', n')$ when $m + n' = m' + n$ — a construction mentioned in the <TopicLink id="math.number.integers">integers</TopicLink> lesson. In both cases the hard work is checking that the relation really is an equivalence relation, and that addition and multiplication do not depend on which member of a class you pick.

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="A glimpse of structure: the quotient map">

Write $A/{\sim}$ for the set of equivalence classes, and $\pi : A \to A/{\sim}$ for the function $\pi(a) = [a]$ that sends each element to its class. Now suppose $f : A \to B$ is any function that is **constant on classes**: whenever $a \sim a'$, $f(a) = f(a')$. Then $f$ “only looks at the class”, so there is exactly one function $\bar f : A/{\sim} \to B$ with $\bar f([a]) = f(a)$.

For example, $f(a) = (-1)^a$ is $1$ for every even integer and $-1$ for every odd one. It is constant on the classes of “same remainder on division by $2$”, so it is really a function on the two classes: $\bar f([0]) = 1$ and $\bar f([1]) = -1$. Category theorists call this property of $\pi$ a **universal property** (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.universal-properties">universal properties</TopicLink>): it describes $A/{\sim}$ completely by how functions *out of* it behave, and the same pattern produces quotient groups and rings (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.quotients">quotients</TopicLink>).

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- The first of the “common notions” in Book I of **Euclid**'s *Elements* (Alexandria, around 300 BCE) reads: *things which are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another*. It is a transitivity principle for equality, used throughout Greek geometry.
- Reasoning by remainders is ancient. The Chinese *Sunzi Suanjing* (*Master Sun's Mathematical Manual*, around the 3rd to 5th century CE) asks for a number that leaves remainder $2$ when counted by threes, $3$ when counted by fives and $2$ when counted by sevens. In India, **Āryabhaṭa**'s *Āryabhaṭīya* (499 CE) gave the *kuṭṭaka* (“pulveriser”) method for problems of this kind, and in China **Qin Jiushao**'s *Shushu Jiuzhang* (1247) gave a general method.
- **Carl Friedrich Gauss**'s *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801) introduced the notation $a \equiv b \pmod{n}$ and treated the remainder classes as objects in their own right.
- **Gottlob Frege**, in *Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* (1884), proposed defining the direction of a line as the class of all lines parallel to it — an early, explicit use of equivalence classes to define a new kind of object. Defining numbers, shapes and spaces as classes became standard in 20th-century mathematics.

</Section>
