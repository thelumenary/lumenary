<Section id="steps" kind="intuition" title="Ask, sort, count, show">

Finding things out with data has four steps.

1. **Ask** a clear question that has a few possible answers.
2. **Sort** the answers into groups called **categories** — here: walk, bus, bike and car. Each answer goes into exactly **one** category.
3. **Count** the answers in each category. Tally marks help.
4. **Show** the counts in a picture, so everyone can see the story at a glance.

Here are the 24 answers, in the order the children were asked:

🚶 🚌 🚶 🚗 🚲 🚶 🚌 🚶 🚲 🚌 🚶 🚗 🚶 🚌 🚲 🚶 🚌 🚶 🚲 🚌 🚗 🚶 🚌 🚲

Can you tell which is most common just by looking? It's hard! Let's sort and count.

</Section>

<Section id="big-ideas" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="data">

**Data** are pieces of information collected to answer a question.

</Definition>

<Definition term="category">

A **category** is one of the groups we sort the answers into. For a simple survey, every answer goes into exactly one category.

</Definition>

<Definition term="tally mark">

A **tally mark** is one stroke that records one answer. Tally marks are grouped in fives: four strokes, then a fifth stroke across them, like a little gate. Groups of five are quick to count: five, ten, fifteen, …

</Definition>

<Definition term="picture graph">

A **picture graph** shows each category as a row (or column) of pictures. Each picture stands for one answer — or for a fixed number of answers, which the **key** tells you. All the pictures are the same size and evenly spaced. A **block graph** is the same idea with blocks or squares instead of pictures.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Example title="Do the totals add up?">

There are 24 children in the class. Add the numbers: $9 + 7 + 5 + 3 = 24$. The totals add up to the number of children asked. That is a good check: if the totals came to 23 or 25, we would know that an answer had been missed or counted twice.

</Example>

<Example title="Reading the graph">

Look at the graph below and answer the questions.

- **Which is most?** Walking: 9 children. **Which is fewest?** Car: 3 children.
- **How many more** children walk than come by bus? Pair them up: 7 walkers have a bus partner, and 2 do not. So $9 - 7 = 2$ more walk.
- **How many altogether** come on wheels (bus, bike or car)? $7 + 5 + 3 = 15$.

So the head teacher learns that 5 children come by bike — enough for a small rack now, with room to grow — and that 9 children walk, so a safe crossing really matters.

</Example>

<Section id="why" kind="human" title="Two rules for honest graphs">

**Rule 1: each answer goes into exactly one category.** Then, when we count each category and add the counts, every child is counted **once** — never forgotten, never twice. That is why the totals add up to the number of children.

What if the question were *"Which fruits do you like?"* and a child said "mango and papaya"? That child would be counted in two categories. Then the totals add up to **more** than the number of children. That's not wrong, but you must not expect the totals to add up. A question like *"What is your favourite fruit?"* gives one answer per child.

**Rule 2: pictures must be the same size and evenly spaced.** We often compare rows by looking at how long they are. That only works if every picture takes up the same amount of room.

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon looks at Ms Wanjiru's data. True or false?

1. "More children walk than come by bike." — **True.** 9 is more than 5.
2. "The four numbers add up to 24, the number of children." — **True.** $9 + 7 + 5 + 3 = 24$.
3. "No category is bigger than walking." — **True.** Check each one: bus 7, bike 5, car 3 are all less than 9.
4. "If children may choose two fruits they like, the fruit totals always add up to the number of children." — **False!** A child who chooses two fruits is counted twice.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"The longest row has the most."** Only if the pictures are all the same size and evenly spaced. Big pictures make a short row look long. Count the pictures, or check that the graph is fair.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"I'll put this answer in both groups."** In a simple survey each answer goes into exactly one category. If an answer is counted twice, the totals no longer add up to the number of people asked.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A tally gate is one mark."** The gate — four strokes with a fifth across them — stands for **five** answers. Count gates as five, ten, fifteen, and then count on the loose marks.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Our graph tells us about everybody."** Our survey tells us about the children we asked — this class, on this day. Another class, or the same class on a rainy day, might give different answers.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="For grown-ups: where data leads">

<Viewpoint lens="sets" title="Sorting is a partition">

Sorting answers so that each lands in exactly one category is a **partition** of the set of answers into disjoint blocks, and "the totals add up" is the addition principle: if $A = A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_k$ with the $A_i$ pairwise disjoint, then $|A| = |A_1| + \dots + |A_k|$. When categories overlap (several answers allowed), the sum of the totals counts each person once for every category they are in, and correcting for overlaps leads to inclusion–exclusion: $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|$. See <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">sets</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.found.equivalence-relations">equivalence relations and partitions</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="From counts to distributions">

Dividing each count by the total turns a tally into **relative frequencies** — here $9/24$, $7/24$, $5/24$ and $3/24$ — the empirical distribution of a categorical variable. Asking a different class, or the same class on another day, gives different counts: that variability is where statistics begins. Later lessons summarise data by averages and spread, compare samples, and ask how far a sample tells us about a larger population. See <TopicLink id="math.prob.data-displays">data displays and the mean</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.prob.statistics-intro">statistics: centre, spread and distributions</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Counting and showing, around the world">

- For centuries the English treasury recorded payments on **tally sticks**: notches cut in a stick that was then split in two, so that each side kept a matching half. They were used until 1826, and burning the old sticks in 1834 started the fire that destroyed the old Palace of Westminster in London.
- In China, Japan and Korea, people often tally with the character **正**, which takes exactly five strokes to write — a tally gate of its own. In some other countries people draw a square and then a diagonal: five strokes again.
- The **Inca** of the Andes recorded counts of people, animals and goods on knotted cords called **khipu**.
- The Scottish engineer **William Playfair** published some of the first bar charts in 1786.
- In Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s, **Otto Neurath**, **Marie Neurath** and the artist **Gerd Arntz** designed picture graphs called **Isotype**. Their key rule is the one in this lesson: show a bigger number with *more* pictures, never with *bigger* pictures.
- For the 1900 World's Fair in Paris, the American scholar **W. E. B. Du Bois** and his students at Atlanta University made beautiful hand-drawn charts showing the lives and achievements of African Americans.
