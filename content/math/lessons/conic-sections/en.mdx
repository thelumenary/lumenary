<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Curves with a secret point">

A solar cooker is a shiny dish that gathers sunlight onto a pot. Families in sunny regions from India to Bolivia use them to cook without firewood. The dish is not just any bowl: its cross-section is a **parabola**, and every ray of sunlight that enters parallel to its axis is reflected through a single point, the **focus**. Satellite dishes and radio telescopes work the same way, in reverse for car headlights.

In some hospitals, kidney stones are shattered without surgery by a *lithotripter*: a shock wave is produced at one focus of a reflector shaped like part of an **ellipsoid**, and every reflected wave arrives together at the other focus — where the stone has been placed. And when Johannes Kepler studied the orbit of Mars, he found it was an **ellipse** with the Sun at one focus. In 2017 astronomers tracked the object ʻOumuamua passing the Sun on a **hyperbola**: it came from interstellar space and is leaving for good.

Parabolas, ellipses and hyperbolas are the **conic sections**: the curves you get by slicing a cone. In this lesson we meet three descriptions of them — slices of a cone, sets of points defined by distances, and second-degree equations — and prove that they agree.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Slicing a cone">

Take a **double cone**: two ice-cream cones joined at their tips, extending forever. Slice it with a flat plane.

- A plane perpendicular to the axis gives a **circle**.
- Tilt the plane a little: the slice is a closed oval, an **ellipse**.
- Tilt it until it is parallel to a line on the cone (a *generator*): the curve no longer closes up. It is a **parabola**.
- Tilt it further, so that it cuts both halves of the cone: you get the two branches of a **hyperbola**.
- A plane through the tip gives a point, a single line or a pair of crossing lines: the **degenerate** conics.

Slices are hard to compute with, so the ancient Greek geometers also described the curves by **distances**. You can draw an ellipse with two pins and a loop of string: put the pins at two points, keep the string taut with a pencil, and move round. The total length from the pencil to the two pins never changes. Gardeners lay out oval flower beds this way.

Three descriptions — slices, distances, equations — of the same curves? That can't be a coincidence. Let's find out why they agree.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="ellipse">

Given two points $F_1$, $F_2$ (the **foci**) and a length $2a$ greater than the distance $F_1F_2$, the **ellipse** is the set of points $P$ with

$$
PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a.
$$

</Definition>

<Definition term="parabola">

Given a point $F$ (the **focus**) and a line $\ell$ (the **directrix**) not through $F$, the **parabola** is the set of points equidistant from $F$ and from $\ell$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="hyperbola">

Given two foci $F_1$, $F_2$ and a length $2a$ with $0 \lt 2a \lt F_1F_2$, the **hyperbola** is the set of points $P$ with

$$
\left|PF_1 - PF_2\right| = 2a.
$$

</Definition>

<Definition term="eccentricity">

For a point $F$, a line $\ell$ not through $F$, and a number $e > 0$, the set of points $P$ with $PF = e \cdot (\text{distance from } P \text{ to } \ell)$ is an ellipse if $e \lt 1$, a parabola if $e = 1$ and a hyperbola if $e > 1$ (we prove this below). The number $e$ is the **eccentricity**. A circle is the limiting case $e = 0$; it has no directrix.

</Definition>

Placing the foci symmetrically on the $x$-axis gives the **standard equations**. In each row, $a$, $b$, $c$ are positive (for the parabola, $p \ne 0$):

| Curve | Foci | Equation | Relation | Eccentricity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ellipse | $(\pm c, 0)$ | $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ | $b^2 = a^2 - c^2$ | $e = c/a \lt 1$ |
| Hyperbola | $(\pm c, 0)$ | $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ | $b^2 = c^2 - a^2$ | $e = c/a > 1$ |
| Parabola | $(0, p)$; directrix $y = -p$ | $x^2 = 4py$ | — | $e = 1$ |

For the ellipse, $a$ is the **semi-major axis** and $b$ the **semi-minor axis**. The hyperbola has **asymptotes** $y = \pm\tfrac{b}{a}x$: its branches get closer and closer to these lines without meeting them. The ellipse with $a = b$ (so $c = 0$) is the circle $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="The equation of a parabola from its focus and directrix">

Take the focus $F = (0, 2)$ and the directrix $y = -2$. A point $P = (x, y)$ is at distance $\sqrt{x^2 + (y - 2)^2}$ from $F$ and $|y + 2|$ from the line. Setting them equal and squaring: $x^2 + y^2 - 4y + 4 = y^2 + 4y + 4$, so $x^2 = 8y$, or $y = \tfrac18 x^2$. In general, focus $(0, p)$ and directrix $y = -p$ give $x^2 = 4py$.

</Example>

<Example title="Finding the foci of an ellipse">

$9x^2 + 25y^2 = 225$. Divide by $225$: $\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1$, so $a = 5$, $b = 3$ and $c = \sqrt{25 - 9} = 4$. The foci are $(\pm 4, 0)$, the eccentricity is $\tfrac45$, and every point of the ellipse has distances to the foci adding up to $2a = 10$. Check at the top point $(0, 3)$: both distances are $\sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$.

</Example>

<Example title="Completing the square">

What is $4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 54y + 61 = 0$? Group and complete the squares (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.quadratics">quadratics</TopicLink>):

$$
4(x^2 - 4x) + 9(y^2 + 6y) + 61 = 4(x - 2)^2 - 16 + 9(y + 3)^2 - 81 + 61,
$$

so the equation is $4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 3)^2 = 36$, that is,

$$
\frac{(x - 2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y + 3)^2}{4} = 1:
$$

an ellipse with centre $(2, -3)$, $a = 3$, $b = 2$ and foci $(2 \pm \sqrt5, -3)$. If the constant had been $97$ instead of $61$, we would have reached $4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 3)^2 = 0$, a single point; with a larger constant, no points at all.

</Example>

<Example title="A hyperbola in disguise">

The graph of $y = 1/x$, that is $xy = 1$, is a hyperbola whose asymptotes are the coordinate axes. Rotating it by $45^\circ$ (substitute $x = (X - Y)/\sqrt2$, $y = (X + Y)/\sqrt2$) turns $xy = 1$ into $X^2 - Y^2 = 2$, which is in standard form with $a = b = \sqrt2$ and $c = 2$. The $xy$ term was hiding the standard form.

</Example>

<Example title="Non-examples">

- $x^2 - y^2 = 0$ factorises as $(x - y)(x + y) = 0$: a pair of crossing lines, not a hyperbola.
- $x^2 + y^2 = 0$ is a single point, and $x^2 + y^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real points at all.
- A parabola is **not** half of a hyperbola and has no asymptotes: on $y = x^2/4$ the slope $x/2$ grows without bound, so no straight line $y = mx + k$ stays close to it (the gap $x^2/4 - mx - k$ tends to infinity).

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Why the descriptions agree">

<Theorem name="Theorem (the parabola)">

A point $(x, y)$ is equidistant from the focus $(0, p)$ and the directrix $y = -p$ if and only if $x^2 = 4py$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Both distances, $\sqrt{x^2 + (y - p)^2}$ and $|y + p|$, are non-negative, so they are equal exactly when their squares are: $x^2 + y^2 - 2py + p^2 = y^2 + 2py + p^2$, which simplifies to $x^2 = 4py$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem (the ellipse)">

Let $0 \le c \lt a$ and $b^2 = a^2 - c^2$. A point $P = (x, y)$ satisfies $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ if and only if $PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a$, where $F_1 = (c, 0)$ and $F_2 = (-c, 0)$. Moreover, on the ellipse

$$
PF_1 = a - \frac{c}{a}x \qquad\text{and}\qquad PF_2 = a + \frac{c}{a}x.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $d_1 = PF_1 = \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2}$ and $d_2 = PF_2 = \sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2}$.

*Equation $\Rightarrow$ distances.* On the ellipse, $y^2 = b^2\left(1 - \frac{x^2}{a^2}\right) = (a^2 - c^2)\left(1 - \frac{x^2}{a^2}\right)$. Substituting,

$$
d_1^2 = x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + a^2 - c^2 - x^2 + \frac{c^2x^2}{a^2} = a^2 - 2cx + \frac{c^2 x^2}{a^2} = \left(a - \frac{c}{a}x\right)^2.
$$

Since $|x| \le a$ on the ellipse and $c \lt a$, the number $a - \frac{c}{a}x$ is positive, so $d_1 = a - \frac{c}{a}x$. Replacing $x$ by $-x$ gives $d_2 = a + \frac{c}{a}x$, and $d_1 + d_2 = 2a$.

*Distances $\Rightarrow$ equation.* Suppose $d_1 + d_2 = 2a$. Subtracting the squares, $d_2^2 - d_1^2 = (x + c)^2 - (x - c)^2 = 4cx$. Since $d_2^2 - d_1^2 = (d_2 - d_1)(d_2 + d_1) = 2a(d_2 - d_1)$, we get $d_2 - d_1 = \frac{2cx}{a}$. Together with $d_1 + d_2 = 2a$ this gives $d_1 = a - \frac{c}{a}x$. Squaring, $(x - c)^2 + y^2 = a^2 - 2cx + \frac{c^2x^2}{a^2}$, which rearranges to $x^2\left(1 - \frac{c^2}{a^2}\right) + y^2 = a^2 - c^2$. Dividing by $a^2 - c^2 = b^2$ gives $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

The same algebra, with $b^2 = c^2 - a^2$, shows that $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ is the hyperbola $|d_1 - d_2| = 2a$: on its right branch $d_1 = \frac{c}{a}x - a$ and $d_2 = \frac{c}{a}x + a$.

<Theorem name="Corollary (focus and directrix of an ellipse)">

If $c > 0$, put $e = c/a$. Every point of the ellipse satisfies $PF_1 = e \cdot (\text{distance from } P \text{ to the line } x = a/e)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$PF_1 = a - \frac{c}{a}x = e\left(\frac{a}{e} - x\right)$, and $\frac{a}{e} - x$ is the distance from $P$ to the vertical line $x = a/e$, because $x \le a \lt a/e$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem (eccentricity decides the type)">

Let $F$ be the origin, let $\ell$ be the line $x = -k$ with $k > 0$, and let $e > 0$. The set of points with $PF = e \cdot (\text{distance to } \ell)$ is an ellipse if $e \lt 1$, a parabola if $e = 1$, and a hyperbola if $e > 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Squaring $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = e\,|x + k|$ gives

$$
(1 - e^2)\,x^2 - 2e^2k\,x + y^2 = e^2k^2.
$$

If $e = 1$, the $x^2$ term vanishes and $y^2 = 2kx + k^2$: a parabola (opening to the right). If $e \ne 1$, complete the square in $x$, with $h = \frac{e^2k}{1 - e^2}$: the equation becomes $(1 - e^2)(x - h)^2 + y^2 = r$ with $r = \frac{e^2k^2}{1 - e^2}$. If $e \lt 1$, both squares have positive coefficients and $r > 0$: an ellipse. If $e > 1$, the coefficients have opposite signs and $r \lt 0$; multiplying by $-1$ gives the standard form of a hyperbola. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem (Dandelin spheres: slices of a cone are ellipses)">

If a plane cuts every generator of one half of a cone (and misses the tip), the curve of intersection is an ellipse.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Inscribe two spheres in the cone, one on each side of the plane, each touching the cone along a circle ($C_1$ and $C_2$) and touching the plane at a single point ($F_1$ and $F_2$). Let $P$ be a point of the curve. The generator through $P$ meets $C_1$ at $Q_1$ and $C_2$ at $Q_2$. Two tangent segments drawn from a point to a sphere have equal length, so $PF_1 = PQ_1$ (both tangent to the first sphere) and $PF_2 = PQ_2$. Hence

$$
PF_1 + PF_2 = PQ_1 + PQ_2 = Q_1Q_2,
$$

and $Q_1Q_2$ — the distance between the two circles measured along a generator — is the same for every generator. So the sum of distances to $F_1$ and $F_2$ is constant: the curve is an ellipse, and the points where the spheres touch the plane are its foci. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

Germinal Pierre Dandelin found this argument in 1822. Similar arguments (with one sphere, or with spheres in both halves of the cone) handle the parabola and the hyperbola.

</Section>

<Section id="reflection" kind="proof" title="The reflective properties">

Light reflects from a curved mirror as it would from the **tangent line** at the point where it hits: the reflected ray is the mirror image, in the tangent line, of the ray that would have continued straight on.

<Theorem name="Reflective property of the parabola">

A ray travelling parallel to the axis of a parabola is reflected through the focus.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let the ray come straight down and hit the parabola at $P$. Let $D$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to the directrix, so the ray, continued straight on, would travel from $P$ towards $D$. By definition $PF = PD$. Let $t$ be the perpendicular bisector of $FD$; it passes through $P$, because $P$ is equidistant from $F$ and $D$.

We claim $t$ is the tangent at $P$. Take any other point $Q$ of $t$. Then $QF = QD$ (as $Q$ is on the perpendicular bisector), and $QD$ is longer than the distance from $Q$ to the directrix, because $QD$ is not perpendicular to the directrix (only $PD$ is). So $QF$ is greater than the distance from $Q$ to the directrix, and $Q$ is not on the parabola. Thus $t$ meets the parabola only at $P$ and is not parallel to the axis (it is perpendicular to $FD$, which is not vertical unless $P$ is the vertex, where $t$ is the horizontal tangent): it is the tangent line.

Reflection in $t$ fixes $P$ and swaps $D$ and $F$ (that is what "perpendicular bisector of $FD$" means). So the mirror image of the ray from $P$ towards $D$ is the ray from $P$ towards $F$: the reflected ray passes through the focus. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Reflective property of the ellipse">

A ray from one focus of an ellipse is reflected through the other focus.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let the ray from $F_1$ hit the ellipse at $P$, and let $t$ be the tangent at $P$. Because the ellipse is an oval curve (convex), every point $Q \ne P$ of $t$ lies outside it, so $QF_1 + QF_2 > 2a = PF_1 + PF_2$. Thus $P$ is the point of $t$ with the **smallest** total distance to $F_1$ and $F_2$.

Now use Heron's trick: reflect $F_2$ in $t$ to get $F_2'$. For every $Q$ on $t$, $QF_2 = QF_2'$, so $QF_1 + QF_2 = QF_1 + QF_2'$, which is smallest when $Q$ lies on the straight segment $F_1F_2'$. So $P$ is on that segment, and the angles that $PF_1$ and $PF_2'$ make with $t$ are vertically opposite, hence equal. By the symmetry of the reflection, the angle between $t$ and $PF_2'$ equals the angle between $t$ and $PF_2$. So $PF_1$ and $PF_2$ make equal angles with the tangent — which is exactly the law of reflection. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Note>

The hyperbola has a matching property: a ray aimed at one focus is reflected, from the outside of the near branch, towards the other focus. Large telescopes of the Cassegrain design combine a parabolic main mirror with a hyperbolic secondary mirror for this reason.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="classification" kind="proof" title="Second-degree equations">

Every conic in the plane has an equation of the form

$$
Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0,
$$

with $A$, $B$, $C$ not all zero. Conversely, which sets does such an equation describe?

<Theorem name="Classification of conics">

The set of real solutions of $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$ is one of: an ellipse (possibly a circle), a parabola, a hyperbola, or a degenerate case — a single point, one line, two parallel lines, two crossing lines, or the empty set. When the curve is non-degenerate, it is

- an **ellipse** if $B^2 - 4AC \lt 0$,
- a **parabola** if $B^2 - 4AC = 0$,
- a **hyperbola** if $B^2 - 4AC > 0$.

</Theorem>

*Proof sketch.* (1) Rotating the axes through an angle $\theta$ with $\cot 2\theta = (A - C)/B$ (or $\theta = 45^\circ$ if $A = C$) removes the $xy$ term; a computation shows that $B^2 - 4AC$ does not change under rotation. (2) Without the $xy$ term the discriminant is $-4AC$, and completing the square in $x$ and $y$ (or in just one of them, if $A$ or $C$ is zero) gives a standard form or one of the degenerate cases. The heart of the matter is the next pair of facts, which do not need any rotation. $\square$

<Theorem name="Lemma (definite and indefinite)">

1. If $A > 0$ and $B^2 - 4AC \lt 0$, then $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 > 0$ for every $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$.
2. If $B^2 - 4AC > 0$, then $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2$ takes both positive and negative values.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

1. Complete the square: $4A\left(Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2\right) = (2Ax + By)^2 + (4AC - B^2)\,y^2$. Both terms are non-negative, and they are both zero only if $y = 0$ and then $x = 0$.
2. If $A \ne 0$, the value at $(1, 0)$ is $A$, and the value at $(-B, 2A)$ is $AB^2 - 2AB^2 + 4A^2C = A(4AC - B^2)$, which has the opposite sign. If $A = 0$, then $B \ne 0$, and on the line $y = 1$ the value $Bx + C$ takes every real number. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

So when $B^2 - 4AC \lt 0$ the quadratic part grows in every direction, and the curve is bounded: an ellipse, a point or nothing. When $B^2 - 4AC > 0$ the quadratic part is zero along two directions — the asymptotes of a hyperbola, or the two lines of a degenerate case. The borderline case $B^2 - 4AC = 0$ gives a parabola (or, degenerately, two parallel lines, one line or nothing). For the eccentricity family above, $A = 1 - e^2$, $B = 0$, $C = 1$, so $B^2 - 4AC = 4(e^2 - 1)$: negative for $e \lt 1$, zero for $e = 1$, positive for $e > 1$, just as it should be.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A parabola is half of a hyperbola", or "a parabola eventually becomes straight".** A parabola has one piece and no asymptotes: its slope grows without bound, and the gap between $y = x^2/4$ and any line $y = mx + k$ tends to infinity. A hyperbola has two branches, each approaching two straight asymptotes.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Every second-degree equation describes a curve."** Some describe a single point ($x^2 + y^2 = 0$), a pair of lines ($x^2 - y^2 = 0$), one line ($(x - y)^2 = 0$), or nothing at all ($x^2 + y^2 + 1 = 0$). Apart from the empty set, these degenerate cases are also plane sections: of a cone through its tip, or (for parallel lines) of a cylinder.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The foci lie on the ellipse."** The foci are *inside* the ellipse, on the major axis, at distance $c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$ from the centre. For a hyperbola they are inside the two branches, at distance $c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ for every conic."** For an ellipse $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$, but for a hyperbola $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$. The foci of a hyperbola are further from the centre than its vertices.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Eccentricity measures size."** It measures *shape*: all circles have $e = 0$ and all parabolas $e = 1$, whatever their size. Scaling a conic does not change $e$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="One curve in the projective plane">

Add to the plane a "line at infinity", one point for each family of parallel lines; this is the **projective plane** (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.projective-geometry">projective geometry</TopicLink>). There, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas become a single kind of curve, differing only in how they meet the line at infinity: an ellipse misses it, a parabola touches it at one point (the direction of its axis), and a hyperbola crosses it at two points (the directions of its asymptotes). This is why a projection — a shadow cast from a point light — can turn one conic into another: the shadow of a circular lampshade on a wall can be a hyperbola. Pascal's theorem (1640) states that the three intersection points of opposite sides of any hexagon inscribed in a conic lie on a line, and it holds for all conics at once.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Orbits">

Kepler's first law (1609) says that planets move on ellipses with the Sun at one focus. Newton showed in the *Principia* (1687) that an inverse-square force of attraction produces exactly conic orbits: in polar coordinates centred at the Sun, the orbit is $r = \ell/(1 + e\cos\theta)$. Planets and periodic comets have $e \lt 1$; an object with exactly the escape energy would follow a parabola; faster ones, such as interstellar visitors and spacecraft on fly-bys, follow hyperbolas. The eccentricity is determined by the energy and angular momentum. See <TopicLink id="math.applied.classical-mechanics">classical mechanics</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Rational and integer points on conics">

Which points of a conic have rational coordinates? On the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, every line through $(-1, 0)$ with rational slope $t$ meets the circle again at the rational point $\left(\frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2}, \frac{2t}{1 + t^2}\right)$, and every rational point arises this way. Clearing denominators gives all Pythagorean triples, such as $(3, 4, 5)$ from $t = \tfrac12$. Integer points are subtler: the hyperbola $x^2 - 2y^2 = 1$ (a **Pell equation**) has infinitely many, $(3, 2), (17, 12), (99, 70), \ldots$, studied by Brahmagupta in the 7th century and solved in general by the *chakravala* method, described by Jayadeva and by Bhāskara II (11th–12th centuries). See <TopicLink id="math.number.elementary-number-theory">number theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Quadratic forms and symmetric matrices">

The quadratic part $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2$ is $\begin{pmatrix} x & y \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} A & B/2 \\ B/2 & C \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$. Its determinant is $AC - B^2/4 = -\tfrac14(B^2 - 4AC)$, so the discriminant is the determinant in disguise. Rotating the axes to remove the $xy$ term is **diagonalising** this symmetric matrix: its eigenvalues are the coefficients in the rotated equation, and an ellipse, parabola or hyperbola corresponds to eigenvalues of equal sign, a zero eigenvalue, or opposite signs. In three variables the same algebra classifies quadric surfaces: ellipsoids, paraboloids and hyperboloids. See <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">linear algebra</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Discovery.** According to later Greek sources, Menaechmus (4th century BCE) discovered the conic sections while trying to double the cube (to construct a length $x$ with $x^3 = 2$): the intersection of two parabolas, or of a parabola and a hyperbola, solves the problem.
- **Apollonius of Perga** (around 200 BCE) wrote the *Conics* in eight books, showing that all three curves arise from a single double cone and giving them their names *ellipse*, *parabola* and *hyperbola*. Diocles proved the focal property of the parabola in *On Burning Mirrors*, and Pappus of Alexandria (4th century CE) stated the focus–directrix property.
- **The Islamic world.** Ibn Sahl (around 984, Baghdad) designed lenses and burning mirrors with conic profiles and stated the law of refraction; Ibn al-Haytham studied parabolic mirrors. ʿUmar al-Khayyām (around 1070) solved every type of cubic equation geometrically by intersecting conics.
- **India.** Brahmagupta (7th century) and Bhāskara II (12th century) solved equations such as $x^2 - Ny^2 = 1$, whose solutions are the integer points on hyperbolas.
- **Europe.** Kepler introduced the word *focus* (Latin for "hearth") in 1604 and discovered elliptical orbits; René Descartes and Pierre de Fermat described conics by second-degree equations in the 1630s; Girard Desargues and Blaise Pascal began their projective study; Newton derived conic orbits from the law of gravitation (1687); Dandelin found his spheres in 1822.

</Section>
