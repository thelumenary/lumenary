<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="What survives suspension">

The homotopy groups of spheres $\pi_{n+k}(S^n)$ are among the most basic and most mysterious objects in topology. $\pi_3(S^2) \cong \Z$ is generated by the Hopf map; $\pi_6(S^3) \cong \Z/12$; $\pi_{10}(S^4) \cong \Z/24 \oplus \Z/3$. No formula is known, and none is expected.

But a pattern appears if you look along the diagonals. Suspending a map $f : S^{n+k} \to S^n$ — taking the double cone on both sides — gives a map $\Sigma f : S^{n+k+1} \to S^{n+1}$, and Hans Freudenthal proved in 1937 that once $n > k + 1$ this changes nothing: the groups $\pi_{n+k}(S^n)$ stabilise. The limits $\pi_k^s$, the **stable stems**, are still hard — they are known only in a finite range — but they are abelian, finite in positive degrees, and they carry a rich algebraic structure.

Stable phenomena turn out to be everywhere:

- **Generalised cohomology theories** — ordinary cohomology, topological K-theory, cobordism — are all represented by the objects of stable homotopy theory, **spectra** (Brown, 1962).
- **Manifolds.** Thom (1954) showed that classifying manifolds up to cobordism is a computation of stable homotopy groups of Thom spectra. Kervaire and Milnor (1963) counted exotic spheres using the stable stems: there are exactly 28 smooth structures on $S^7$, up to orientation-preserving diffeomorphism.
- **Algebra and number theory.** The stable stems are approached by spectral sequences built from the Steenrod algebra, and organised by **formal groups** — objects that came from number theory — through Quillen's theorem (1969) and chromatic homotopy theory. Bernoulli numbers appear in the answers.

This lesson is an orientation: suspension and spectra, cohomology theories, Thom's theorem with a complete proof of its easy half, the J-homomorphism, and a glimpse of the chromatic picture, with what is known and what is open.

</Section>

So $\pi_0^s = \Z$ (degree), $\pi_1^s = \Z/2$ (generated by the suspended Hopf map $\eta$), $\pi_2^s = \Z/2$ ($\eta^2$), $\pi_3^s = \Z/24$ ($\nu$, from the quaternionic Hopf map), $\pi_4^s = \pi_5^s = 0$, $\pi_6^s = \Z/2$ ($\nu^2$). Unstable phenomena change on suspension: the infinite cyclic summand of $\pi_7(S^4)$, generated by the quaternionic Hopf map, becomes torsion — the Hopf map suspends to the generator $\nu$ of $\pi_3^s = \Z/24$.

The guiding analogy: **stable homotopy theory is to homotopy theory what linear algebra is to geometry.** Suspension is invertible in the stable world, maps can be added, every map has a cofibre and the cofibre sequences are long exact both ways — fibre sequences and cofibre sequences coincide. Spectra are the "abelian groups" of homotopy theory: just as every abelian group is a set with extra structure that makes addition possible, a spectrum is a space with infinitely many deloopings. (This is an analogy, made precise by the theory of stable ∞-categories: see <TopicLink id="math.cat.stable-infinity-categories">stable ∞-categories</TopicLink>.)

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Suspension, spectra and cohomology theories">

All spaces are pointed CW complexes; $[X, Y]$ denotes pointed homotopy classes of maps.

<Definition term="suspension and stable homotopy groups">

The **reduced suspension** of $X$ is $\Sigma X = S^1 \wedge X$; for example $\Sigma S^n \cong S^{n+1}$. Suspension of maps gives homomorphisms $\pi_{n+k}(\Sigma^m X) \to \pi_{n+k+1}(\Sigma^{m+1}X)$, and the **stable homotopy groups** are $\pi_k^s(X) = \operatorname{colim}_n \pi_{n+k}(\Sigma^n X)$. The **stable stems** are $\pi_k^s = \pi_k^s(S^0) = \operatorname{colim}_n \pi_{n+k}(S^n)$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="spectrum">

A **spectrum** $E$ is a sequence of pointed spaces $E_0, E_1, E_2, \dots$ with structure maps $\Sigma E_n \to E_{n+1}$ (equivalently, adjoint maps $E_n \to \Omega E_{n+1}$). It is an **$\Omega$-spectrum** if the adjoint maps are weak equivalences. Its **homotopy groups** are $\pi_k(E) = \operatorname{colim}_n \pi_{n+k}(E_n)$, defined for all $k \in \Z$, including negative $k$. Examples: the **suspension spectrum** $\Sigma^\infty X = (X, \Sigma X, \Sigma^2 X, \dots)$, whose homotopy groups are $\pi_*^s(X)$; the **sphere spectrum** $\mathbb{S} = \Sigma^\infty S^0$; the **Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum** $HA = (K(A, n))_n$. Spectra form the **stable homotopy category**, in which $\Sigma$ is an equivalence (Boardman; in modern form the homotopy category of the stable ∞-category of spectra).

</Definition>

<Definition term="generalised cohomology theory">

A (reduced) **generalised cohomology theory** is a sequence of contravariant functors $\tilde E^n$ from pointed CW complexes to abelian groups, $n \in \Z$, with

1. **homotopy invariance**: homotopic maps induce the same homomorphism;
2. **exactness**: for a cofibre sequence $A \to X \to X/A$, the sequence $\tilde E^n(X/A) \to \tilde E^n(X) \to \tilde E^n(A)$ is exact;
3. **suspension**: natural isomorphisms $\tilde E^{n+1}(\Sigma X) \cong \tilde E^n(X)$;
4. **additivity**: $\tilde E^n(\bigvee_\alpha X_\alpha) \cong \prod_\alpha \tilde E^n(X_\alpha)$.

These are the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms **without the dimension axiom**: the **coefficients** $E^n = \tilde E^n(S^0)$ may be non-zero for $n \ne 0$. A spectrum $E$ defines one by $\tilde E^n(X) = [\Sigma^\infty X, \Sigma^n E]$, which for an $\Omega$-spectrum is $[X, E_n]$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="cobordism and Thom spectra">

Two closed $n$-manifolds $M, N$ are **cobordant** if $M \sqcup N$ is the boundary of a compact $(n+1)$-manifold. Cobordism classes form a group $\mathfrak{N}_n$ under disjoint union (every element has order 2, since $M \sqcup M = \partial(M \times [0,1])$), and $\mathfrak{N}_* = \bigoplus_n \mathfrak{N}_n$ is a ring under Cartesian product. The **Thom space** $\mathrm{Th}(V)$ of a vector bundle is the one-point compactification of its total space (for a compact base). The **Thom spectrum** $MO$ has spaces $MO(k) = \mathrm{Th}(\gamma_k)$, the Thom spaces of the universal $k$-plane bundles over $BO(k)$; similarly $MSO$ (oriented), $MU$ (complex) and $\mathbb{S} \simeq M\mathrm{fr}$ (framed).

</Definition>

<Definition term="formal group law and height">

A (one-dimensional, commutative) **formal group law** over a commutative ring $R$ is a power series $F(x, y) \in R[[x, y]]$ with $F(x, 0) = x$, $F(x, y) = F(y, x)$ and $F(F(x, y), z) = F(x, F(y, z))$. Examples: the **additive** law $x + y$ and the **multiplicative** law $x + y + xy$. Its **$n$-series** is $[n]_F(x) = F(x, [n-1]_F(x))$, $[0]_F(x) = 0$. Over a field of characteristic $p$, either $[p]_F(x) = 0$ (**height** $\infty$) or $[p]_F(x) = u\,x^{p^h} + (\text{higher terms})$ with $u \ne 0$, and $h \ge 1$ is the **height**.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Examples: cohomology theories that are not ordinary cohomology">

<Example title="Ordinary cohomology">

$HA^n(X) = \tilde H^n(X; A)$, with coefficients $HA^n(S^0) = A$ for $n = 0$ and $0$ otherwise: the dimension axiom holds. Its formal group law (for $A = \Z$, via the first Chern class of complex line bundles, $c_1(L \otimes M) = c_1(L) + c_1(M)$) is additive.

</Example>

<Example title="Complex K-theory">

For a compact space $X$, $KU^0(X)$ is the Grothendieck group of complex vector bundles on $X$ (Atiyah–Hirzebruch, 1961). **Bott periodicity** (1959), $\Omega^2(\Z \times BU) \simeq \Z \times BU$, makes it 2-periodic: $KU^{n+2}(X) \cong KU^n(X)$, and the coefficients are $\pi_*(KU) = \Z[\beta^{\pm 1}]$ with $|\beta| = 2$. So $KU^{n}(\text{point}) \cong \Z$ for every even $n$: **K-theory is not ordinary cohomology with any coefficients**, since those vanish on a point outside degree $0$. For line bundles, with $c_1(L) = 1 - L^{-1}$ (up to the Bott class), $c_1(L \otimes M) = c_1(L) + c_1(M) - c_1(L)c_1(M)$: a multiplicative formal group law. Real K-theory $KO$ is 8-periodic, with $\pi_0, \dots, \pi_7 = \Z, \Z/2, \Z/2, 0, \Z, 0, 0, 0$.

</Example>

<Example title="Cobordism">

Unoriented cobordism $MO$ has coefficients $\pi_*(MO) = \mathfrak{N}_*$, non-zero in degrees $0, 2, 4, 5, 6, \dots$; complex cobordism has $\pi_*(MU) = \Z[x_1, x_2, \dots]$, $|x_i| = 2i$ (Milnor, Novikov, 1960). By Quillen's theorem (1969) the formal group law of $MU$ is the **universal** formal group law, and $\pi_*(MU)$ is the Lazard ring that classifies formal group laws. The sphere spectrum is framed cobordism: $\pi_n^s \cong \Omega_n^{\mathrm{fr}}$, the group of closed $n$-manifolds with a trivialisation of the stable normal bundle, up to framed cobordism (Pontryagin).

</Example>

<Example title="Stable cohomotopy and the Hopf invariant">

The sphere spectrum gives stable cohomotopy $\pi^n_s(X) = [\Sigma^\infty X, \Sigma^n \mathbb{S}]$, whose coefficients are the stable stems. The Hopf map $\eta : S^3 \to S^2$ has Hopf invariant 1: the preimages of two points are circles that link once. Its stable class generates $\pi_1^s = \Z/2$ — stably, $2\eta = 0$.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Central theorems">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (Freudenthal suspension theorem, 1937)">

If $X$ is an $(n-1)$-connected CW complex, the suspension homomorphism $\pi_q(X) \to \pi_{q+1}(\Sigma X)$ is an isomorphism for $q < 2n - 1$ and surjective for $q = 2n - 1$. In particular $\pi_{n+k}(S^n) \to \pi_{n+k+1}(S^{n+1})$ is an isomorphism for $n > k + 1$.

</Theorem>

*Proof sketch.* Write $\Sigma X$ as the union of two cones $C_+X \cup C_-X$ meeting in $X$. The suspension map is the composite $\pi_q(X) \cong \pi_{q+1}(C_+X, X) \to \pi_{q+1}(\Sigma X, C_-X) \cong \pi_{q+1}(\Sigma X)$, and the middle map is an isomorphism in the stated range by the **homotopy excision theorem** (Blakers–Massey), since $(C_\pm X, X)$ are $n$-connected. See Hatcher, *Algebraic Topology*, Corollary 4.24; a different proof uses the James construction $J(X) \simeq \Omega\Sigma X$. For $n > k + 1$ we have $n + k < 2n - 1$, which gives the second statement.

<Theorem name="Proposition 2 (structure of the stable stems)">

1. Every stable homotopy group $\pi_k^s(X)$ is abelian.
2. $\pi_0^s \cong \Z$, by degree.
3. (Serre, 1953.) $\pi_k^s$ is finite for every $k > 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

(1) $\pi_k^s(X)$ is the colimit of the groups $\pi_{n+k}(\Sigma^n X)$, which are abelian once $n + k \ge 2$ by the Eckmann–Hilton argument (formalised below); a colimit of abelian groups along homomorphisms is abelian. (2) $\pi_n(S^n) \cong \Z$ by Hopf's degree theorem, and suspension preserves degree. (3) Serre's theorem uses his spectral sequence and his theory of classes of abelian groups to show that rationally $\pi_{n+k}(S^n) \otimes \Q = 0$ for $n$ odd and $k > 0$; since stable groups are computed with $n$ odd and large, and are finitely generated, they are finite. A proof is in Hatcher's *Spectral Sequences*, or Mosher–Tangora.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (Brown representability, 1962)">

Every reduced generalised cohomology theory $\tilde E^*$ on pointed CW complexes is represented by an $\Omega$-spectrum $E$: $\tilde E^n(X) \cong [X, E_n]$, naturally in $X$.

</Theorem>

The proof builds $E_n$ cell by cell so that $[-, E_n]$ agrees with $\tilde E^n$ on spheres and then on all complexes, using the Mayer–Vietoris property (from exactness) and additivity; the suspension isomorphism supplies the structure maps. Categorically, it is a Yoneda-type statement: a contravariant functor to sets that turns homotopy colimits into limits is representable.

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (Pontryagin 1947: Stiefel–Whitney numbers are cobordism invariants)">

If a closed $n$-manifold $M$ is the boundary of a compact $(n+1)$-manifold $W$, then every Stiefel–Whitney number $w_I[M] = \langle w_{i_1}(M) \cdots w_{i_r}(M), [M] \rangle \in \mathbb{F}_2$, $i_1 + \dots + i_r = n$, vanishes.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $i : M \to W$ be the inclusion. A collar of $M$ in $W$ gives $TW|_M \cong TM \oplus \underline{\R}$, so by the Whitney sum formula $w(M) = i^* w(W)$, and hence $w_I(M) = i^* w_I(W)$. In the long exact sequence of the pair $(W, M)$ with $\mathbb{F}_2$ coefficients, the connecting map $\partial : H_{n+1}(W, M) \to H_n(M)$ sends the fundamental class $[W, M]$ to $[M]$. Therefore
$$
w_I[M] = \langle i^* w_I(W), \partial [W, M] \rangle = \langle \delta\, i^* w_I(W), [W, M] \rangle = 0,
$$
because $\delta \circ i^* = 0$ (consecutive maps in the long exact sequence of the pair in cohomology).

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

The whole proof is Stokes' theorem in mod-$2$ disguise. A characteristic number of $M$ is a class restricted from $W$ and evaluated on the boundary $\partial[W, M]$; that is the same as evaluating $\delta\, i^*$ of the class on $[W, M]$, and $\delta \circ i^* = 0$. The only geometry is the collar, which makes the characteristic classes of $M$ restrictions of those of $W$.

</Narrator>

<Theorem name="Corollary 5 (which projective spaces bound)">

$\R P^n$ is the boundary of a compact manifold if and only if $n$ is odd.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The total Stiefel–Whitney class of $\R P^n$ is $w(\R P^n) = (1 + a)^{n+1}$, $a \in H^1(\R P^n; \mathbb{F}_2)$ the generator. If $n = 2m$ is even, $w_n(\R P^n) = \binom{2m+1}{2m} a^n = (2m+1)a^n = a^n$, so $w_n[\R P^n] = 1 \ne 0$ and $\R P^n$ does not bound, by Theorem 4. If $n = 2m - 1$ is odd, the antipodal map acts on each circle fibre of the Hopf fibration $S^{2m-1} \to \C P^{m-1}$ as a half turn, so $\R P^{2m-1} = S^{2m-1}/\{\pm 1\}$ is again a circle bundle over $\C P^{m-1}$ — the unit circle bundle of a complex line bundle — and it is the boundary of the associated disc bundle. (Consistently, the binomial coefficients $\binom{2m}{i}$ are even for odd $i$, so all its Stiefel–Whitney numbers vanish.) Both parities are formalised below.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 6 (Thom 1954)">

1. **Pontryagin–Thom.** $\mathfrak{N}_n \cong \pi_n(MO) = \operatorname{colim}_k \pi_{n+k}(\mathrm{Th}(\gamma_k))$; similarly $\Omega^{SO}_n \cong \pi_n(MSO)$ and $\Omega_n^{\mathrm{fr}} \cong \pi_n^s$.
2. $\mathfrak{N}_* \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x_i : i \ge 2,\ i \ne 2^k - 1]$, with $x_{2j} = [\R P^{2j}]$ (odd generators are Dold manifolds).
3. Two closed manifolds are cobordant if and only if they have the same Stiefel–Whitney numbers (the converse of Theorem 4).

</Theorem>

*Proof sketch of (1).* Embed $M^n \subset S^{n+k}$ with normal bundle $\nu$ and tubular neighbourhood $N \cong \nu$. Collapse everything outside $N$ to the base point: $S^{n+k} \to N/\partial N \cong \mathrm{Th}(\nu)$. Compose with the Thom map of the classifying map $\nu \to \gamma_k$: this gives an element of $\pi_{n+k}(MO(k))$. Conversely, a map $S^{n+k} \to MO(k)$ can be made transverse to the zero section $BO(k) \subset MO(k)$, and the preimage is a closed $n$-manifold. Cobordisms correspond to homotopies (apply the same constructions to $S^{n+k} \times [0, 1]$). Statement (2) is a computation: Thom determined the homotopy type of $MO$ as a product of Eilenberg–MacLane spectra $H\mathbb{F}_2$ using the Steenrod algebra. See Milnor–Stasheff, *Characteristic Classes*, Chapters 17–18. The experiment below compares (2) with Stiefel–Whitney numbers of products of projective spaces.

<Theorem name="Theorem 7 (Bott periodicity, 1959)">

$\Omega^2 BU \simeq \Z \times BU$ and $\Omega^8 BO \simeq \Z \times BO$. Hence $\pi_i(U) \cong \pi_{i+2}(U)$ and $\pi_i(O) \cong \pi_{i+8}(O)$, and complex and real K-theory are represented by $\Omega$-spectra of periods 2 and 8.

</Theorem>

<Theorem name="Theorem 8 (the J-homomorphism; Whitehead, Adams, Quillen, Sullivan, Becker–Gottlieb)">

An element of $\pi_r(\mathrm{SO}(n))$, a map $S^r \to \mathrm{SO}(n)$, defines a map $S^r \times S^{n-1} \to S^{n-1}$ and, by the Hopf construction, an element of $\pi_{r+n}(S^n)$; stably this is the **J-homomorphism** $J : \pi_r(\mathrm{SO}) \to \pi_r^s$. For $r = 4k - 1$, $\pi_r(\mathrm{SO}) \cong \Z$ by Bott periodicity, and the image of $J$ is a cyclic direct summand of $\pi_{4k-1}^s$ of order
$$
m(2k) = \text{denominator of } \frac{B_{2k}}{4k},
$$
where $B_{2k}$ is the Bernoulli number ($B_2 = \tfrac16$, $B_4 = -\tfrac{1}{30}$, $B_6 = \tfrac{1}{42}$, $B_8 = -\tfrac{1}{30}$). So $|\operatorname{im} J| = 24, 240, 504, 480$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, 4$; and indeed $\pi_3^s = \Z/24$, $\pi_7^s = \Z/240$, $\pi_{11}^s = \Z/504$, $\pi_{15}^s = \Z/480 \oplus \Z/2$.

</Theorem>

Adams (1965–66) proved that the image has order $m(2k)$ or $2m(2k)$ and that it is exactly $m(2k)$ if the **Adams conjecture** holds; the conjecture was proved by Quillen (1971), Sullivan (1974) and Becker–Gottlieb (1975). Equivalently, $m(2k)$ is the denominator of $\zeta(1 - 2k)/2$, since $\zeta(1-2k) = -B_{2k}/2k$. The lower bound comes from the **e-invariant**, defined using K-theory and Adams operations.

<Theorem name="Theorem 9 (Kervaire–Milnor 1963), statement">

For $n \ge 5$ the group $\Theta_n$ of homotopy $n$-spheres up to diffeomorphism (under connected sum) is finite, with a subgroup $bP_{n+1}$ of spheres that bound parallelisable manifolds, and an injection $\Theta_n / bP_{n+1} \hookrightarrow \pi_n^s / \operatorname{im} J$. For $k \ge 2$, $|bP_{4k}| = 2^{2k-2}(2^{2k-1} - 1) \cdot \mathrm{numerator}(4|B_{2k}|/k)$. Since $\pi_7^s = \operatorname{im} J$, $\Theta_7 = bP_8 \cong \Z/28$.

</Theorem>

<Theorem name="Theorem 10 (Adams spectral sequence, 1958), statement">

For a finite spectrum $X$ there is a spectral sequence
$$
E_2^{s,t} = \operatorname{Ext}^{s,t}_{\mathcal{A}}\big(H^*(X; \mathbb{F}_p), \mathbb{F}_p\big) \Longrightarrow \pi_{t-s}(X)^\wedge_p,
$$
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the mod $p$ Steenrod algebra, converging to the $p$-completed stable homotopy groups. Adams used it (with secondary operations) to prove the **Hopf invariant one theorem** (1960): a map $S^{2n-1} \to S^n$ of Hopf invariant one exists only for $n = 2, 4, 8$. Consequences: $\R^n$ is a real division algebra only for $n = 1, 2, 4, 8$, and $S^{n-1}$ is parallelisable only for $n = 1, 2, 4, 8$.

</Theorem>

<Theorem name="Theorem 11 (heights of the basic formal group laws)">

Over $\mathbb{F}_p$ the multiplicative formal group law has $[p](x) = x^p$, so height 1; the additive law has $[p](x) = 0$, so height $\infty$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For the multiplicative law, $1 + F(x, y) = (1 + x)(1 + y)$, so by induction $1 + [n](x) = (1 + x)^n$. In characteristic $p$, $(1 + x)^p = 1 + x^p$ because the binomial coefficients $\binom{p}{i}$, $0 < i < p$, are divisible by $p$. Hence $[p](x) = x^p$. For the additive law $[n](x) = nx$, and $px = 0$ in characteristic $p$. (Both are formalised below.)

</Proof>

**Chromatic homotopy theory**, in outline. By Quillen's theorem a complex-oriented cohomology theory has a formal group law, and the moduli of formal groups — stratified by height — organises the stable homotopy category. Ordinary rational cohomology sees height 0, K-theory height 1, elliptic cohomology and topological modular forms heights $\le 2$, and the **Morava K-theories** $K(n)$ isolate height $n$. Two deep theorems make this rigorous: the **nilpotence theorem** (Devinatz–Hopkins–Smith, 1988), which says $MU_*$ detects nilpotence of self-maps of finite spectra, and the **periodicity theorem** (Hopkins–Smith, 1998), which produces $v_n$-periodic self-maps. The stable stems are then built, prime by prime, from layers of height $0, 1, 2, \dots$; the image of J is (essentially) the height-1 layer.

</Section>

<Section id="landscape" kind="human" title="The landscape: what is known and what is open">

**Established.** The theorems above; the chromatic convergence and thick subcategory theorems (Hopkins–Ravenel, Hopkins–Smith); the construction of topological modular forms (Goerss, Hopkins, Miller and others) and its use to detect large parts of the stable stems; the solution of the **Kervaire invariant one problem** outside dimension 126 by Hill, Hopkins and Ravenel (2016): elements of Kervaire invariant one exist only in dimensions $2, 6, 14, 30, 62$ and possibly $126$. Computations of the stable stems at the prime 2, using the Adams spectral sequence, motivic homotopy theory and machine computation, now reach dimension 90 (Isaksen, Wang and Xu, 2023); among their consequences, Wang and Xu (2017) showed $\pi_{61}^s = 0$, which implies that $S^{61}$ has a unique smooth structure — the only odd-dimensional spheres with this property are $S^1, S^3, S^5$ and $S^{61}$.

**Recent and announced.** A 2023 preprint by Burklund, Hahn, Levy and Schlank disproves Ravenel's **telescope conjecture** at all heights $\ge 2$; a 2024 preprint by Lin, Wang and Xu constructs Kervaire invariant one elements in dimension 126. These are major announcements whose refereeing and absorption are under way.

**Open problems.**

- The stable stems in general: no description of $\pi_k^s$ for all $k$ is known or expected; even the pattern of their orders is mysterious beyond the chromatic layers.
- Hopkins' **chromatic splitting conjecture**, describing how the $K(n)$-local spheres fit together, is open in general (known at low heights).
- Which smooth manifolds admit exotic structures in a given dimension, and the size of $\Theta_n$ in general, depend on the unknown parts of $\pi_n^s / \operatorname{im} J$.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The stable homotopy groups of spheres are known."** They are known only in a finite range — through dimension 90 at the prime 2, and in smaller or comparable ranges at odd primes — and the computations need heavy machinery. The chromatic picture explains large structured parts (the image of J, the families detected by topological modular forms), but there is no formula for $\pi_k^s$, and none is expected.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A generalised cohomology theory is just cohomology with exotic coefficients."** Ordinary cohomology with coefficients in $A$ vanishes on a point outside degree 0. K-theory has $KU^{-2}(\mathrm{pt}) \cong \Z$, and cobordism has $\mathfrak{N}_2 = \pi_2(MO) \cong \mathbb{F}_2$ (generated by $\R P^2$): they violate the dimension axiom, so they are not $H^*(-; A)$ for any $A$. They are not even determined by their coefficients: the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence from ordinary cohomology to K-theory has non-trivial differentials in general.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Stable means the unstable groups do not matter."** Stabilisation forgets a lot: $\pi_3(S^2) \cong \Z$ becomes $\pi_1^s \cong \Z/2$, and $\pi_7(S^4) \cong \Z \oplus \Z/12$ becomes $\pi_3^s \cong \Z/24$. Unstable homotopy theory — the EHP sequence, $v_n$-periodic unstable families — is a subject of its own.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A spectrum is just a sequence of spaces."** The structure maps are essential, and so is the right notion of morphism. Making the smash product of spectra associative and commutative on the nose required new foundations in the 1990s (symmetric spectra, orthogonal spectra, $S$-modules), now subsumed by the stable ∞-category of spectra.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Cohomology theories as the shadows of spectra">

For a topologist the main point is representability: every generalised cohomology theory is $[-, E]$ for a spectrum $E$ (Brown), so operations between theories are maps between spectra, and long exact sequences come from cofibre sequences. Homotopy invariants of manifolds become stable homotopy: characteristic numbers through Thom spectra, framings through the sphere spectrum, exotic spheres through $\pi_*^s / \operatorname{im} J$. The prerequisites are <TopicLink id="math.geom.homotopy-theory">homotopy theory</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.geom.vector-bundles-characteristic-classes">vector bundles and characteristic classes</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Cobordism and smooth structures">

For a geometric topologist, stable homotopy theory is the algebra of manifolds. The Pontryagin–Thom construction identifies framed manifolds up to framed cobordism with the stable stems, and Kervaire–Milnor convert the stems into counts of exotic spheres — 28 on $S^7$, 992 bounding parallelisable manifolds in dimension 11, and a unique smooth structure on $S^{61}$. In dimension 4, the 10/8 theorem on intersection forms of spin 4-manifolds was sharpened using equivariant stable homotopy theory (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.low-dimensional-topology">low-dimensional topology</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="The Steenrod algebra and the Lazard ring">

Algebraically, the stable stems are approximated by homological algebra over Hopf algebras. The Adams spectral sequence starts from $\operatorname{Ext}$ over the Steenrod algebra $\mathcal{A}$ — computed by minimal resolutions, now by computer to high degrees; the Adams–Novikov spectral sequence starts from $\operatorname{Ext}$ over $MU_*MU$, the Hopf algebroid of coordinate changes of formal group laws. Quillen's theorem identifies $MU_*$ with the Lazard ring $L$, which Lazard (1955) had shown to be a polynomial ring classifying formal group laws: topology rediscovered an algebraic object. See <TopicLink id="math.algebra.homological-algebra">homological algebra</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Bernoulli numbers, zeta values and formal groups">

Number theory appears twice. First, through special values: the image of J in $\pi_{4k-1}^s$ has order the denominator of $\zeta(1-2k)/2$ (for $k = 1$: $\zeta(-1) = -\tfrac{1}{12}$, order 24), and the same denominators govern the torsion in the algebraic K-theory of $\Z$ (for example $K_3(\Z) \cong \Z/48$, Lee–Szczarba). Second, through formal groups: a formal group of height $n$ over $\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p$ is unique up to isomorphism (Lazard), its Lubin–Tate deformation theory gives the **Morava E-theories**, and its automorphism group, the Morava stabiliser group, is the group of units in the maximal order of the division algebra over $\Q_p$ of invariant $1/n$. Chromatic homotopy theory is, in part, the cohomology of these $p$-adic groups. See <TopicLink id="math.number.arithmetic-geometry">arithmetic geometry</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="K-theory charges and topological phases">

In string theory, conserved charges of D-branes take values in K-theory rather than in cohomology (Minasian–Moore 1997, Witten 1998). In condensed matter physics, Kitaev's "periodic table" (2009) classifies topological insulators and superconductors by real and complex K-theory, and its eight-fold pattern is Bott periodicity of $KO$. The Witten genus and the elliptic genera of quantum field theory led to elliptic cohomology and topological modular forms. These physical derivations are heuristic; the classifications they predict are, where proved, theorems of K-theory.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="categories" title="Spectra as a stable ∞-category">

Categorically, spectra are the stabilisation of spaces: the universal stable ∞-category with a functor from spaces, $\mathrm{Sp} = \lim(\cdots \xrightarrow{\Omega} \mathcal{S}_* \xrightarrow{\Omega} \mathcal{S}_*)$. The stable homotopy category is its homotopy category, which is triangulated; Brown representability is a statement about this category; and the smash product makes $\mathrm{Sp}$ symmetric monoidal, so that ring spectra ($\mathbb{E}_\infty$-rings such as $KU$, $MU$, $\mathrm{tmf}$) can be studied like commutative rings — "brave new algebra". See <TopicLink id="math.cat.stable-infinity-categories">stable ∞-categories</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.cat.triangulated-categories">triangulated categories</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **1930s–1950s: the first stable phenomena.** Heinz Hopf (Germany–Switzerland) found the Hopf map and its invariant (1931); Hans Freudenthal (Germany–Netherlands) proved the suspension theorem (1937); Lev Pontryagin (USSR) related framed manifolds to homotopy groups of spheres; Jean-Pierre Serre (France) introduced spectral sequences into homotopy theory and proved finiteness (1951–53); René Thom (France) computed cobordism (1954); Hirosi Toda (Japan) computed homotopy groups of spheres far into the unstable range (1962).
- **1958–1963: spectra and cohomology theories.** Elon Lages Lima (Brazil), in his Chicago thesis (1958) under Edwin Spanier, introduced spectra; George Whitehead showed they define homology theories (1962) and Edgar Brown proved representability (1962); Raoul Bott (Hungary–USA) proved periodicity (1959); Michael Atiyah and Friedrich Hirzebruch (UK, Germany) created topological K-theory; Frank Adams (UK) built his spectral sequence (1958) and solved the Hopf invariant one problem (1960); Michel Kervaire and John Milnor (Switzerland, USA) counted exotic spheres (1963).
- **1960s–1980s: complex cobordism and formal groups.** John Milnor and Sergei Novikov (USSR) computed $\pi_*(MU)$ (1960); Novikov introduced the Adams–Novikov spectral sequence (1967); Daniel Quillen (USA) connected complex cobordism and formal group laws (1969); Jack Morava (USA) introduced Morava K-theories and the chromatic viewpoint; Douglas Ravenel (USA) formulated his conjectures (1984), most of which were proved by Ethan Devinatz, Michael Hopkins and Jeffrey Smith (1988, 1998).
- **1990s–present.** Michael Boardman's stable homotopy category (1960s) received new foundations (symmetric and orthogonal spectra, $S$-modules) and was absorbed into ∞-categories by Jacob Lurie; Hopkins, Haynes Miller, Paul Goerss and others constructed topological modular forms; Michael Hill, Hopkins and Ravenel solved most of the Kervaire invariant problem (2009 announcement, 2016); Daniel Isaksen, Guozhen Wang and Zhouli Xu (USA, China) pushed the computation of the stems to dimension 90 with motivic and machine methods.

</Section>
