| points | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="What a proof is">

A **proof** is a finite chain of steps leading from accepted starting points — definitions, axioms and theorems already proved — to the statement we want. Each step must be justified: anyone who checks it should agree that it follows. A proof is not a feeling of certainty, and not a pile of examples; it is an argument that would convince a careful sceptic.

Most statements we prove have the shape "if $P$ then $Q$", possibly "for every $n$". The methods of this lesson are four ways of organising the chain, each resting on a law of logic from <TopicLink id="math.found.propositional-logic">propositional logic</TopicLink>:

| Method | To prove | We assume | and derive | Logic behind it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| direct | $P \to Q$ | $P$ | $Q$ | the meaning of $\to$ |
| cases | $P \to Q$, when $P$ means "$A$ or $B$" | $A$, then separately $B$ | $Q$ both times | $(A \to Q) \wedge (B \to Q) \equiv (A \vee B) \to Q$ |
| contrapositive | $P \to Q$ | $\neg Q$ | $\neg P$ | $P \to Q \equiv \neg Q \to \neg P$ |
| contradiction | $S$ | $\neg S$ | something impossible | $(\neg S \to \bot) \to S$ is a tautology |

The fourth row uses $\bot$ for "a contradiction", a statement that is certainly false, such as "$1 = 0$" or "$q$ and not $q$". The fifth tool, **induction**, is different: it is a property of the natural numbers themselves, and it is how we prove infinitely many statements $P(0), P(1), P(2), \dots$ in one go.

<Narrator who="chloe">

A good habit before any proof: write down exactly what you are allowed to assume and exactly what you must reach. Half of all mistakes are proofs of a different statement.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions we will use">

<Definition term="even, odd, divides">

An integer $n$ is **even** if $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$, and **odd** if $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$. For integers $a$ and $b$, **$a$ divides $b$**, written $a \mid b$, if $b = ak$ for some integer $k$.

</Definition>

We use one fact about integers, which follows from division with remainder: **every integer is either even or odd, and not both.** (Dividing $n$ by $2$ leaves remainder $0$ or $1$, and the remainder is unique.)

<Definition term="prime">

A **prime** is a natural number $p > 1$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$. Every natural number $n > 1$ has a smallest divisor $d > 1$, and that $d$ is prime (a divisor of $d$ bigger than $1$ would be a smaller divisor of $n$).

</Definition>

<Definition term="induction">

**Ordinary induction.** Let $P(n)$ be a statement about natural numbers $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ If

1. $P(0)$ is true (the **base case**), and
2. for every $n$, $P(n)$ implies $P(n + 1)$ (the **inductive step**),

then $P(n)$ is true for every natural number $n$. (To start at another number $n_0$, take $n_0$ as the base case.)

**Strong induction.** If, for every $n$, the truth of $P(k)$ for all $k < n$ implies $P(n)$, then $P(n)$ is true for every $n$.

**The well-ordering principle.** Every non-empty set of natural numbers has a least element.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="direct" kind="proof" title="Direct proof and proof by cases">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1">

The sum of two odd integers is even.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $m$ and $n$ be odd. By definition $m = 2a + 1$ and $n = 2b + 1$ for some integers $a, b$. Then

$$
m + n = 2a + 2b + 2 = 2(a + b + 1),
$$

and $a + b + 1$ is an integer, so $m + n$ is even.

</Proof>

Notice the shape: unpack the definitions of the hypothesis, compute, and repack into the definition of the conclusion. Most direct proofs look like this.

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (divisibility is transitive)">

For integers $a, b, c$: if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Since $a \mid b$, there is an integer $k$ with $b = ak$; since $b \mid c$, there is an integer $l$ with $c = bl$. Then $c = (ak)l = a(kl)$, and $kl$ is an integer, so $a \mid c$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (proof by cases)">

For every integer $n$, the number $n(n + 1)$ is even.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Every integer is even or odd, so it is enough to treat the two cases.

*Case 1: $n$ is even*, say $n = 2k$. Then $n(n + 1) = 2\,k(n + 1)$, which is even.

*Case 2: $n$ is odd*, say $n = 2k + 1$. Then $n + 1 = 2(k + 1)$ and $n(n + 1) = 2\,n(k + 1)$, which is even.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="contrapositive" kind="proof" title="Proof by contrapositive">

<Theorem name="Theorem 4">

Let $n$ be an integer. If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.

</Theorem>

A direct attempt stalls: from $n^2 = 2k$ we would want to "take square roots", and $\sqrt{2k}$ tells us nothing useful. The contrapositive is easy.

<Proof>

We prove the contrapositive: *if $n$ is not even, then $n^2$ is not even.* If $n$ is not even, it is odd, $n = 2k + 1$. Then

$$
n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1,
$$

which is odd, hence not even. Since a statement is equivalent to its contrapositive, the theorem is proved.

</Proof>

<Note>

Be careful to prove the contrapositive $\neg Q \to \neg P$ and not the converse $Q \to P$. "If $n$ is even then $n^2$ is even" is true and easy — but it is a different theorem.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="contradiction" kind="proof" title="Proof by contradiction">

To prove $S$ by contradiction, assume $\neg S$ and reason until you reach something impossible. Since true assumptions cannot lead to a false conclusion by valid steps, the assumption $\neg S$ must be false — so $S$ is true.

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (Euclid)">

There are infinitely many prime numbers.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose, for a contradiction, that there are only finitely many primes, and list them all: $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k$. Let

$$
N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1 .
$$

Then $N > 1$, so $N$ has a smallest divisor $q > 1$, and $q$ is prime. Since our list contains every prime, $q = p_i$ for some $i$. But then $q$ divides the product $p_1 \cdots p_k$ and also $N$, so it divides their difference $N - p_1 \cdots p_k = 1$. No prime divides $1$. This contradiction shows that the assumption was false: there are infinitely many primes.

</Proof>

<Note>

The proof shows more: **any** finite list of primes misses the prime $q$. Euclid himself (*Elements*, Book IX, Proposition 20) argued this way — "prime numbers are more than any assigned multitude of primes" — so the contradiction is optional. Notice also what the proof does *not* say: $N$ itself need not be prime. For the first six primes, $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 + 1 = 30031 = 59 \cdot 509$.

</Note>

<Theorem name="Theorem 6">

$\sqrt{2}$ is irrational: there are no integers $a, b$ with $b \neq 0$ and $(a/b)^2 = 2$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose there were. Cancelling common factors, we may assume $a$ and $b$ are not both even. From $a^2 = 2b^2$, $a^2$ is even, so $a$ is even by Theorem 4: $a = 2c$. Then $4c^2 = 2b^2$, so $b^2 = 2c^2$ is even, and $b$ is even by Theorem 4 again. Now $a$ and $b$ are both even — a contradiction.

</Proof>

This classic chains two methods: a contradiction whose key step is the contrapositive Theorem 4. More on irrational numbers in <TopicLink id="math.number.real-numbers">real numbers</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="induction" kind="proof" title="Induction">

Picture an infinite row of dominoes. If the first falls, and each falling domino knocks over the next, then all of them fall. The base case pushes the first domino; the inductive step guarantees the chain reaction.

<Theorem name="Theorem 7 (Gauss's formula)">

For every natural number $n$: $\;0 + 1 + 2 + \dots + n = \dfrac{n(n + 1)}{2}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $P(n)$ be the statement $0 + 1 + \dots + n = n(n+1)/2$.

*Base case.* $P(0)$ says $0 = 0 \cdot 1/2$, which is true.

*Inductive step.* Let $n$ be a natural number and assume $P(n)$ (the **induction hypothesis**). Then

$$
0 + 1 + \dots + n + (n + 1) = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2} + (n + 1) = \frac{(n + 1)(n + 2)}{2},
$$

which is $P(n + 1)$. By induction, $P(n)$ holds for every $n$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 8">

For every natural number $n$: $\;1 + 3 + 5 + \dots + (2n - 1) = n^2$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Base case* $n = 0$: the empty sum is $0 = 0^2$. *Inductive step:* if the first $n$ odd numbers add up to $n^2$, then the first $n + 1$ add up to $n^2 + (2n + 1) = (n + 1)^2$.

</Proof>

**Why the base case matters.** Consider the claim "$n^2 + n + 1$ is even for every $n$". The inductive step works perfectly: $(n+1)^2 + (n+1) + 1 = (n^2 + n + 1) + 2(n + 1)$, so if $n^2 + n + 1$ were even, the next value would be even too. But the base case fails — $0^2 + 0 + 1 = 1$ is odd — and in fact, by Theorem 3, $n^2 + n = n(n+1)$ is always even, so $n^2 + n + 1$ is **never** even. The dominoes are lined up beautifully; nobody pushed the first one.

**Strong induction** lets the inductive step use *all* earlier cases, not just the previous one.

<Theorem name="Theorem 9 (stamps)">

With stamps worth $3$ and $5$ (in any currency), every amount of at least $8$ can be made exactly.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $P(n)$ be "$n = 3a + 5b$ for some natural numbers $a, b$". We prove $P(n)$ for every $n \ge 8$ by strong induction. The amounts $8 = 3 + 5$, $9 = 3 + 3 + 3$ and $10 = 5 + 5$ can be made. Now let $n \ge 11$ and assume $P(k)$ for every $k$ with $8 \le k < n$. Since $n - 3 \ge 8$, the amount $n - 3$ can be made, $n - 3 = 3a + 5b$; adding one more $3$ gives $n = 3(a + 1) + 5b$.

</Proof>

The step reaches back three places, which is why we needed three starting cases. Strong induction also proves that every natural number $n > 1$ is a product of primes: either $n$ is prime, or $n = ab$ with $1 < a, b < n$, and $a$ and $b$ are products of primes by the induction hypothesis — the existence half of the <TopicLink id="math.number.divisibility-gcd">fundamental theorem of arithmetic</TopicLink>.

<Theorem name="Theorem 10 (three equivalent principles)">

Given the basic arithmetic of $\mathbb{N}$, ordinary induction, strong induction and the well-ordering principle imply one another.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Ordinary ⇒ strong.* Assume the hypothesis of strong induction for $P$. Let $Q(n)$ be "$P(k)$ holds for every $k < n$". $Q(0)$ is true, since there is no $k < 0$. If $Q(n)$ holds, then $P(n)$ holds by the hypothesis of strong induction, so $Q(n + 1)$ holds. By ordinary induction $Q(n)$ holds for every $n$; and $P(n)$ follows from $Q(n + 1)$.

*Strong ⇒ well-ordering.* Let $S$ be a set of natural numbers with no least element; we show $S$ is empty. Let $P(n)$ be "$n \notin S$". If $P(k)$ holds for all $k < n$, then $n \notin S$ — otherwise $n$ would be the least element of $S$. By strong induction, no $n$ is in $S$. So every non-empty set has a least element.

*Well-ordering ⇒ ordinary.* Suppose $P(0)$ holds and $P(n) \to P(n + 1)$ for every $n$, but $P$ fails somewhere. Let $m$ be the least $n$ for which $P(n)$ fails. Then $m \neq 0$, so $m = j + 1$; since $j < m$, $P(j)$ holds, and therefore $P(j + 1) = P(m)$ holds — a contradiction.

</Proof>

<Section id="errors" kind="human" title="Spot the error">

Learning to find the flaw in a wrong proof is as important as writing a right one. Each of these "proofs" has exactly one fatal step. Try to find it before reading the diagnosis.

<Example title="All horses are the same colour">

*Claim:* in every set of $n \ge 1$ horses, all the horses have the same colour.
*"Proof" by induction:* one horse has the same colour as itself. Now take $n + 1$ horses. Remove one: the remaining $n$ have one colour, by the induction hypothesis. Put it back and remove a different one: again the remaining $n$ share one colour. The two groups overlap, so all $n + 1$ horses share a colour.

*Diagnosis:* the inductive step fails from $n = 1$ to $n = 2$. With two horses, the two groups of size one do **not** overlap, so nothing connects their colours. An inductive step must work for *every* $n$, including the smallest. (This puzzle is usually credited to George Pólya.)

</Example>

<Example title="2 = 1">

*"Proof":* let $a = b$. Then $a^2 = ab$, so $a^2 - b^2 = ab - b^2$, that is, $(a + b)(a - b) = b(a - b)$. Cancelling, $a + b = b$, so $2b = b$ and $2 = 1$.

*Diagnosis:* "cancelling" divides by $a - b$, which is $0$. Hidden assumptions — here, that a number is non-zero — are the most common flaw in algebraic proofs.

</Example>

<Example title="The largest natural number is 1">

*"Proof":* let $N$ be the largest natural number. Since $N^2 \ge N$ and $N$ is the largest, $N^2 = N$, so $N = 0$ or $N = 1$; and $1 > 0$, so $N = 1$.

*Diagnosis:* the first sentence assumes, without justification, that a largest natural number exists. Every step after that is correct — which shows only that the assumption is false. (This is known as Perron's paradox.) A contradiction blames *some* assumption; make sure it is the one you meant to refute.

</Example>

<Example title="A circular proof">

*Claim:* if $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even. *"Proof":* let $n$ be even, $n = 2k$; then $n^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)$ is even.

*Diagnosis:* this proves the **converse**. It assumes the conclusion ("$n$ is even") and derives the hypothesis. Compare it with the proof of Theorem 4.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"I checked it for many numbers, so it is proved."** Examples are evidence, not proof. The circle regions agree with $2^{n-1}$ five times and then fail; $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime forty times and then fails.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Proving the converse instead of the statement.** To prove "if $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even" you must start from "$n^2$ is even" (or, by contrapositive, from "$n$ is odd"). Starting from "$n$ is even" proves a different theorem.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The inductive step is the real work, so the base case can be skipped."** Without a base case an induction proves nothing: "$n^2 + n + 1$ is even" has a valid inductive step and is false for every $n$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Assuming what is to be proved.** A proof that uses its conclusion as a step — perhaps hidden in a phrase like "clearly" or in a diagram — is circular, however long it is.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Blaming the wrong assumption.** A contradiction shows that *at least one* of your assumptions is false. If you made an extra, unjustified assumption along the way ("let $N$ be the largest natural number"), the contradiction may be its fault, not the fault of the hypothesis you meant to refute.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Euclid's proof shows that $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$ is prime."** It shows only that its prime factors are new. $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 + 1 = 59 \cdot 509$.

</Misconception>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="logic" title="proofs as mathematical objects">

Logicians study proofs themselves. In a *formal system* a proof is a finite object — a list or tree of formulas, each an axiom or obtained from earlier ones by a rule — and checking it is mechanical, as Lean does. Gerhard Gentzen's work in the 1930s made the structure of proofs a subject of its own; see <TopicLink id="math.found.proof-theory">proof theory</TopicLink>. Logic also distinguishes our methods sharply: proving $\neg P$ by assuming $P$ and deriving a contradiction is fine in every logic, but proving $P$ by refuting $\neg P$ uses the law of excluded middle, which constructive mathematicians do not assume. Euclid's theorem survives either way, because his argument actually *constructs* a new prime.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="induction is recursion">

A proof by induction is a recipe: to get $P(5)$, start from $P(0)$ and apply the inductive step five times. Under the *propositions-as-types* correspondence this is literally a recursive program, and in Lean the proof of `gauss_sum` *is* a recursive function on natural numbers. Programmers use the same idea to prove loops correct: a *loop invariant* is a statement true before the first iteration (base case) and preserved by each iteration (inductive step). See <TopicLink id="math.comp.algorithms-and-efficiency">algorithms, correctness and efficiency</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="combinatorics" title="proof by counting">

A different kind of proof of Gauss's formula counts one set in two ways. How many ways are there to choose two different numbers from $\{0, 1, \dots, n\}$? Choosing the larger one first: if it is $j$, there are $j$ choices for the smaller, giving $0 + 1 + \dots + n$. On the other hand the number of pairs from $n + 1$ objects is $\binom{n+1}{2} = \frac{(n+1)n}{2}$. Such *double counting* arguments often explain a formula better than an induction, which verifies it.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="proofs without words">

The staircase picture proves Gauss's formula for every $n$ at once, if one reads it as a general construction. The odd-number formula has a picture too: an $n \times n$ square grows into an $(n+1) \times (n+1)$ square by adding an L-shaped border of $2n + 1$ squares — the shape the Pythagoreans called a *gnomon*. Pictures can mislead (the circle regions!), so a picture becomes a proof only when we can say precisely why it works for every case.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Euclid's *Elements*** (Alexandria, around 300 BCE) organised geometry and number theory as a chain of propositions deduced from definitions and postulates — the model of mathematical proof for two thousand years. Book IX, Proposition 20 is the infinitude of primes; Book VII contains the theory of divisibility used in our <TopicLink id="math.number.divisibility-gcd">next number-theory lesson</TopicLink>.
- **China.** In his commentary (263 CE) on *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, Liu Hui justified the book's procedures with careful arguments, for example dissection proofs of area and volume formulas.
- **India.** The *Yuktibhāṣā* of Jyeṣṭhadeva (Kerala, around 1530) explains in detail the reasons (*yukti*) behind the infinite series of Mādhava's school, including arguments that proceed step by step through all whole numbers.
- **Early induction.** Around 1000 CE al-Karajī in Baghdad gave arguments about sums of cubes and binomial coefficients that pass from each case to the next, which historians see as an early form of induction; Levi ben Gershon (Gersonides, 1321), Francesco Maurolico (1575) and Blaise Pascal (1654) used the method more explicitly. The name "mathematical induction" was popularised by Augustus De Morgan in 1838.
- **Foundations.** Richard Dedekind (1888) and Giuseppe Peano (1889) made induction one of the defining properties of the natural numbers.

</Section>
