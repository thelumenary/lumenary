<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Grains on a chessboard">

A well-known legend, told in many versions across India, Persia and the Arab world, says that the inventor of chess asked a king for a modest reward: one grain of wheat on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, doubling on each of the $64$ squares. The king laughed at so small a request — until his treasurers worked it out. The last square alone needs $2^{63} \approx 9.2 \times 10^{18}$ grains. The whole board needs $2^{64} - 1$, more wheat than the world has ever harvested.

Or take a sheet of paper $0.1$ mm thick. Fold it in half $42$ times (if you could): it would be about $440\,000$ km thick, farther than the Moon.

**Multiplying by the same factor again and again** — *exponential growth* — is utterly unlike **adding the same amount again and again**. This lesson makes exponents work for all real numbers. It introduces **logarithms** to answer "how many doublings?", and it meets the number $e \approx 2.71828$, which turns up whenever growth is continuous.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Adding versus multiplying">

Compare two savings plans over five years. In plan A you add $100$ each year; in plan B your savings grow by $10\%$ each year (multiply by $1.1$).

| year | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A: add 100 | 1000 | 1100 | 1200 | 1300 | 1400 | 1500 |
| B: multiply by 1.1 | 1000 | 1100 | 1210 | 1331 | 1464.1 | 1610.51 |

In A the **differences** between consecutive years are constant; in B the **ratios** are. At first they look alike. Soon B pulls ahead, and the gap widens faster and faster: after $30$ years plan A holds $4000$ and plan B more than $17\,000$.

Decay works the same way. A medicine or a radioactive substance with a **half-life** of $8$ days keeps $\tfrac12$ of what it had every $8$ days: $100\% \to 50\% \to 25\% \to 12.5\% \to \cdots$ In this model it never quite reaches zero.

<Narrator who="chloe">

We know what $2^3$ means: $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2$. But what could $2^{-1}$ or $2^{1/2}$ or $2^{\sqrt 2}$ possibly mean? You can't multiply $2$ by itself half a time! Watch how the rules of exponents leave us no choice.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

For a real number $a > 0$ and a positive integer $n$, $a^n = a \cdot a \cdots a$ ($n$ factors). From this, $a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$ and $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$ by counting factors.

<Definition term="zero, negative and rational exponents">

For $a > 0$, integers $m$ and positive integers $n$:

$$
a^0 = 1, \qquad a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}, \qquad a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a} \ \text{(the positive $n$-th root)}, \qquad a^{m/n} = \left(a^{1/n}\right)^m.
$$

</Definition>

For an irrational exponent such as $\sqrt 2$, $a^{\sqrt 2}$ is the number that $a^{1.4}, a^{1.41}, a^{1.414}, \dots$ approach. This gives $2^{\sqrt 2} \approx 2.665$. Making this precise needs the completeness of the <TopicLink id="math.number.real-numbers">real numbers</TopicLink>. Mathlib instead defines $a^x = e^{x \ln a}$, which gives the same values.

<Definition term="exponential function">

For a fixed base $a > 0$, $a \neq 1$, the function $x \mapsto a^x$ on $\R$ is the **exponential function with base $a$**. It is increasing if $a > 1$ (growth) and decreasing if $0 < a < 1$ (decay).

</Definition>

<Definition term="logarithm">

For $a > 0$ with $a \ne 1$ and $x > 0$, the **logarithm of $x$ to base $a$**, $\log_a x$, is the number $y$ with $a^y = x$. The **natural logarithm** is $\ln x = \log_e x$.

</Definition>

So $\log_2 8 = 3$ because $2^3 = 8$, $\log_{10} 0.01 = -2$ because $10^{-2} = 0.01$, and $\log_a 1 = 0$ for every base. A logarithm answers the question "**which power?**", or in growth terms, "how many doublings (or tenfoldings)?"

<Definition term="the number e">

$e$ is the limit of $\left(1 + \tfrac1n\right)^n$ as $n$ grows without bound; $e = 2.718281828\ldots$ The function $x \mapsto e^x$ is called **the** exponential function, $\exp x$.

</Definition>

That this limit exists is a theorem, proved below.

</Section>

<Section id="laws" kind="proof" title="Why the definitions are forced">

<Theorem name="The laws of exponents determine the exponents">

Suppose a function $E : \R \to \R$, not identically zero, satisfies the law $E(x + y) = E(x)\,E(y)$ for all real $x, y$, and let $a = E(1)$. Then:

1. $E(0) = 1$;
2. $E(-x) = \dfrac{1}{E(x)}$, and $E(x) > 0$ for all $x$;
3. $E(n x) = E(x)^n$ for every integer $n$; in particular $E(n) = a^n$;
4. $E\!\left(\tfrac{m}{n}\right)$ is the positive $n$-th root of $a^m$, for all integers $m$ and $n > 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

1. Pick $x$ with $E(x) \neq 0$. Then $E(x) = E(x + 0) = E(x)E(0)$, and dividing by $E(x)$ gives $E(0) = 1$.
2. $E(x)E(-x) = E(x - x) = E(0) = 1$, so $E(-x) = 1/E(x)$ and $E(x) \ne 0$. Also $E(x) = E\!\left(\tfrac x2 + \tfrac x2\right) = E\!\left(\tfrac x2\right)^2 \ge 0$, so $E(x) > 0$.
3. For $n \ge 0$ by induction: $E((n + 1)x) = E(nx + x) = E(x)^n E(x)$. For negative $n$ use part 2.
4. By part 3, $E\!\left(\tfrac mn\right)^n = E\!\left(n \cdot \tfrac mn\right) = E(m) = a^m$, and $E\!\left(\tfrac mn\right) > 0$ by part 2. A positive number has only one positive $n$-th root: if $0 < u < v$ then $u^n < v^n$.

</Proof>

So if we want the law $a^{x + y} = a^x a^y$ to keep working, then $a^0$ *must* be $1$, $a^{-n}$ *must* be $1/a^n$, and $a^{m/n}$ *must* be $\sqrt[n]{a^m}$. The definitions are not conventions chosen for convenience: they are the only ones consistent with the law. Part 4 also shows that $a^{m/n}$ does not depend on how the fraction is written ($a^{2/4} = a^{1/2}$), because it is pinned down by the value of $\tfrac mn$ alone.

<Theorem name="Laws of logarithms">

For $a > 0$, $a \ne 1$, $x, y > 0$ and every real $r$:

$$
\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y, \qquad \log_a\!\left(\frac xy\right) = \log_a x - \log_a y, \qquad \log_a(x^r) = r \log_a x,
$$

and the change-of-base formula $\log_a x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln a}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $u = \log_a x$ and $v = \log_a y$, so $a^u = x$ and $a^v = y$. Then $a^{u + v} = a^u a^v = xy$, so $\log_a(xy) = u + v$. Similarly $a^{u - v} = x/y$ and $a^{ru} = (a^u)^r = x^r$. For change of base, write $x = a^u$ and take $\ln$ of both sides: $\ln x = u \ln a$, so $u = \ln x / \ln a$ (and $\ln a \ne 0$ because $a \ne 1$).

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Exponentials and logarithms are inverse functions">

For $a > 0$, $a \neq 1$, the function $x \mapsto a^x$ is a bijection from $\R$ onto $(0, \infty)$. Its inverse is $y \mapsto \log_a y$:

$$
a^{\log_a y} = y \ \ (y > 0), \qquad \log_a(a^x) = x \ \ (x \in \R).
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Injective:* if $a > 1$ and $x < y$, then $a^y = a^x a^{y - x}$ with $a^{y - x} > 1$, so $a^y > a^x$. The function is strictly increasing, hence injective (for $0 < a < 1$ it is strictly decreasing). *Surjective onto $(0, \infty)$:* every value is positive by the theorem above. That every $y > 0$ is actually reached uses the completeness of the real numbers (an intermediate value argument); we take it from the construction of $\ln$, as Mathlib does. A bijection has an inverse, and by definition that inverse is $\log_a$.

</Proof>

The graph of $y = \log_a x$ is therefore the reflection of the graph of $y = a^x$ in the line $y = x$, as for every inverse function (see <TopicLink id="math.analysis.functions-and-graphs">functions and their graphs</TopicLink>).

<Theorem name="log₂ 3 is irrational">

There are no integers $m, n$ with $n > 0$ and $\log_2 3 = \tfrac mn$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose $\log_2 3 = \tfrac mn$. Then $2^{m/n} = 3$, so $2^m = 3^n$. Since $3 > 1$, $\log_2 3 > 0$ and so $m > 0$. But $2^m$ is even and $3^n$ is odd, so they cannot be equal.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="growth" kind="proof" title="Exponential growth beats every polynomial">

<Theorem name="Bernoulli's inequality">

For every real $h \ge -1$ and every whole number $n$: $(1 + h)^n \ge 1 + nh$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Induction on $n$; the case $n = 0$ reads $1 \ge 1$. If $(1 + h)^n \ge 1 + nh$, multiply by $1 + h \ge 0$:

$$
(1 + h)^{n + 1} \ge (1 + nh)(1 + h) = 1 + (n + 1)h + nh^2 \ge 1 + (n + 1)h.
$$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Exponentials outgrow powers">

Let $a > 1$ and let $k$ be a whole number. Then $\dfrac{a^n}{n^k}$ grows beyond every bound as $n \to \infty$. In particular $a^n > n^k$ for all large enough $n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $a = 1 + h$ with $h > 0$. By the binomial theorem every term of $(1 + h)^n$ is positive, so for $n \ge k + 1$, keeping just the term containing $h^{k+1}$,

$$
a^n \ge \binom{n}{k+1} h^{k+1} = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k)}{(k+1)!}\, h^{k+1}.
$$

If $n \ge 2(k + 1)$, each factor $n - j$ with $j \le k$ is at least $\tfrac n2$. So

$$
\frac{a^n}{n^k} \ge \frac{(n/2)^{k+1} h^{k+1}}{(k+1)!\; n^k} = \frac{(h/2)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\cdot n,
$$

a fixed positive number times $n$, which grows beyond every bound.

</Proof>

The crossover can come late. $2^n > n^{10}$ holds for $n = 1$, fails for every $n$ from $2$ to $58$, and holds for every $n \ge 59$. Exponential growth is slow to start and then unstoppable.

<Theorem name="The limit defining e exists">

The numbers $s_n = \left(1 + \tfrac1n\right)^n$ increase with $n$ and satisfy $2 \le s_n < 3$. Hence they approach a limit $e$ with $2 < e < 3$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$s_n \ge 1 + n \cdot \tfrac1n = 2$ by Bernoulli's inequality. By the binomial theorem,

$$
s_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom nk \frac{1}{n^k} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!}\left(1 - \frac1n\right)\left(1 - \frac2n\right)\cdots\left(1 - \frac{k-1}{n}\right).
$$

Each bracket grows when $n$ grows, and $s_{n+1}$ has one more (positive) term than $s_n$. So $s_{n+1} > s_n$. Each bracket is less than $1$, so

$$
s_n \le \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{k!} \le 1 + 1 + \frac12 + \frac14 + \cdots + \frac{1}{2^{n-1}} < 3,
$$

using $k! \ge 2^{k-1}$. An increasing sequence that is bounded above has a limit; this is the completeness of the real numbers. The limit is at least $s_2 = \tfrac94 > 2$. For the upper bound, $3! = 6$ is larger than $2^2 = 4$, so the estimate above loses at least $\tfrac14 - \tfrac16 = \tfrac1{12}$ once $n \ge 3$. Every $s_n$ is therefore at most $3 - \tfrac1{12}$, and so is the limit.

</Proof>

A refinement of the same argument shows $e = 1 + 1 + \tfrac1{2!} + \tfrac1{3!} + \cdots$. More generally, compounding a rate $r$ ever more often gives $\left(1 + \tfrac rn\right)^n \to e^r$. That is why continuous growth at rate $r$ is described by $e^{rt}$.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Simplifying with the laws">

$8^{2/3} = \left(8^{1/3}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4$. Also $\dfrac{3^{5} \cdot 3^{-2}}{3^{4}} = 3^{5 - 2 - 4} = 3^{-1} = \tfrac13$, and $\log_2 48 - \log_2 3 = \log_2 16 = 4$.

</Example>

<Example title="Solving an exponential equation">

Solve $5 \cdot 3^x = 200$. Divide by $5$: $3^x = 40$. Take logarithms: $x = \log_3 40 = \dfrac{\ln 40}{\ln 3} \approx 3.358$. Check: $3^{3.358} \approx 40.0$.

</Example>

<Example title="Half-life">

Iodine-131, used in medicine to treat thyroid disease, has a half-life of about $8$ days. Of an initial amount $A_0$, after $t$ days there remains $A(t) = A_0 \left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/8}$. When is only $10\%$ left? We need $\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/8} = \tfrac1{10}$, so $\tfrac t8 \ln \tfrac12 = \ln \tfrac1{10}$ and

$$
t = 8 \cdot \frac{\ln 10}{\ln 2} \approx 26.6 \text{ days}.
$$

</Example>

<Example title="Doubling time">

Savings growing by $5\%$ a year double when $1.05^t = 2$, that is, after $t = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\ln 1.05} \approx 14.2$ years. Since $\ln(1 + r) \approx r$ for small $r$, the doubling time is about $\dfrac{0.693}{r}$. Bankers call this the **rule of 70**: at $5\%$, about $70/5 = 14$ years.

</Example>

<Example title="Logarithmic scales">

Some quantities range over so many powers of ten that we measure them by their logarithms. Acidity is measured by $\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{H}^+]$, where $[\mathrm{H}^+]$ is the hydrogen-ion concentration in moles per litre. Lemon juice at pH $2$ has $10^{5}$ times the hydrogen-ion concentration of a solution at pH $7$. A logarithmic scale turns "times $10$" into "plus $1$".

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"$\log(x + y) = \log x + \log y$."** The logarithm turns **products** into sums: $\log(xy) = \log x + \log y$. There is no simple rule for $\log(x + y)$. Test: $\log_{10}(10 + 10) = \log_{10} 20 \approx 1.30$, but $\log_{10} 10 + \log_{10} 10 = 2$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$a^{-n}$ is negative."** A negative exponent means a reciprocal, not a negative number: $2^{-3} = \tfrac18 > 0$. For $a > 0$, every power $a^x$ is positive, as the theorem above proves.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Confusing exponential and linear growth.** "Growing by $5$ per year" (add the same amount: linear) is very different from "growing by $5\%$ per year" (multiply by the same factor: exponential). In a table, linear growth has constant **differences**; exponential growth has constant **ratios**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Exponential means fast."** Exponential growth with a small rate is slow at first, and it can trail a polynomial for a long time: $1.01^n$ is smaller than $n$ for every $n$ from $2$ to $651$. What makes it exponential is that it eventually overtakes *every* polynomial.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="exp translates addition into multiplication">

The law $e^{x + y} = e^x e^y$ says that $\exp$ is a **structure-preserving map** from the real numbers under addition, $(\R, +)$, to the positive real numbers under multiplication, $(\R_{>0}, \times)$. It is a bijection, and its inverse $\ln$ preserves structure too: $\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y$. So $\exp$ is an **isomorphism**: the two structures are the same structure in different clothes. That is exactly why logarithm tables worked: to multiply two numbers, look up their logarithms, add, and look up the answer (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.structure-preserving-maps">structure-preserving maps</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Why a piano can never be perfectly in tune">

A musical octave doubles a frequency, and a perfect fifth multiplies it by $\tfrac32$. Could some number of fifths equal a whole number of octaves? That would mean $\left(\tfrac32\right)^q = 2^p$, that is, $3^q = 2^{p + q}$, which the parity argument forbids. Equivalently, $\log_2 \tfrac32$ is irrational. Twelve fifths overshoot seven octaves by the *Pythagorean comma* $\tfrac{3^{12}}{2^{19}} \approx 1.0136$. Modern equal temperament compromises: its fifth is $2^{7/12} \approx 1.4983$, slightly flat of $1.5$.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="The function equal to its own slope">

Analysts characterise $e^x$ in several equivalent ways: as the power series $\sum_k x^k/k!$ (which is how Mathlib defines it), as the limit of $\left(1 + \tfrac xn\right)^n$, or as the unique function with $f' = f$ and $f(0) = 1$. The last property says that the graph's slope at every point equals its height, which is what makes $e$ the natural base for calculus (see <TopicLink id="math.analysis.intro-calculus">introductory calculus</TopicLink>). Continuity really is needed in the "laws force the definitions" theorem. The equation $E(x + y) = E(x)E(y)$ also has wildly discontinuous solutions, whose construction (going back to Georg Hamel, 1905) needs the axiom of choice. Every *continuous* solution that is not identically zero is $a^x$ for $a = E(1)$.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Why radioactive decay is exponential">

Each unstable nucleus decays independently, with the same small probability per second, regardless of its age. So in each short time interval a fixed *fraction* of the remaining nuclei decays, and a fixed fraction lost per unit time is exactly exponential decay. Carbon-14 has a half-life of about $5730$ years. Willard Libby's radiocarbon dating (late 1940s) measures how much carbon-14 is left in once-living material to estimate its age. This is a model built on assumptions: steady production of carbon-14 in the past, which is corrected using calibration curves.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Adding exponents.** In *The Sand Reckoner* (3rd century BCE), Archimedes noted that multiplying terms of a geometric progression corresponds to adding their positions. Jain mathematicians in India, for example in Vīrasena's *Dhavalā* commentary (9th century), worked with the *ardhaccheda* of a number, the number of times it can be halved, an idea akin to a base-2 logarithm. Michael Stifel's *Arithmetica integra* (1544) set the powers of $2$, including negative exponents, beside their exponents.
- **Logarithms.** John Napier published the first table of logarithms in 1614. Jost Bürgi computed a similar table independently and published it in 1620, and Henry Briggs produced base-10 tables (1617–1624). Logarithm tables and slide rules turned multiplication into addition for navigators, astronomers and engineers for more than three centuries, until electronic calculators replaced them in the 1970s.
- **The hyperbola and e.** Grégoire de Saint-Vincent and Alphonse Antonio de Sarasa (1640s) noticed that areas under the hyperbola $y = 1/x$ behave like logarithms. Jacob Bernoulli met the limit $\left(1 + \tfrac1n\right)^n$ in 1683 while studying compound interest. Leonhard Euler named the number $e$, gave its series and many of its properties in his *Introductio in analysin infinitorum* (1748), and Charles Hermite proved in 1873 that $e$ is transcendental.

- Eli Maor, *e: The Story of a Number* (1994) — the history and mathematics of $e$ and logarithms.
- Silvanus P. Thompson, *Calculus Made Easy* (1910) — its chapter on "the compound interest law" is a classic introduction to $e$.
