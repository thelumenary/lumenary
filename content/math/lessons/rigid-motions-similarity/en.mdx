<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Drag, rotate, flip, zoom">

Open a photo on a phone. You can **drag** it across the screen, **rotate** it, **flip** it into its mirror image, or **pinch** to zoom in and out. After the first three moves the picture is exactly the same size and shape as before — only its position has changed. After zooming it is bigger or smaller, but still the same *shape*: faces are not distorted, and corners keep their angles. Stretching it in one direction only would make everyone look tall and thin.

Tilers in Isfahan and Fez fitting identical star-shaped tiles together, an architect drawing a building at a scale of $1 : 100$, a surveyor measuring a tree from its shadow: all of them rely on the same precise ideas. Moves that keep shapes *identical* give us **congruence**; adding zooms gives us **similarity**. In the <TopicLink id="math.geom.coordinate-plane">coordinate plane</TopicLink> each move becomes a simple formula, and we can *prove* what each one does.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Moving a flag around the plane">

Think of a small flag: a right triangle with corners $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (2, 0)$ and $C = (0, 1)$. It is lopsided on purpose — you can always tell whether it has been flipped. Now move it:

- **slide** it 3 to the right and 1 up: every point moves by the same amount;
- **turn** it a quarter turn about the origin: $B$ swings up to $(0, 2)$;
- **flip** it over the $x$-axis: $C$ drops to $(0, -1)$, and the flag now points the other way round;
- **zoom** it by a factor of 2 from the origin: $B$ moves out to $(4, 0)$ and $C$ to $(0, 2)$.

The first three moves never change a length or an angle. The fourth doubles every length, but keeps every angle. And doing moves one after another gives new moves — sometimes surprising ones: two flips can make a turn.

“Never change a length” — for *every* pair of points, in *every* position? Pictures cannot check infinitely many cases. Coordinates and algebra can.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

A **transformation** of the plane is a rule that sends every point $P$ to a point $P'$, its **image**.

<Definition term="rigid motion (isometry)">

A **rigid motion**, or **isometry**, is a transformation that preserves distances: for all points $P$ and $Q$, the distance from $P'$ to $Q'$ equals the distance from $P$ to $Q$.

</Definition>

Here are the rigid motions we will use, written in coordinates. (Rotations are about the origin and anticlockwise unless we say otherwise.)

| Move | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| translation by $(a, b)$ | $(x, y) \mapsto (x + a,\ y + b)$ |
| rotation by $90^\circ$ | $(x, y) \mapsto (-y,\ x)$ |
| rotation by $180^\circ$ | $(x, y) \mapsto (-x,\ -y)$ |
| rotation by $270^\circ$ ($90^\circ$ clockwise) | $(x, y) \mapsto (y,\ -x)$ |
| reflection in the $x$-axis | $(x, y) \mapsto (x,\ -y)$ |
| reflection in the $y$-axis | $(x, y) \mapsto (-x,\ y)$ |
| reflection in the line $y = x$ | $(x, y) \mapsto (y,\ x)$ |

<Definition term="dilation">

A **dilation** with centre $O$ and scale factor $k > 0$ sends each point $P$ to the point $P'$ on the ray from $O$ through $P$ with $OP' = k \cdot OP$. With centre the origin: $(x, y) \mapsto (kx,\ ky)$. (The centre itself stays put.)

</Definition>

<Definition term="congruent and similar">

Two figures are **congruent** if some rigid motion (possibly a sequence of several) maps one exactly onto the other. Two figures are **similar** if a dilation followed by a rigid motion maps one onto the other.

</Definition>

<Definition term="composite">

Doing a transformation $f$ and then a transformation $g$ is the **composite** $g \circ f$ (read “$g$ after $f$”): $(g \circ f)(P) = g(f(P))$. See <TopicLink id="math.cat.composition">composition</TopicLink>.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Images of the flag">

Rotate the flag $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (2, 0)$, $C = (0, 1)$ by $90^\circ$: $(x, y) \mapsto (-y, x)$ gives $A' = (0, 0)$, $B' = (0, 2)$, $C' = (-1, 0)$. Then translate by $(3, 1)$: $A'' = (3, 1)$, $B'' = (3, 3)$, $C'' = (2, 1)$. So the flag is **congruent** to the triangle with corners $(3, 1)$, $(3, 3)$, $(2, 1)$.

</Example>

<Example title="Showing two triangles are similar">

Is the flag similar to the triangle $(1, 1)$, $(1, 5)$, $(-1, 1)$? Its legs are 4 and 2, twice the flag's legs 2 and 1. Dilate by 2: $(0, 0)$, $(4, 0)$, $(0, 2)$. Rotate by $90^\circ$: $(0, 0)$, $(0, 4)$, $(-2, 0)$. Translate by $(1, 1)$: $(1, 1)$, $(1, 5)$, $(-1, 1)$. ✓ Similar, with scale factor 2.

</Example>

<Example title="Undoing a move">

Translation by $(3, 1)$ is undone by translation by $(-3, -1)$. Rotation by $90^\circ$ is undone by rotation by $270^\circ$ (three more quarter turns make a full turn). Every reflection undoes itself.

</Example>

<Example title="Rectangles that are not similar">

A $1 \times 2$ rectangle and a $1 \times 3$ rectangle are not similar. A similarity multiplies *all* lengths by the same $k$. Matching the short sides needs $k = 1$, but then the long side $2$ becomes $2$, not $3$; matching the short side of the first to the long side of the second needs $k = 3$, and then $2$ becomes $6$, not $1$. By contrast, any two **squares** are similar: a square of side $s$ becomes a square of side $t$ under a dilation with $k = t / s$.

</Example>

<Example title="Measuring a tree with its shadow">

At the same moment, a pole $1.5$ m tall casts a shadow $2$ m long, and a tree casts a shadow $12$ m long. The sun's rays are parallel, so the pole, its shadow and the ray form a triangle similar to the one formed by the tree: the scale factor is $12 / 2 = 6$, and the tree is $6 \times 1.5 = 9$ m tall.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="What the moves keep">

We use the distance formula from <TopicLink id="math.geom.pythagorean-theorem">the Pythagorean theorem</TopicLink>: the distance between $P = (x_1, y_1)$ and $Q = (x_2, y_2)$ is $\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}$, where $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ and $\Delta y = y_2 - y_1$. It is enough to compare $\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2$.

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (these moves are rigid)">

Translations, rotations by multiples of $90^\circ$, and reflections in the axes and in the line $y = x$ are rigid motions.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Apply each rule to $P$ and $Q$ and look at the differences.

- *Translation by $(a, b)$:* the differences become $(x_2 + a) - (x_1 + a) = \Delta x$ and $\Delta y$. Unchanged.
- *Rotation by $90^\circ$:* $P' = (-y_1, x_1)$, $Q' = (-y_2, x_2)$, so the differences become $-\Delta y$ and $\Delta x$, and $(-\Delta y)^2 + \Delta x^2 = \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2$.
- *Reflection in the $x$-axis:* the differences become $\Delta x$ and $-\Delta y$, and $(-\Delta y)^2 = \Delta y^2$.
- *Reflection in $y = x$:* the differences are swapped, which does not change the sum of their squares.

Rotations by $180^\circ$ and $270^\circ$ are two and three quarter turns, and the reflection in the $y$-axis is similar to the $x$-axis case; so these follow too (by Theorem 2 below, or directly).

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (composites and inverses)">

If $f$ and $g$ are rigid motions, so is the composite $g \circ f$. If a rigid motion $f$ has an inverse $g$ (a transformation that undoes it), then $g$ is a rigid motion too.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For any $P$, $Q$: the distance from $g(f(P))$ to $g(f(Q))$ equals the distance from $f(P)$ to $f(Q)$, because $g$ is rigid; and that equals the distance from $P$ to $Q$, because $f$ is rigid.

For the inverse: since $g$ undoes $f$, we have $f(g(P)) = P$ and $f(g(Q)) = Q$. Because $f$ is rigid, the distance from $g(P)$ to $g(Q)$ equals the distance from $f(g(P))$ to $f(g(Q))$, which is the distance from $P$ to $Q$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (rigid motions keep angles)">

A rigid motion sends every angle to an angle of the same size. Congruent figures have equal corresponding lengths and equal corresponding angles.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take an angle $\angle BAC$ and complete it to the triangle $ABC$. A rigid motion sends it to a triangle $A'B'C'$ with the same three side lengths. A triangle is fixed by its three sides (the SSS rule: you cannot build two differently shaped triangles from the same three sticks), so the two triangles have the same angles, and $\angle B'A'C' = \angle BAC$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (two reflections make a rotation)">

Reflecting in a line $\ell_1$ and then in a line $\ell_2$, where the two lines cross at a point $O$ at an angle $\theta$ (measured anticlockwise from $\ell_1$ to $\ell_2$), is the rotation about $O$ by $2\theta$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Both reflections keep the distance from $O$, since $O$ is on both mirrors. So we only need to follow directions. Measure the direction of a point $P$ as an angle from $\ell_1$, so that $\ell_2$ is at angle $\theta$. Reflection in the line at angle $\theta$ sends direction $\psi$ to direction $2\theta - \psi$ (the mirror sits exactly halfway between them). So $P$, at direction $\varphi$, goes first to direction $-\varphi$ (reflection in $\ell_1$, the line at angle $0$), and then to direction $2\theta - (-\varphi) = \varphi + 2\theta$. Every point stays at the same distance from $O$ and turns by $2\theta$: that is the rotation by $2\theta$.

</Proof>

For example, the $x$-axis and the line $y = x$ cross at $45^\circ$, so reflecting in the $x$-axis and then in $y = x$ is the rotation by $90^\circ$: in coordinates, $(x, y) \mapsto (x, -y) \mapsto (-y, x)$. ✓ In the other order the angle from the first mirror to the second is $-45^\circ$, and we get the rotation by $-90^\circ$: $(x, y) \mapsto (y, x) \mapsto (y, -x)$. Two reflections in the same line give the rotation by $0^\circ$: nothing moves.

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (order matters)">

Composing transformations is not commutative: in general, $g \circ f \neq f \circ g$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

One counterexample is enough. Let $f$ be the translation by $(1, 0)$ and $g$ the rotation by $90^\circ$. Then $g(f(0, 0)) = g(1, 0) = (0, 1)$, but $f(g(0, 0)) = f(0, 0) = (1, 0)$. The two composites send the origin to different points, so they are different transformations. The two reflections above are another counterexample.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 6 (dilations)">

A dilation with scale factor $k > 0$ multiplies every length by $k$, sends every line segment to a parallel segment, keeps every angle, and multiplies every area by $k^2$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take the centre to be the origin, so $(x, y) \mapsto (kx, ky)$. (For another centre, translate it to the origin first, dilate, and translate back; translations change none of these quantities.)

*Lengths.* The differences become $k\,\Delta x$ and $k\,\Delta y$, so the distance becomes $\sqrt{k^2 \Delta x^2 + k^2 \Delta y^2} = k\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}$.

*Directions.* The image segment has the same slope: $\frac{k\,\Delta y}{k\,\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ (and vertical segments stay vertical). So each segment is sent to a parallel segment pointing the same way.

*Angles.* An angle is formed by two rays; their images point in the same two directions, so the angle between them is the same.

*Areas.* A rectangle $a \times b$ with sides along the axes becomes $ka \times kb$, with area $k^2 ab$. A triangle's base and height are both multiplied by $k$, so its area $\tfrac12 \cdot \text{base} \cdot \text{height}$ is multiplied by $k^2$. Any polygon can be cut into triangles, so its area is multiplied by $k^2$ too.

</Proof>

So similar figures have the **same angles**, and corresponding lengths in the **same ratio** $k$. Doubling every length makes the area **four** times as large, and tripling makes it nine times.

Look at Theorem 2 again. Rigid motions can be composed and undone, and composing with “do nothing” changes nothing. Mathematicians call a collection of moves like this a **group** — and the same pattern appeared in clock arithmetic. That is no coincidence: the turns of a square *are* a 4-hour clock.

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**“Similar figures must be the same size.”** That is *congruent*. Similar figures have the same shape — the same angles and proportional sides — but may differ in size: every photo enlargement is similar to the original.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“Any two rectangles are similar.”** Rectangles all have four right angles, but their sides need not be in the same ratio: $1 \times 2$ and $1 \times 3$ are not similar. Equal angles are enough for triangles to be similar, but not for quadrilaterals.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“The order of transformations never matters.”** Turning and then sliding usually differs from sliding and then turning (Theorem 5), and two reflections in crossing lines turn one way or the other depending on the order. Some pairs do commute — two translations, or two rotations about the same centre — but you must check.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“Doubling the lengths doubles the area.”** A dilation with factor $k$ multiplies areas by $k^2$: doubling every length makes the area four times as big. (And volumes of solids are multiplied by $k^3$.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“A reflection is just a rotation viewed differently.”** A reflection reverses orientation: an anticlockwise $A \to B \to C$ becomes clockwise, and the signed area changes sign. No sequence of translations and rotations can do that, so a left hand and a right hand are congruent only if reflections are allowed.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Moves form groups">

Theorem 2 says that the rigid motions of the plane can be composed, that composing is associative, that “do nothing” is a rigid motion, and that every rigid motion has an inverse that is again rigid. These are exactly the rules of a **group**. The eight grid symmetries of the computation form a smaller group with 8 elements, the symmetry group of a square; its four rotations alone form a cyclic group that behaves exactly like addition modulo 4 (see <TopicLink id="math.number.modular-arithmetic">clock arithmetic</TopicLink>). Because $g \circ f \ne f \circ g$ in general, these groups are **non-commutative** — a feature that check-digit designers have even put to work (see <TopicLink id="math.applied.check-digits">check digits</TopicLink>). See <TopicLink id="math.algebra.groups">groups</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="A geometry is what its moves keep">

In 1872 Felix Klein proposed that a geometry is the study of the properties that a group of transformations leaves unchanged. **Euclidean geometry** studies what rigid motions keep: lengths, angles, areas. **Similarity geometry** allows dilations too, and keeps only angles and ratios of lengths — which is why a map and the land it shows have “the same geometry” in that sense. Allowing even more transformations gives affine and projective geometry, where angles stop mattering but straight lines remain straight. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.transformation-geometry">transformation geometry</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="categories" title="Maps that preserve structure">

The plane with its distances is a mathematical structure, and rigid motions are exactly the maps that preserve it. Composites of structure-preserving maps preserve structure, and so do identity maps (Theorem 2) — the two rules that define a **category**. Dilations preserve less (only ratios), so they belong to a different, larger category. Asking “which maps preserve this structure?” is one of the guiding questions of modern mathematics; see <TopicLink id="math.cat.structure-preserving-maps">structure-preserving maps</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Greece.** In Euclid's *Elements* (about 300 BCE), the proof of Book I, Proposition 4 (side–angle–side congruence) imagines placing one triangle on top of another — an early, informal use of rigid motion. Later writers report that Thales measured the height of a pyramid from its shadow by similar triangles; the story is told centuries after his time.
- **China.** Liu Hui's *Sea Island Mathematical Manual* (263 CE) shows how to find the height of a distant island and its distance using two poles and similar triangles — practical surveying without ever reaching the island.
- **The Islamic world.** Craftsmen across North Africa, Iran and Central Asia built geometric tilings whose patterns repeat under translations, rotations and reflections, such as those of the Alhambra in Granada (14th century) and of buildings in Isfahan.
- **Modern times.** Felix Klein's *Erlangen programme* (1872) put transformations at the centre of geometry. In 1891 Evgraf Fedorov in Russia proved that there are exactly 17 essentially different symmetry types of repeating wallpaper patterns.
