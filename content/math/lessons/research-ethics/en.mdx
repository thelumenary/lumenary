<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Three situations">

**Amina**, a doctoral student in Nairobi, proves a lemma she needs for her thesis. A month later, reading a 1978 paper for a different reason, she finds the same lemma, with a different proof. **Three collaborators** — in São Paulo, Hanoi and Toronto — finish a paper: one had the key idea, one did the long computations, one wrote most of the text. In what order should their names appear? **A postdoc** in Lyon asks an AI system to prove a technical lemma, gets a fluent proof, and then asks three other AI systems, which all say it is correct. May she state the lemma as proved?

None of these is exotic; every research mathematician meets them. Two principles organise the answers:

- **Mathematical truth does not depend on who discovers it, but credit for a discovery belongs to those who made it.** Citation and attribution are how the community keeps that record.
- **A claim is established only when it has been proved and the proof has been checked** — by people, or by a verified formal method. Everything else must be labelled for what it is: a citation, a conjecture, a heuristic, a computation.

This lesson builds on <TopicLink id="math.found.reading-literature">reading the mathematical literature</TopicLink>, where you learned to classify the claims in a paper; here you learn to make your own claims, and give your own credit, honestly.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Why mathematics has its own ethics">

Mathematics shares the general principles of research integrity — honesty, fairness, responsibility — but three features give them a particular shape.

1. **The literature is a dependency graph.** A theorem rests on the results it uses, which rest on others. A citation is an edge in that graph: it says "this argument depends on that one". A missing citation hides a dependency; a false lemma infects everything downstream until it is found and the record corrected. (This is the same graph a formalisation blueprint makes explicit; see <TopicLink id="math.comp.formalising-published-mathematics">formalising published mathematics</TopicLink>.)
2. **Proofs can be checked.** Unlike most empirical claims, a mathematical error can usually be identified definitively. That makes correction possible — and expected. Admitting an error costs little compared with the damage a known, uncorrected error does to everyone who relies on it.
3. **Credit is the currency, and it is unevenly distributed.** Hiring, grants and invitations depend on who is known to have done what. Researchers who are early in their careers, far from major centres, or from groups historically excluded from mathematics are the most easily harmed when credit is misassigned — and the history of mathematics has many such cases.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="plagiarism and attribution">

**Plagiarism** is presenting another person's words, ideas or results as one's own. **Attribution** credits the origin of an idea, result, proof, code or data set. Plagiarism does not require copying words: restating someone else's proof in your own words without credit is plagiarism of ideas.

</Definition>

<Definition term="citation">

A **citation** identifies the source of a result, definition, method or piece of software precisely enough for a reader to find it (author, title, venue, year; theorem numbers where useful; versions for preprints and software). A **primary** citation names the original source; a **secondary** one names a book or survey where the result is presented. Cite what you actually read.

</Definition>

<Definition term="authorship and acknowledgement">

An **author** has made a substantial intellectual contribution to the work and takes responsibility for the whole of it. In mathematics authors are customarily listed **alphabetically**, and the order carries no information about relative contribution. Help that does not amount to authorship — a useful conversation, a reference, a careful reading — belongs in the **acknowledgements**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="erratum, corrigendum, retraction">

An **erratum** or **corrigendum** is a published correction of an error in a published paper (strictly, an erratum corrects an error introduced by the publisher and a corrigendum one made by the authors, but usage varies). A **retraction** withdraws a paper whose main results are invalid, or that should not have been published.

</Definition>

<Definition term="status of a claim">

Every mathematical claim in your writing has a **status**: *proved here* (with a complete proof), *cited* (proved elsewhere, with a reference), *conjectured* (believed, not proved), *heuristic* (supported by a plausible but non-rigorous argument), or *supported by computation* (verified in finitely many cases, or numerically). Honest writing makes the status of each claim visible.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="cases" kind="examples" title="Worked cases">

<Example title="A lemma you proved, already in print">

Amina must cite the 1978 paper as the source of the lemma. She may still include her own proof if it is genuinely different or simpler — "the following lemma is due to [X, 1978, Lemma 3]; we give a different proof, which adapts to our setting" — but she may not present the lemma as new. Independent rediscovery is common and honourable; claiming priority after learning of earlier work is not.

</Example>

<Example title="Well-known results">

Results in every textbook (the Baire category theorem, Sylow's theorems) need no primary citation, but if you use a specific form, or a version that not every reader will know, cite a standard reference where it is proved — "by the Baire category theorem in the form of [Munkres, Theorem 48.2]". Results that are "well known to experts" but nowhere written down are the dangerous case: either give a proof, or a reference where one can be found, or say plainly that you are relying on folklore.

</Example>

<Example title="Talks, conversations, preprints">

A result heard in a talk or a conversation is not yours to publish. Ask the person before using an unpublished result, and cite it as "personal communication" or by the preprint (with its arXiv identifier and version). If someone's remark in a conversation gave you the key idea, say so in the acknowledgements — or, if the contribution was substantial, consider asking whether they would like to be a co-author.

</Example>

<Example title="Software, databases, formal libraries">

Cite software with its version (a computer algebra system, a SAT solver, Lean with the Mathlib commit), databases with the date accessed (the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences, the L-functions and Modular Forms Database), and make your own code available. A computer-assisted proof should say what was computed, how, and how the computation was checked; a computation that cannot be reproduced is a weak foundation.

</Example>

<Example title="AI assistance">

The postdoc should disclose how AI systems were used (for a literature search, for drafting text, for code, for proof ideas), take full responsibility for everything in the paper, and **check the proof herself or formally verify it**. The agreement of three further AI systems is not a verification: as the model in the proof section shows, agreement is evidence only to the extent that the checkers' errors are independent, and systems trained on overlapping data with similar methods share blind spots. AI systems cannot be authors, because they cannot take responsibility; major publishers and arXiv adopted policies to that effect in 2023. Two further hazards: AI-generated text may reproduce published work without attribution, and AI-generated references may not exist — check every reference against the source.

</Example>

<Example title="Authorship among three collaborators">

All three made substantial intellectual contributions — the idea, the computations and the exposition are all part of the work — so all three are authors, listed alphabetically. Some journals ask for a short statement of who did what; that is the place for detail, not the order of names. Responsibility is shared: each author should understand and stand behind the whole paper. "Honorary" authorship — adding a senior name that contributed nothing — is a misrepresentation; so is leaving out a student who contributed substantially.

</Example>

<Example title="An error in your own published paper">

Check the error carefully, then act promptly: tell the editor and publish a corrigendum (or, if the main result fails, a retraction); post a revised version of the preprint with a note explaining the change; and tell the people you know rely on the result. When Andrew Wiles's 1993 proof of Fermat's Last Theorem was found to have a gap during refereeing, he announced the gap publicly in December 1993; with Richard Taylor he repaired it in 1994, and the work was published in 1995 as two papers, one by Wiles and one by Taylor and Wiles, recording exactly who did what.

</Example>

<Example title="An error in someone else's paper">

Make sure first — ask a colleague to check your reasoning. Then write to the authors privately, specifically and courteously, and give them time to respond. If the error is important and remains uncorrected, publish a note or comment that states the problem precisely and credits what is correct. When Percy Heawood showed in 1890 that Alfred Kempe's 1879 proof of the four colour theorem was flawed, he did so in a paper that also extracted from Kempe's method a correct proof of the five colour theorem.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Two pieces of mathematics behind the ethics">

**First: how an error was repaired.** Cauchy's *Cours d'analyse* (1821) stated that if a series of functions continuous near a point converges there, its sum is continuous there. In 1826 Abel remarked that the theorem "admits exceptions", citing the series $\sin x - \tfrac12 \sin 2x + \tfrac13 \sin 3x - \cdots$, which converges for every real $x$, to $x/2$ on $(-\pi, \pi)$, and so jumps at $x = \pi$. A simpler exception:

<Theorem name="Proposition (the pointwise sum theorem is false)">

Let $c(x) = \max(0, \min(x, 1))$ and $f_n(x) = c(x)^n$. Each $f_n : \R \to \R$ is continuous, and $f_n(x) \to g(x)$ for every $x$, where $g(x) = 0$ for $x < 1$ and $g(x) = 1$ for $x \ge 1$. The limit $g$ is not continuous.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$c$ is continuous, so each $f_n$ is. If $x \ge 1$ then $c(x) = 1$ and $f_n(x) = 1$ for all $n$. If $x < 1$ then $0 \le c(x) < 1$, so $c(x)^n \to 0$. If $g$ were continuous, then since $g(0) = 0$ and $g(1) = 1$ the intermediate value theorem would give $x$ with $g(x) = \tfrac12$; but $g$ takes only the values $0$ and $1$.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Where exactly does Cauchy's argument break on this example? To make $c(x)^n < \varepsilon$ you need $n > \log \varepsilon / \log c(x)$, and as $x \to 1^-$ that $n$ grows without bound: the choice of $n$ depends on $x$. Uniform convergence is precisely the demand that one $n$ serve every $x$ — the hypothesis the old argument was silently using.

</Narrator>

The repair, found by Seidel and by Stokes in 1847 and made central by Weierstrass, is to strengthen the hypothesis.

<Theorem name="Theorem (uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous)">

If $f_n : \R \to \R$ are continuous and $f_n \to g$ uniformly — $\sup_x |f_n(x) - g(x)| \to 0$ — then $g$ is continuous.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $a \in \R$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $n$ with $|f_n(x) - g(x)| < \varepsilon/3$ for all $x$, then $\delta > 0$ with $|f_n(x) - f_n(a)| < \varepsilon/3$ whenever $|x - a| < \delta$. For such $x$,
$$
|g(x) - g(a)| \le |g(x) - f_n(x)| + |f_n(x) - f_n(a)| + |f_n(a) - g(a)| < \varepsilon.
$$

</Proof>

Historians still discuss how Cauchy's statement should be read — his notion of convergence and of infinitely small quantities was not ours, and Imre Lakatos (1976) used the episode as a case study in how proofs and concepts evolve together. What is not in dispute is the pattern: a published claim failed, a counterexample was credited to the person who found it, and the theorem was repaired by finding the hypothesis the proof actually used. That pattern is the ethical ideal.

**Second: what agreement is worth.** Consider $k$ checkers — referees, programs or AI systems — who all accept a proof. Model them as follows. A correct proof is always accepted. A flawed proof is accepted by each checker with probability $q$; with probability $\rho$ the checkers share a blind spot and return one common verdict, and otherwise they judge independently. Then all $k$ accept a flawed proof with probability $\rho q + (1 - \rho) q^k$, and if the prior probability that the proof is correct is $\pi_0$, Bayes' rule gives
$$
P(\text{correct} \mid k \text{ acceptances}) = \frac{\pi_0}{\pi_0 + (1 - \pi_0)\big(\rho q + (1 - \rho) q^k\big)}.
$$

<Theorem name="Proposition (correlated agreement has a ceiling)">

Let $0 < \pi_0 < 1$, $0 \le q$ and $0 \le \rho \le 1$. For every $k$, the posterior above is at most $\dfrac{\pi_0}{\pi_0 + (1 - \pi_0)\rho q}$, which is strictly less than $1$ when $\rho q > 0$. If instead $\rho = 0$ and $q < 1$, the posterior tends to $1$ as $k \to \infty$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Since $(1 - \rho)q^k \ge 0$, the denominator is at least $\pi_0 + (1 - \pi_0)\rho q > 0$, and dividing $\pi_0$ by a larger positive number gives a smaller result. If $\rho q > 0$ then $(1 - \pi_0)\rho q > 0$, so the ceiling's denominator exceeds $\pi_0$ and the ceiling is below $1$. If $\rho = 0$ and $0 \le q < 1$, then $q^k \to 0$, so the denominator tends to $\pi_0$ and the posterior to $1$.

</Proof>

With $\pi_0 = \tfrac12$, $q = \tfrac35$ and $\rho = \tfrac12$ the ceiling is $\tfrac{10}{13} \approx 0.77$: a hundred agreeing checkers still leave at least a $\tfrac{3}{13}$ chance — nearly one in four — that the proof is wrong. The model is a toy — real checkers' errors are correlated in ways nobody can quantify — but its moral is robust. **Agreement is evidence only to the extent that the checkers fail independently**, which is precisely what cannot be assumed for AI systems with overlapping training data and similar architectures, or for referees who all skim the same hard step. And even independent agreement is evidence, not proof. This is the sense in which *agreement among several AI systems is not evidence that a proof is correct*: it cannot establish correctness, and because the correlation of their errors is unknown, it does not even tell you how far your confidence should move. **Proofs must be checked by people or by verified formal methods.**

<Narrator who="margaret">

Put numbers into the formula with $\pi_0 = \tfrac12$ and $q = \tfrac35$. Three independent checkers give $\frac{1}{1 + 0.216} \approx 0.82$, and ten give about $0.994$. Three correlated ones ($\rho = \tfrac12$) give $\frac{1}{1 + 0.3 + 0.108} \approx 0.71$ — already within $0.06$ of the ceiling $\tfrac{10}{13} \approx 0.77$. Seven more correlated checkers raise it only to about $0.77$; seven more independent ones would have taken $0.82$ to $0.994$.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="status" kind="human" title="Conjecture, heuristic, evidence: labelling claims">

The commonest dishonesty in mathematical writing is not fabrication but **blurred status**: a conjecture cited as if proved, a heuristic presented as an argument, numerical evidence described as "confirming" a statement. Some history explains why the labels matter.

- **Euler's conjecture on sums of like powers** (1769): at least $k$ positive $k$-th powers are needed to sum to a $k$-th power. It was believed for almost two centuries. In 1966 L. J. Lander and T. R. Parkin found, by a computer search, $27^5 + 84^5 + 110^5 + 133^5 = 144^5$; their paper in the *Bulletin of the AMS* is two sentences long. In 1988 Noam Elkies found infinitely many counterexamples for fourth powers, and Roger Frye found the smallest, $95800^4 + 217519^4 + 414560^4 = 422481^4$.
- **Fermat numbers.** Fermat believed that every $F_n = 2^{2^n} + 1$ is prime; $F_0, \dots, F_4$ are. In 1732 Euler showed that $641$ divides $F_5 = 4294967297$. No $F_n$ with $n \ge 5$ is known to be prime.
- **Mersenne's list** (1644) of exponents $p \le 257$ with $2^p - 1$ prime contained five errors, corrected over three centuries by Pervushin (1883), Cole (1903), Powers (1911 and 1914) and others. Cole's factorisation $2^{67} - 1 = 193707721 \times 761838257287$, presented at a meeting of the American Mathematical Society in 1903, is a famous example of a correction that needed no words.
- **Overwhelming evidence can mislead.** Pólya's conjecture (that for every $n \ge 2$, at least half the numbers from $1$ to $n$ have an odd number of prime factors, counted with multiplicity) holds up to $906{,}150{,}256$ and fails at $906{,}150{,}257$ (Haselgrove 1958 proved that it fails; Tanaka found the first counterexample in 1980). The Mertens conjecture was disproved by Odlyzko and te Riele (1985) without an explicit counterexample, which is believed to be astronomically large.

Arthur Jaffe and Frank Quinn (1993) argued that speculative "theoretical mathematics" should be labelled as such, so that credit goes both to those who conjecture and to those who prove; William Thurston's reply (1994) stressed that understanding and ideas also deserve credit, and that the community's norms must recognise them. Both agreed on the essential point: **say what you have proved, and what you have not.**

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Well-known results need no citation."** Textbook results need no *primary* citation, but the specific version you use should be traceable, and results that are merely "well known to experts" need either a proof or a reference. The reader must be able to check every step; a missing citation is a missing step.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"AI-generated text and proofs are exempt from attribution and verification."** They are subject to both. Disclose how AI systems were used; check that generated text does not reproduce others' work without credit and that every reference exists; and verify every generated proof as you would a stranger's — by reading it carefully or by formal verification. Agreement among several AI systems does not count as verification.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Admitting an error does more harm than concealing it."** A concealed error goes on harming everyone who builds on it, and when it is found — errors in important work usually are — the concealment does far more damage to a reputation than the error would have. Published corrections are a normal part of mathematics; Wiles's announcement of the gap in his 1993 proof is remembered as exemplary.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The first author did the most work."** In mathematics the order is normally alphabetical and says nothing about contribution. (In fields with other conventions, such as much of the experimental sciences, readers interpret order differently — be aware of this when collaborating across fields.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Citing a paper means I have checked its proofs."** It means you rely on it. If your result depends essentially on a long or recent paper whose proof you have not checked, it is honest to say so, and good practice to check the parts you use.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Proofs and refutations: Euler's formula">

Lakatos's *Proofs and Refutations* (1976) is built around Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ for polyhedra and Cauchy's 1813 proof of it. Counterexamples soon appeared: a "picture frame" (a polyhedral torus) has $V - E + F = 0$, as Lhuilier observed in 1813. The response that survived was neither to ban the counterexamples ("monster-barring") nor to abandon the theorem, but to find the hypothesis the proof actually used: the formula holds for polyhedra whose surface is homeomorphic to a sphere, and for a closed orientable surface of genus $g$ the Euler characteristic is $2 - 2g$. Topology was partly born from taking counterexamples seriously and crediting them. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.surfaces-euler-characteristic">surfaces and Euler characteristic</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="The hypothesis the proof used">

Cauchy's sum theorem and its repair are the analyst's version of the same story. The faulty argument exchanged two limits — $\lim_{x \to a} \lim_{n} f_n(x) = \lim_n \lim_{x \to a} f_n(x)$ — and the exchange is justified exactly when the convergence is uniform near $a$. Uniform convergence, isolated by Seidel and Stokes and systematised by Weierstrass, became one of the central concepts of analysis; similar stories lie behind dominated convergence, differentiation under the integral sign and Fubini's theorem. In each case the corrected statement credits the counterexample by building its lesson into the hypotheses. See <TopicLink id="math.analysis.real-analysis">real analysis</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Gaps, repairs and names">

Number theory supplies both the model of a repaired proof — Wiles's announcement of the gap in 1993 and the Taylor–Wiles repair — and cautionary tales about names. The conjecture that every elliptic curve over $\Q$ is modular grew from problems posed by Yutaka Taniyama at a 1955 symposium in Tokyo and Nikkō, was sharpened by Goro Shimura, and was publicised by André Weil; its naming became a subject of public dispute (Serge Lang, *Notices of the AMS*, 1995). Today it is usually called the **modularity theorem**, proved for semistable curves by Wiles and Taylor–Wiles and in general by Breuil, Conrad, Diamond and Taylor (2001). Names are attributions, and attributions can be wrong. See <TopicLink id="math.number.arithmetic-geometry">arithmetic geometry</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="Independence is the whole question">

The agreement model is an exercise in conditional probability, and its lesson is a familiar one: evidence from several sources multiplies only if the sources are conditionally independent. With independent checkers, $k$ acceptances multiply the odds that a proof is correct by $q^{-k}$; with a common cause of error, the multiplier is bounded. Statisticians meet the same structure in correlated measurements, epidemiologists in confounding, and anyone reading reviews in duplicated opinions. The model's parameters cannot be measured for real AI systems, which is a further reason not to treat their agreement as verification. See <TopicLink id="math.prob.probability-theory">probability theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="Computer-assisted proofs and reproducibility">

The four colour theorem (Appel and Haken, 1976) was the first famous proof that no person could check by hand, and its acceptance was debated for years. Two responses settled it: independent re-proofs with new programs (Robertson, Sanders, Seymour and Thomas, 1997), and a formal proof in Coq that checks the computations too (Gonthier, 2005). The ethical norms that emerged apply to every computation in a paper: publish the code and data, record versions, describe how results were checked, and cite the software and its authors — computations are contributions, and so are the tools. See <TopicLink id="math.comp.computational-reproducibility">computational reproducibility</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History: credit across centuries and continents">

- **Cardano and Tartaglia.** In 1539 Niccolò Tartaglia gave Gerolamo Cardano his method for solving cubic equations, after Cardano swore not to publish it. When Cardano and Lodovico Ferrari learned that Scipione del Ferro had found the method earlier, Cardano published it in his *Ars Magna* (1545), crediting both del Ferro and Tartaglia. Tartaglia regarded this as a betrayal, and a bitter public dispute followed — an early case in which correct attribution and a broken promise pulled in different directions.
- **Newton and Leibniz.** The priority dispute over the calculus was "settled" in 1712 by a committee of the Royal Society, whose report favoured Newton; Newton, then the Society's president, wrote much of it himself and anonymously reviewed it. Today both are credited with independent invention — and the episode is a standing warning about judging one's own case.
- **Kerala and Europe.** The series $\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \cdots$ and the series for the arctangent were found by Mādhava of Saṅgamagrāma (c. 1340–1425) and his school in Kerala, centuries before Gregory and Leibniz; the name "Mādhava–Leibniz series" is now common. Similarly, the arithmetic triangle named after Pascal was known to al-Karajī and Omar Khayyam in Persia and to Jia Xian and Yang Hui in China; in China it is called Yang Hui's triangle. Stephen Stigler's "law of eponymy" (1980) — no discovery is named after its first discoverer — is, as he noted, not named after its first discoverer either.
- **Credit withheld.** Sophie Germain corresponded with Lagrange and Gauss under the name "Monsieur Le Blanc". Emmy Noether's lectures in Göttingen were announced under David Hilbert's name until she was allowed to habilitate in 1919. Srinivasa Ramanujan's 1913 letter to G. H. Hardy brought the work of a largely self-taught clerk in Madras to the attention it deserved. Fair attribution is also a matter of who is allowed to be seen.
- **Collaboration norms.** Harald Bohr recorded the "axioms" of Hardy and Littlewood's collaboration, the last being that all their papers would be joint, whatever the share of each — a humorous statement of the principle behind alphabetical order. The American Mathematical Society's statement *The Culture of Research and Scholarship in Mathematics: Joint Research and Its Publication* (2004) describes the alphabetical convention and its meaning.
- **Errors acknowledged.** Vladimir Voevodsky, in an essay of 2014, described how the proof of a key lemma in one of his own papers turned out to be wrong years after publication, and how a counterexample found by Carlos Simpson in 1998 to the main claim of a 1991 paper by Mikhail Kapranov and Voevodsky took years to be accepted. These experiences led him to devote his later work to computer-verified foundations.
