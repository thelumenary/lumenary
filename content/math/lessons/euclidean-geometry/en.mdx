<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Why prove what you can see?">

A carpenter in Nairobi is building a door frame. She has cut the two long sides to the same length, and the two short sides to the same length, and joined them. Is the frame a true rectangle, with four right angles? She does not reach for a protractor. She measures the two diagonals with a tape. If they are equal, the frame is "square". Builders all over the world use this trick. But *why* does it work? Could a frame have equal diagonals and still be skewed?

Here is a second puzzle. A famous "proof" that **every triangle is isosceles** goes like this. Take any triangle $ABC$. Let the bisector of the angle at $A$ meet the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ at a point $P$, drawn inside the triangle. Drop perpendiculars from $P$ to $AB$ and $AC$. A few pairs of congruent triangles later, the argument concludes that $AB = AC$. Every step seems to follow from the picture — and the conclusion is plainly false, since a triangle with sides $3$, $4$, $5$ is not isosceles.

The error is not in any calculation. It is in the **picture**: when $AB \ne AC$, the point $P$ is never actually inside the triangle (we will see where it really is). This is why mathematicians prove geometric facts from stated assumptions, not from how a diagram looks. Euclid of Alexandria organised geometry this way around 300 BCE, and his *Elements* became the model of proof for two thousand years. In this lesson we will build up the main theorems about triangles and circles from a few axioms, see exactly where each axiom is used, and find out what happens when one of them is dropped.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="The rules of the game">

A proof in geometry is a chain of statements, each justified by an axiom, a definition or an earlier theorem. The diagram helps us *think*; it is never a reason.

Euclid began with five **postulates**. In modern words:

1. Any two points can be joined by a straight line segment.
2. A segment can be extended indefinitely.
3. A circle can be drawn with any centre and any radius.
4. All right angles are equal.
5. If a line crossing two other lines makes the interior angles on one side add up to less than two right angles, then the two lines, extended far enough, meet on that side.

The fifth, the **parallel postulate**, is longer and less obvious than the others. For two thousand years mathematicians tried to prove it from the first four. They all failed, and in the 19th century it became clear why: it *cannot* be proved from them. We will come back to this.

Euclid's own list had gaps. He sometimes used facts that he never stated, such as "a line that enters a triangle through one side must leave through another". In 1899 David Hilbert gave a complete list of axioms for plane geometry, which fills those gaps. We will not list all of Hilbert's axioms. Instead we take as known the basic facts about points, lines, betweenness, lengths and angle measure that you have used for years, together with three things we state explicitly:

- **Angles on a straight line** add up to $180^\circ$, and vertically opposite angles are equal.
- The **SAS axiom** (below). Euclid "proved" it by picking one triangle up and laying it on the other. That is really an assumption about rigid motions (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">rigid motions, congruence and similarity</TopicLink>), so Hilbert made it an axiom.
- The **parallel postulate**, in the form of Playfair's axiom.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Watch for the parallel postulate in every proof below. Some theorems need it and some don't — and that difference turns out to be one of the great discoveries in the history of mathematics.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="congruent triangles">

Triangles $ABC$ and $A'B'C'$ are **congruent**, written $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle A'B'C'$, if corresponding sides and corresponding angles are equal: $AB = A'B'$, $BC = B'C'$, $CA = C'A'$, $\angle A = \angle A'$, $\angle B = \angle B'$ and $\angle C = \angle C'$. The order of the letters records which vertex corresponds to which.

</Definition>

<Definition term="the SAS axiom">

If $AB = A'B'$, $AC = A'C'$ and the **included** angles are equal, $\angle BAC = \angle B'A'C'$, then $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle A'B'C'$.

</Definition>

From SAS one can prove two more **congruence criteria** (Euclid, *Elements* Book I, Propositions 8 and 26):

- **ASA**: two angles and the side between them determine the triangle;
- **SSS**: three sides determine the triangle.

From ASA and the angle sum of a triangle (proved below) follows **AAS**. But two famous combinations are *not* criteria: **AAA** only gives similar triangles (the same shape, perhaps a different size), and **SSA** fails, as we will see.

<Definition term="parallel lines">

Two lines in a plane are **parallel** if they do not meet, however far they are extended.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Playfair's axiom (the parallel postulate)">

Through a point not on a given line there is **exactly one** line parallel to the given line.

</Definition>

Playfair's axiom (popularised by John Playfair in 1795, though it was known much earlier) is equivalent to Euclid's fifth postulate, given the other axioms, and is easier to use.

When a line $t$, a **transversal**, crosses two lines $\ell$ and $m$, it makes eight angles. Two angles on opposite sides of $t$ and between $\ell$ and $m$ are called **alternate angles**.

<Definition term="circle words">

A **circle** with **centre** $O$ and **radius** $r$ is the set of points at distance $r$ from $O$. A **chord** joins two points of the circle; a **diameter** is a chord through $O$. For a chord $AC$:

- the **central angle** is $\angle AOC$;
- an **inscribed angle** is $\angle ABC$ where $B$ is another point of the circle. Its vertex is on the circle and its sides are chords. We say it **subtends** the arc $AC$ that does not contain $B$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="perpendicular bisector and circumcentre">

The **perpendicular bisector** of a segment $AB$ is the line through the midpoint of $AB$ perpendicular to $AB$. A circle through all three vertices of a triangle is its **circumcircle**, and its centre is the **circumcentre**.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Proofs with congruent triangles">

The best way to learn to write geometric proofs is to read and write short ones. In each proof below, every step names its reason.

<Example title="The isosceles triangle theorem">

**Claim.** If $AB = AC$ in triangle $ABC$, then $\angle B = \angle C$.

**Proof.** Compare triangle $ABC$ with *itself*, read in a different order: triangle $ACB$. We have $AB = AC$ and $AC = AB$ (given), and the included angles $\angle BAC$ and $\angle CAB$ are the same angle. By SAS, $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle ACB$. Corresponding angles are equal, so $\angle ABC = \angle ACB$. $\blacksquare$

This elegant proof is credited to Pappus of Alexandria (4th century CE). Euclid's own proof (Book I, Proposition 5) uses a more complicated figure, and medieval students nicknamed it the *pons asinorum*, the "bridge of donkeys".

</Example>

<Example title="Opposite sides of a parallelogram">

A **parallelogram** is a quadrilateral $ABCD$ with $AB \parallel DC$ and $AD \parallel BC$.

**Claim.** In a parallelogram, $AB = DC$ and $AD = BC$.

**Proof.** Draw the diagonal $AC$. Since $AB \parallel DC$ and $AC$ is a transversal, the alternate angles are equal: $\angle BAC = \angle DCA$. Since $AD \parallel BC$, also $\angle DAC = \angle BCA$. The side $AC$ is shared. By ASA, $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA$, so $AB = CD$ and $BC = DA$. $\blacksquare$

The step "alternate angles are equal" uses the **parallel postulate**; see the next section.

</Example>

<Example title="The carpenter's diagonal test">

**Claim.** If a parallelogram $ABCD$ has equal diagonals, $AC = BD$, then it is a rectangle.

**Proof.** Compare triangles $DAB$ and $CBA$. They share the side $AB$, $AD = BC$ (opposite sides of a parallelogram, just proved) and $BD = AC$ (given). By SSS, $\triangle DAB \cong \triangle CBA$, so $\angle DAB = \angle CBA$. But $AD \parallel BC$, so these two angles are co-interior angles on the transversal $AB$ and add up to $180^\circ$. Two equal angles with sum $180^\circ$ are each $90^\circ$. The opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal (by the congruence $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA$ above), so all four angles are right angles. $\blacksquare$

So the carpenter is right, *provided* her frame is a parallelogram — which it is, because she made opposite sides equal. (A quadrilateral that does not cross itself and has both pairs of opposite sides equal is a parallelogram: draw the diagonal $AC$, use SSS to get $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA$, and then the equal alternate angles $\angle BAC = \angle DCA$ give $AB \parallel DC$ by Proposition I.27 below.)

</Example>

<Example title="A non-example: SSA">

Two sides and an angle that is **not** between them do not determine a triangle. Put $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (4, 3)$, $C = (3, 0)$ and $C' = (5, 0)$. Then

- $AB = 5$ in both triangles $ABC$ and $ABC'$;
- $BC = \sqrt{1^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{10}$ and $BC' = \sqrt{1^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{10}$;
- the angle at $A$ is the same, because $C$ and $C'$ lie on the same ray from $A$.

But $AC = 3$ and $AC' = 5$, so the triangles are not congruent.

When exactly does this happen? Let the angle at $A$ be acute, let $AB = c$ and $BC = a$, and let $F$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to the line through $A$ and $C$, with $BF = h$. A point $X$ of that line has $BX = a$ exactly when $FX = \sqrt{a^2 - h^2}$, by Pythagoras. So there is no such point if $a \lt h$, one (namely $F$, giving a right angle at $C$) if $a = h$, and two, one on each side of $F$, if $a > h$. Both lie on the ray from $A$ exactly when $\sqrt{a^2 - h^2} \lt AF = \sqrt{c^2 - h^2}$, that is, when $a \lt c$. So SSA gives **two** different triangles exactly when $h \lt a \lt c$. If the angle at $A$ is right or obtuse, at most one point lies on the ray, so SSA does determine the triangle — a fact sometimes called the *hypotenuse–leg* (or RHS) criterion when the angle is right.

</Example>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Parallel lines and the angle sum">

The next theorem is proved *without* the parallel postulate. It rests on the **exterior angle inequality** (Euclid I.16): an exterior angle of a triangle is greater than each of the two interior angles not next to it. Euclid's proof of I.16 uses SAS and vertical angles, not the parallel postulate; we take it as known.

<Theorem name="Proposition (Euclid I.27)">

If a transversal makes equal alternate angles with two lines, the lines are parallel.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose the transversal crosses $\ell$ at $P$ and $m$ at $Q$, with equal alternate angles, and suppose $\ell$ and $m$ meet at a point $R$. Then $P$, $Q$, $R$ form a triangle. One of the two alternate angles is an interior angle of this triangle, and the other is an exterior angle at the other end of $PQ$. By the exterior angle inequality, the exterior angle is *greater* than that interior angle — but they are equal. This contradiction shows that $\ell$ and $m$ do not meet. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem (alternate angles)">

If two parallel lines are crossed by a transversal, then alternate angles are equal.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $\ell \parallel m$, and let the transversal cross them at $P$ and $Q$. Through $P$ draw the line $\ell'$ that makes an alternate angle with $m$ **equal** to the one at $Q$. By Proposition I.27, $\ell'$ is parallel to $m$. Now $\ell$ and $\ell'$ are both lines through $P$ parallel to $m$. By **Playfair's axiom**, there is only one, so $\ell = \ell'$, and the alternate angles made by $\ell$ are equal. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem (angle sum of a triangle)">

The angles of every triangle add up to $180^\circ$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let the triangle be $ABC$, with angles $\alpha$ at $A$, $\beta$ at $B$ and $\gamma$ at $C$. Through $C$ draw the line $\ell$ parallel to $AB$ (it exists, and is unique, by Playfair's axiom). The line $CA$ is a transversal of the parallel lines $\ell$ and $AB$, so the angle between $\ell$ and $CA$ on the side of $A$ equals $\alpha$ (alternate angles). In the same way, the angle between $\ell$ and $CB$ on the other side equals $\beta$. These two angles and $\gamma$ fill the straight angle at $C$ along $\ell$, so $\alpha + \gamma + \beta = 180^\circ$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Corollaries">

1. **Exterior angle theorem.** An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior angles not next to it.
2. **Polygons.** The interior angles of a convex polygon with $n$ sides add up to $(n - 2) \cdot 180^\circ$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

1. The exterior angle at $C$ and $\gamma$ lie on a straight line, so the exterior angle is $180^\circ - \gamma = \alpha + \beta$.
2. The diagonals from one vertex cut the polygon into $n - 2$ triangles, whose angles together make up exactly the angles of the polygon. Each triangle contributes $180^\circ$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Note>

The exterior angle *inequality* (I.16) holds without the parallel postulate; the exterior angle *theorem* (an equality) needs it. On a sphere, where lines are great circles and there are no parallels at all, the angles of a triangle add up to **more** than $180^\circ$. In the hyperbolic plane, where there are infinitely many parallels through a point, they add up to **less**. In fact, given the other axioms (including Archimedes' axiom about lengths), "every triangle has angle sum $180^\circ$" is **equivalent** to the parallel postulate.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="circles" kind="proof" title="Circles: the inscribed angle theorem">

<Theorem name="Inscribed angle theorem">

An inscribed angle is half the central angle subtending the same arc: if $A$, $B$, $C$ lie on a circle with centre $O$, and $B$ lies on the same side of the line $AC$ as $O$ (or $O$ lies on $AC$), then $\angle AOC = 2\,\angle ABC$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Case 1: the centre lies on a side of the inscribed angle*, say on $BC$, so $BC$ is a diameter. The triangle $OAB$ is isosceles, since $OA = OB$ (both are radii). By the isosceles triangle theorem, $\angle OAB = \angle OBA = \theta$, say. The angle $\angle AOC$ is an exterior angle of triangle $OAB$ (because $B$, $O$, $C$ lie on a line), so by the exterior angle theorem $\angle AOC = \theta + \theta = 2\theta = 2\,\angle ABC$.

*Case 2: the centre lies inside the angle* $\angle ABC$. Draw the diameter $BD$ through $O$. It splits both angles: $\angle ABC = \angle ABD + \angle DBC$ and $\angle AOC = \angle AOD + \angle DOC$. By Case 1, applied twice, $\angle AOD = 2\,\angle ABD$ and $\angle DOC = 2\,\angle DBC$. Adding gives $\angle AOC = 2\,\angle ABC$.

*Case 3: the centre lies outside the angle* $\angle ABC$ (but on the same side of $AC$ as $B$). Again draw the diameter $BD$. Now one angle is the *difference* of two, say $\angle ABC = \angle DBC - \angle DBA$ and $\angle AOC = \angle DOC - \angle DOA$. Subtracting the two instances of Case 1 gives the result. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

If $B$ is on the other side of $AC$ from $O$, the same argument shows that $\angle ABC$ is half the **reflex** central angle, $360^\circ - \angle AOC$. Three corollaries follow at once.

<Theorem name="Corollaries">

1. **Thales' theorem.** An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle: if $AC$ is a diameter and $B$ is on the circle, then $\angle ABC = 90^\circ$.
2. **Angles in the same segment.** Inscribed angles subtending the same arc are equal.
3. **Cyclic quadrilaterals.** If the four vertices of a quadrilateral $ABCD$ lie on a circle (in this order), its opposite angles add up to $180^\circ$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

1. The central angle is the straight angle $\angle AOC = 180^\circ$, so $\angle ABC = 90^\circ$.
2. Each is half of the same central angle.
3. $\angle ABC$ and $\angle ADC$ subtend the two arcs into which $A$ and $C$ cut the circle. Their central angles add up to $360^\circ$, so the inscribed angles add up to $180^\circ$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="circumcentre" kind="proof" title="The circumcentre">

<Theorem name="Lemma (the perpendicular bisector as a locus)">

A point $P$ is equidistant from $A$ and $B$ if and only if $P$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $M$ be the midpoint of $AB$. If $P = M$ there is nothing to prove, so let $P \ne M$.

If $P$ is on the perpendicular bisector, then triangles $PMA$ and $PMB$ have $MA = MB$, a common side $PM$, and right angles at $M$. By SAS they are congruent, so $PA = PB$.

Conversely, if $PA = PB$, then triangles $PMA$ and $PMB$ have three equal sides, so by SSS they are congruent. Hence $\angle PMA = \angle PMB$. These two angles lie on a straight line, so each is $90^\circ$, and $PM$ is the perpendicular bisector. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem (circumcentre)">

The three perpendicular bisectors of the sides of a triangle $ABC$ meet in a single point $O$, and the circle with centre $O$ through $A$ passes through $B$ and $C$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

First, the perpendicular bisectors of $AB$ and $BC$ meet. Suppose instead that they were parallel. The line $AB$ is perpendicular to the first bisector, so it crosses it, and therefore (by Playfair's axiom) it also crosses the second; by the alternate angles theorem it is perpendicular to the second bisector as well. So $AB$ and $BC$ would both be perpendicular to the second bisector. Two lines perpendicular to the same line are parallel or equal (Proposition I.27), and $AB$ and $BC$ share the point $B$, so they would be the same line: $A$, $B$, $C$ would be collinear, which is not a triangle. This step uses the parallel postulate.

Let $O$ be the meeting point. By the lemma, $OA = OB$ (as $O$ is on the bisector of $AB$) and $OB = OC$ (as $O$ is on the bisector of $BC$). So $OA = OC$, and by the lemma again $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $CA$. All three bisectors pass through $O$, and $OA = OB = OC$ is the radius of a circle through all three vertices. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

The parallel postulate really is needed here. In the hyperbolic plane there are triangles whose perpendicular bisectors never meet, so they have no circumcircle; in fact, given Hilbert's other axioms, "every triangle has a circumcircle" is equivalent to the parallel postulate. Farkas Bolyai, the father of János Bolyai, gave an attempted proof of the parallel postulate that relied, without noticing, on this equivalent statement.

<Note>

**The isosceles "proof", resolved.** The bisector of the angle at $A$ and the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ do meet — but, unless $AB = AC$, they meet on the **circumcircle**, at the midpoint of the arc $BC$ that does not contain $A$. That point is *outside* the triangle. When the figure is drawn correctly, one of the two feet of the perpendiculars from $P$ lands on a side and the other on an extended side, and the final step of the "proof" (adding equal pieces on one side, but subtracting them on the other) no longer gives $AB = AC$. A careful proof would have had to justify where $P$ lies, and it cannot.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Arguing from the diagram.** "It looks like $P$ is inside the triangle", "it looks like a right angle", "those lines look parallel". A diagram is a guide to thinking, not evidence. The "every triangle is isosceles" fallacy shows how a slightly wrong picture leads to a false theorem. Every step of a proof must follow from what is *given* and what has been *proved*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"SSA is a congruence criterion."** It is not: the angle must be *included* between the two sides. The example $A = (0,0)$, $B = (4,3)$, $C = (3,0)$, $C' = (5,0)$ gives two different triangles with the same two sides and the same non-included angle. (When the given angle is right or obtuse, SSA does pin down the triangle.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"AAA is a congruence criterion."** Equal angles give the same *shape*, not the same size: a triangle with sides $3, 4, 5$ and one with sides $6, 8, 10$ have the same angles. AAA is a criterion for **similarity**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Someone eventually proved the parallel postulate from Euclid's other axioms."** Nobody did, and nobody can: it is **independent** of them. The hyperbolic plane satisfies all the other axioms and violates it, so no proof from the other axioms can exist. Many historical "proofs" quietly assumed an equivalent statement, such as "the angle sum is $180^\circ$" or "every triangle has a circumcircle".

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"An inscribed angle is always half the central angle $\angle AOC$."** Only when $B$ and $O$ are on the same side of the chord $AC$. On the other side, the inscribed angle is half the *reflex* angle $360^\circ - \angle AOC$, which is why opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to $180^\circ$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="logic" title="Independence, models and a decidable theory">

A statement is **independent** of a set of axioms when neither it nor its negation can be proved from them. The standard way to show this is to build *models*: a structure satisfying all the axioms and the statement, and another satisfying all the axioms and its negation. The Euclidean plane is a model of Hilbert's axioms *with* the parallel postulate; Eugenio Beltrami (1868) and later Felix Klein and Henri Poincaré built models of the hyperbolic plane inside Euclidean geometry, satisfying the other axioms but *not* the parallel postulate. So hyperbolic geometry is consistent if Euclidean geometry is.

Logic says something even stronger about elementary geometry. Alfred Tarski showed (published 1948–1951) that the first-order theory of the Euclidean plane is **complete** and **decidable**: there is an algorithm that decides, for any statement in its language, whether it is a theorem. This does not contradict Gödel's incompleteness theorems, because elementary geometry cannot define the natural numbers. See <TopicLink id="math.found.first-order-logic">first-order logic</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.found.incompleteness">Gödel's incompleteness theorems</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Geometry is the study of what rigid motions preserve">

In 1872 Felix Klein proposed that a geometry is described by a **group of transformations**, and its theorems are the properties those transformations preserve (the *Erlangen programme*). For Euclidean geometry the group is the group of rigid motions (translations, rotations, reflections and their composites). Two triangles are congruent exactly when a rigid motion carries one onto the other, and SAS, ASA and SSS say which measurements are enough to determine a triangle up to rigid motion. Allowing dilations as well gives similarity geometry, in which AAA *is* a criterion. Spherical and hyperbolic geometry have their own groups of motions. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.transformation-geometry">transformation geometry</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.geom.non-euclidean-geometry">non-Euclidean geometry</TopicLink>.

On a sphere of radius $r$, a triangle with angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ (in radians) has area $r^2(\alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi)$ (Girard's theorem). The angle sum measures how curved the surface is — the first case of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem of <TopicLink id="math.geom.curves-surfaces">differential geometry</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Coordinates turn geometry into algebra">

With René Descartes's coordinates, every point is a pair of numbers, a line is a linear equation, and a circle is a quadratic equation. The circumcentre is the solution of two linear equations (one for each perpendicular bisector), and "the bisectors meet" becomes "the determinant is not zero", which fails exactly when $A$, $B$, $C$ are collinear. Lean's Mathlib uses precisely this algebraic model.

Algebra also answers questions that defeated the Greek geometers. A length constructible with straightedge and compass from a unit length satisfies a polynomial equation built from repeated square roots. Pierre Wantzel used this in 1837 to prove that a general angle cannot be trisected and a cube cannot be doubled with these tools. See <TopicLink id="math.algebra.fields-galois">fields and Galois theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Is space Euclidean?">

Euclidean geometry describes the space of everyday measurement superbly: surveyors, engineers and architects rely on it every day. But whether physical space obeys the parallel postulate is a question about the world, not about logic. In Einstein's general theory of relativity, mass curves space-time, and light passing near the Sun is deflected (first measured during the solar eclipse of 1919). Over the distances of a building or a city, the deviation from Euclidean geometry is far too small to detect with rulers. On the largest scales, measurements of the cosmic microwave background show that space is very nearly flat, as far as current precision can tell. See <TopicLink id="math.applied.relativity">the geometry of space-time</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Set-theoretic connection">

Geometric figures are sets of points. A circle is the set $\{P : OP = r\}$, and the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ is the set $\{P : PA = PB\}$ — that is exactly what our lemma says. Such a set described by a property is traditionally called a **locus**. Concurrency is a statement about intersections: the circumcentre theorem says that the intersection of the three perpendicular bisectors is a single point, $\{P : PA = PB\} \cap \{P : PB = PC\} = \{O\}$. Two lines are parallel when, as sets, they are disjoint (or, in Mathlib's convention, one is a translate of the other).

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Before Euclid.** Builders and scribes in Egypt, Mesopotamia, India and China used geometric facts long before anyone proved them. The Indian *Śulba-sūtras* (first millennium BCE) give constructions for fire altars, including the relation between the sides and diagonal of a rectangle. Greek tradition credits **Thales of Miletus** (6th century BCE) with the theorem on the angle in a semicircle.
- **Euclid's *Elements*** (Alexandria, about 300 BCE) arranged geometry as a chain of propositions deduced from definitions, postulates and common notions. It was translated into Arabic around 800 CE by al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf ibn Maṭar, and later revised by Isḥāq ibn Ḥunayn and Thābit ibn Qurra; Latin versions made from the Arabic spread through Europe. In 1607 **Xu Guangqi** and Matteo Ricci translated the first six books into Chinese (*Jihe yuanben*); the Chinese word for geometry, *jihe*, comes from this translation.
- **In China**, **Liu Hui** (3rd century CE) justified the geometry in *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* by cutting and rearranging figures.
- **The parallel postulate.** Proclus (5th century CE) already doubted that it should be a postulate. **Ibn al-Haytham** (around 1000), **ʿUmar al-Khayyām** (around 1077) and **Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī** (13th century) each tried to prove it, and in doing so proved theorems about quadrilaterals that reappeared in the work of Giovanni Saccheri (1733) and Johann Heinrich Lambert (written 1766).
- **Non-Euclidean geometry.** Carl Friedrich Gauss explored the consequences of denying the postulate but did not publish. **Nikolai Lobachevsky** (from 1829, in Kazan) and **János Bolyai** (1832) independently published a consistent geometry in which the postulate fails. Beltrami's models (1868) showed that it is exactly as consistent as Euclid's.
- **Modern foundations.** David Hilbert's *Grundlagen der Geometrie* (1899) gave a complete axiom system; Tarski proved the decidability of elementary geometry. Today, proof assistants such as Lean check geometric proofs line by line.

</Section>
