<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Knowing one graph means knowing many">

A student in Lagos and her cousin in Toronto compare the weather by video call. She thinks in degrees Celsius, he in Fahrenheit. The conversion

$$
F = \tfrac95\,C + 32
$$

is a function: one input, exactly one output. Its graph is a straight line. To convert back, he needs the **inverse** function $C = \tfrac59(F - 32)$, whose graph is the first line reflected in the diagonal. If the forecast says "$10$ degrees warmer tomorrow", that is another function, $C \mapsto C + 10$. Doing one function after another is **composition**.

This lesson is about seeing functions whole. If you know the graph of $y = x^2$, you know the graph of $y = 2(x - 3)^2 + 1$ without plotting a single new point. If you know the graph of $f$, you know the graph of $f^{-1}$ for free. And from a formula like $\dfrac{2x + 1}{x - 3}$ you will be able to predict where its graph shoots off to infinity and what it looks like far away.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="A graph is a portrait of a function">

In the lesson on <TopicLink id="math.found.functions">functions</TopicLink> we met a function as a rule that gives every input in its domain exactly one output. For real functions, we can draw all the pairs (input, output) at once as points $(x, f(x))$ in the plane. That picture is the **graph**.

The graph lets you *read off* properties at a glance:

- the **domain** is the shadow of the graph on the $x$-axis, and the **range** is its shadow on the $y$-axis;
- the **zeros** are where it meets the $x$-axis;
- **increasing** means the graph climbs as you move right;
- a vertical line meets the graph of a function **at most once** (one input, one output) — the *vertical line test*;
- a horizontal line meets it at most once exactly when the function is **injective** — the *horizontal line test* — and that, we will prove, is what an inverse needs.

<Narrator who="chloe">

A puzzle before we start. The graph of $y = (x - 2)^2$ has its lowest point at $x = 2$, to the *right* of the lowest point of $y = x^2$. But we *subtracted* $2$! Why does subtracting move things right? Keep it in mind; the proof below explains it.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="real function, natural domain, graph">

A **real function** $f$ assigns to each number $x$ in its **domain** $D \subseteq \R$ exactly one real number $f(x)$. When a function is given only by a formula, its **natural domain** is the set of all real $x$ for which the formula makes sense. The **graph** of $f$ is the set of points

$$
\operatorname{graph} f = \{\, (x, f(x)) : x \in D \,\} \subseteq \R \times \R.
$$

The **range** is the set of outputs $\{ f(x) : x \in D \}$.

</Definition>

For example, $\sqrt{x - 1}$ has natural domain $[1, \infty)$, $\dfrac{1}{x^2 - 4}$ has natural domain $\R \setminus \{-2, 2\}$, and every polynomial has natural domain $\R$.

<Definition term="increasing, even and odd">

$f$ is **strictly increasing** on a set $S$ if $x < y$ implies $f(x) < f(y)$ for all $x, y \in S$ (and **strictly decreasing** if it implies $f(x) > f(y)$). On a domain symmetric about $0$, $f$ is **even** if $f(-x) = f(x)$ for all $x$, and **odd** if $f(-x) = -f(x)$ for all $x$.

</Definition>

The graph of an even function is symmetric in the $y$-axis ($x^2$, $|x|$, $x^4 - 3x^2$). The graph of an odd function is symmetric under a half-turn about the origin ($x^3$, $1/x$, $x^3 - 5x$). Most functions, such as $x^2 + x$, are neither.

<Definition term="composition and inverse">

The **composite** $g \circ f$ is the function $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$. Its natural domain is the set of $x$ in the domain of $f$ with $f(x)$ in the domain of $g$. A function $g : B \to A$ is an **inverse** of $f : A \to B$ if

$$
g(f(x)) = x \text{ for all } x \in A \quad\text{and}\quad f(g(y)) = y \text{ for all } y \in B.
$$

We then write $g = f^{-1}$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="asymptotes and end behaviour (informal)">

The line $y = L$ is a **horizontal asymptote** of $f$ if $f(x)$ gets as close to $L$ as we like once $x$ is large enough (or once $x$ is negative enough); we write $f(x) \to L$ as $x \to \infty$ (or $-\infty$). The line $x = a$ is a **vertical asymptote** if $|f(x)|$ grows beyond every bound as $x$ approaches $a$ from at least one side. The **end behaviour** of $f$ describes what $f(x)$ does as $x \to \pm\infty$.

</Definition>

These last definitions use the idea of a limit informally. It is made precise in <TopicLink id="math.analysis.intro-calculus">calculus</TopicLink>, and the Lean proof below uses the precise version.

</Section>

<Section id="families" kind="examples" title="A gallery of function families">

<Example title="The basic shapes">

- **Linear** $y = mx + c$: a straight line with slope $m$ (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-relationships">linear relationships</TopicLink>).
- **Quadratic** $y = ax^2 + bx + c$: a parabola (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.quadratics">quadratics</TopicLink>).
- **Powers** $y = x^n$: for even $n$ U-shaped and even; for odd $n$ S-shaped, odd and strictly increasing.
- **Absolute value** $y = |x|$: a V with its corner at the origin; $|x| = x$ for $x \ge 0$ and $|x| = -x$ for $x < 0$.
- **Square root** $y = \sqrt{x}$ on $[0, \infty)$: half of a sideways parabola.
- **Reciprocal** $y = 1/x$ on $x \ne 0$: a hyperbola with asymptotes $x = 0$ and $y = 0$.

</Example>

<Example title="A piecewise function: a block tariff">

Many electricity and water utilities charge in blocks. Here is an illustrative tariff in the style used by several municipalities in southern Africa: the first $50$ kWh in a month cost $1.20$ per kWh and each further kWh costs $1.80$. The monthly bill for $x$ kWh is

$$
B(x) = \begin{cases} 1.2\,x, & 0 \le x \le 50, \\ 60 + 1.8\,(x - 50), & x > 50. \end{cases}
$$

The two pieces agree at $x = 50$ (both give $60$), so the graph has no jump, only a bend where the slope increases from $1.2$ to $1.8$.

</Example>

<Example title="A rational function">

$r(x) = \dfrac{2x + 1}{x - 3}$ has natural domain $x \ne 3$. Dividing, $r(x) = 2 + \dfrac{7}{x - 3}$. So:

- as $x \to \pm\infty$, $\tfrac{7}{x - 3} \to 0$ and $r(x) \to 2$: **horizontal asymptote** $y = 2$;
- as $x \to 3$, $\tfrac{7}{x - 3}$ becomes huge, positive just to the right of $3$ and negative just to the left: **vertical asymptote** $x = 3$;
- the **zero** is at $2x + 1 = 0$, so $x = -\tfrac12$; the $y$-intercept is $r(0) = -\tfrac13$.

Its graph is the hyperbola $y = 7/x$ stretched and moved: right by $3$ and up by $2$.

</Example>

<Example title="Holes are not asymptotes">

$\dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \dfrac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1} = x + 1$ for every $x \ne 1$. Its graph is the line $y = x + 1$ with the single point $(1, 2)$ missing — a *hole*, not an asymptote. A zero of the denominator gives a vertical asymptote only if it does not cancel against the numerator.

</Example>

<Example title="End behaviour of polynomials">

For large $|x|$ the leading term wins. For $p(x) = -2x^3 + 50x^2 + 7$,

$$
p(x) = -2x^3\left(1 - \frac{25}{x} - \frac{7}{2x^3}\right),
$$

and the bracket tends to $1$. So $p(x) \to -\infty$ as $x \to \infty$ and $p(x) \to +\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$, like $-2x^3$, even though $p(10) = 3007$ is positive.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="transformations" kind="examples" title="Transforming graphs">

Starting from the graph of $y = f(x)$:

| New function | What happens to the graph |
| --- | --- |
| $y = f(x) + k$ | moves **up** by $k$ |
| $y = f(x - h)$ | moves **right** by $h$ |
| $y = a\,f(x)$ | stretches vertically by the factor $\lvert a \rvert$ (and reflects in the $x$-axis if $a < 0$) |
| $y = f(bx)$ | squeezes horizontally by the factor $\lvert b \rvert$ (and reflects in the $y$-axis if $b < 0$) |

<Example title="Building y = −2|x − 1| + 3 from y = |x|">

Start with the V of $y = |x|$, corner at $(0, 0)$. Replace $x$ by $x - 1$: the corner moves right to $(1, 0)$. Multiply by $-2$: the V flips upside down and becomes twice as steep. Add $3$: the corner rises to $(1, 3)$. The result is an upside-down V with its peak at $(1, 3)$, crossing the $x$-axis where $|x - 1| = \tfrac32$, at $x = -\tfrac12$ and $x = \tfrac52$.

</Example>

<Example title="The order matters">

$y = f(2x - 6)$ is $y = f\big(2(x - 3)\big)$: squeeze by $2$ and shift right by $3$, **not** by $6$. Always factor out $b$ first to read off $h$.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Proofs">

<Theorem name="Transformation rule">

Let $b \ne 0$. A point $(x, y)$ lies on the graph of $y = f(x)$ if and only if the point $\left(\tfrac{x}{b} + h,\ a y + k\right)$ lies on the graph of $y = a\,f\big(b(X - h)\big) + k$. In particular ($a = b = 1$, $k = 0$), the graph of $y = f(x - h)$ is the graph of $f$ moved $h$ units to the right.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $g(X) = a f(b(X - h)) + k$. If $y = f(x)$ and $X = \tfrac{x}{b} + h$, then $b(X - h) = x$, so $g(X) = a f(x) + k = ay + k$: the moved point is on the graph of $g$. Conversely, every point $(X, g(X))$ of the graph of $g$ arises this way, from the point $(x, f(x))$ with $x = b(X - h)$. So the graph of $g$ is exactly the image of the graph of $f$ under the move.

For the shift: the output that $f$ produces at input $x_0$, the function $x \mapsto f(x - h)$ produces when $x - h = x_0$, that is, at $x = x_0 + h$ — **later** by $h$. That is why subtracting $h$ moves the graph to the right.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Inverses and bijections">

A function $f : A \to B$ has an inverse if and only if it is a bijection (injective and surjective). The inverse is then unique.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $g$ is an inverse and $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$, then $x_1 = g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) = x_2$, so $f$ is injective. Every $y \in B$ equals $f(g(y))$, so $f$ is surjective. Conversely, if $f$ is a bijection, each $y \in B$ is $f(x)$ for exactly one $x \in A$. Define $g(y)$ to be that $x$; then $g(f(x)) = x$ and $f(g(y)) = y$. If $g'$ is another inverse, then $g'(y) = g'(f(g(y))) = g(y)$ for every $y$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Strictly monotone functions are injective">

If $f$ is strictly increasing (or strictly decreasing) on a set $S$, then $f$ is injective on $S$. So $f$ has an inverse from $S$ onto its range $f(S)$, and if $f$ is strictly increasing so is $f^{-1}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $x \ne y$ in $S$, say $x < y$, then $f(x) < f(y)$, so $f(x) \ne f(y)$. For the inverse: let $y_1 < y_2$ be in $f(S)$ and suppose $f^{-1}(y_1) \ge f^{-1}(y_2)$. Since $f$ is increasing, $f(f^{-1}(y_1)) \ge f(f^{-1}(y_2))$, that is, $y_1 \ge y_2$, a contradiction.

</Proof>

This is how we invert functions that are not injective: **restrict the domain** to an interval where they are strictly monotone. $f(x) = x^2$ is not injective on $\R$ ($f(-3) = f(3)$), but it is strictly increasing on $[0, \infty)$, and there its inverse is $\sqrt{x}$.

<Theorem name="The graph of the inverse is a reflection">

If $g = f^{-1}$, then $(x, y)$ lies on the graph of $f$ if and only if $(y, x)$ lies on the graph of $g$. So the graph of $f^{-1}$ is the mirror image of the graph of $f$ in the line $y = x$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $y = f(x)$ then $g(y) = g(f(x)) = x$, so $(y, x)$ is on the graph of $g$. If $x = g(y)$ then $f(x) = f(g(y)) = y$. Finally, the reflection in the line $y = x$ is exactly the map $(x, y) \mapsto (y, x)$: the midpoint $\left(\tfrac{x + y}{2}, \tfrac{x + y}{2}\right)$ of the two points lies on the line, and the segment joining them has direction $(1, -1)$, perpendicular to the line.

</Proof>

<Example title="Finding an inverse by algebra">

To invert $f(x) = \dfrac{2x + 1}{x - 3}$ ($x \ne 3$), solve $y = \dfrac{2x + 1}{x - 3}$ for $x$:

$$
y(x - 3) = 2x + 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; xy - 2x = 3y + 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \frac{3y + 1}{y - 2} \quad (y \ne 2).
$$

So $f^{-1}(y) = \dfrac{3y + 1}{y - 2}$, defined for $y \ne 2$. The value $y = 2$, the horizontal asymptote of $f$, is not in the range of $f$, and it becomes the vertical asymptote of $f^{-1}$. Reflection swaps the roles of horizontal and vertical.

</Example>

<Theorem name="Even and odd parts">

Every function $f : \R \to \R$ can be written in **exactly one** way as $f = e + o$ with $e$ even and $o$ odd, namely

$$
e(x) = \frac{f(x) + f(-x)}{2}, \qquad o(x) = \frac{f(x) - f(-x)}{2}.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Existence:* $e(-x) = \tfrac{f(-x) + f(x)}{2} = e(x)$, $o(-x) = \tfrac{f(-x) - f(x)}{2} = -o(x)$, and $e(x) + o(x) = f(x)$.

*Uniqueness:* if $e_1 + o_1 = e_2 + o_2$, then $d = e_1 - e_2 = o_2 - o_1$ is both even and odd, so $d(x) = d(-x) = -d(x)$ and $d(x) = 0$ for every $x$.

</Proof>

For a polynomial, the even part collects the even powers and the odd part the odd powers: $x^3 + 2x^2 - x + 5$ splits as $(2x^2 + 5) + (x^3 - x)$.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"$f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{1}{f(x)}$."** The $-1$ in $f^{-1}$ means "undo $f$", not "one over". For $f(x) = x + 1$, $f^{-1}(x) = x - 1$, but $\dfrac{1}{f(x)} = \dfrac{1}{x + 1}$. At $x = 1$ these are $0$ and $\tfrac12$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$y = f(x - 2)$ moves the graph to the left."** It moves it **right**. The new function reaches each old output $2$ units later: at $x = 2$ it does what $f$ did at $0$. The transformation rule above proves it.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Composition is commutative."** With $f(x) = x + 1$ and $g(x) = x^2$, $(g \circ f)(x) = (x + 1)^2$ but $(f \circ g)(x) = x^2 + 1$; at $x = 1$ they give $4$ and $2$. Even the *domains* can differ: $\sqrt{x^2 - 4}$ is defined for $|x| \ge 2$, but $(\sqrt x)^2 - 4$ only for $x \ge 0$. See <TopicLink id="math.cat.composition">composition</TopicLink>.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A graph can never cross its horizontal asymptote."** It can — the asymptote describes the far-away behaviour only. $\dfrac{2x^2 + x}{x^2 + 1} \to 2$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, yet it equals $2$ at $x = 2$, where $2x^2 + x = 2x^2 + 2$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Every function has an inverse."** Only bijections do. $x^2$ on $\R$ has none, because $(-3)^2 = 3^2$. Its "inverse" $\sqrt{x}$ is the inverse of $x^2$ *restricted to* $[0, \infty)$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="sets" title="A function is its graph">

In set theory a function from $A$ to $B$ *is* a subset $G \subseteq A \times B$ such that every $a \in A$ appears in exactly one pair $(a, b) \in G$. The vertical line test is that definition drawn in the plane. The inverse of a bijection is obtained by swapping every pair, which is the reflection of the graph in the line $y = x$.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Transformations form a group">

The moves $(x, y) \mapsto \left(\tfrac{x}{b} + h,\ ay + k\right)$ with $a, b \ne 0$ are transformations of the plane. Doing two of them in a row gives another of the same kind, each can be undone by another, and "do nothing" ($a = b = 1$, $h = k = 0$) is one of them. So they form a **group** acting on graphs. Felix Klein's *Erlangen programme* (1872) proposed studying a geometry through the group of transformations that preserve it (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.transformation-geometry">transformation geometry</TopicLink>). From this point of view, $y = x^2$ and $y = 2(x - 3)^2 + 1$ are "the same shape" because one transformation in the group carries one graph to the other.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Continuity and inverses on intervals">

Topology explains *why* restricting to intervals works so well. A **continuous** function on an interval that is injective must be strictly monotone. The proof uses the intermediate value theorem: a continuous function that went up and then down would take some value twice. Moreover, the inverse of a continuous, strictly monotone function on an interval is again continuous. So the function is a *homeomorphism* between two intervals: a stretching of one line segment onto another without tearing. The bisection method in the computation section relies on the same intermediate value theorem.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Asymptotes are limits">

"The graph approaches $y = 2$" is shorthand for a precise statement: for every tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$ there is an $N$ such that $|r(x) - 2| < \varepsilon$ whenever $x > N$. For $r(x) = 2 + \tfrac{7}{x - 3}$ we can take $N = 3 + \tfrac{7}{\varepsilon}$. Analysts study such estimates systematically, and the end behaviour of polynomials ("the leading term wins") is the first example of comparing rates of growth (see <TopicLink id="math.analysis.intro-calculus">introductory calculus</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Graphs before functions.** Around 1350, **Nicole Oresme** in Paris drew diagrams in which the "intensity" of a quality (such as speed) was plotted as a height above a line representing time.
- **Coordinates.** In the 1630s **René Descartes** and **Pierre de Fermat** independently connected equations with curves, founding analytic geometry.
- **Functions as infinite expressions.** In Kerala in the 14th and 15th centuries, **Mādhava of Saṅgamagrāma** and his successors expressed the sine, cosine and arctangent as infinite series. These were among the earliest descriptions of functions by power series.
- **The word "function".** **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** used the word in the late 17th century for quantities attached to a curve. **Johann Bernoulli** (1718) and **Leonhard Euler** made it mean an expression built from a variable, and Euler introduced the notation $f(x)$ in the 1730s.
- **Arbitrary rules.** In the 19th century, **Nikolai Lobachevsky** and **Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet** (1830s) argued that a function may be *any* rule assigning outputs to inputs, given by a formula or not. That idea led to the set-theoretic definition used today.
