<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Reading the shape of a graph">

A graph of change, drawn in the <TopicLink id="math.geom.coordinate-plane">coordinate plane</TopicLink>, shows how one quantity depends on another — very often, how something changes over **time**. Read it from left to right, like a sentence:

- **Going up** (segments A and C): the distance from home is increasing — Keza is riding away.
- **Flat** (B and D): the distance is not changing — she has stopped.
- **Going down** (E): the distance is decreasing — she is coming back.

The **steepness** tells us how fast the quantity changes. Segment E is the steepest: she covers $6$ km in $20$ minutes on the way home, faster than on the way out. (Perhaps the road home is downhill — but the graph does not show hills!)

<Narrator who="chloe">

A distance–time graph is not a picture of the road. Segment E goes *down*, but Keza was not riding downhill on the graph — she was getting closer to home. If she rode up a steep hill slowly, the graph would rise *gently*. Always ask: what is on each axis?

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Rates of change">

<Definition term="average rate of change">

If a quantity has the value $y(a)$ at $x = a$ and $y(b)$ at $x = b$, with $a \neq b$, its **average rate of change** between $a$ and $b$ is

$$
\frac{y(b) - y(a)}{b - a} = \frac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}.
$$

On the graph, it is the **slope** of the straight segment joining the two points (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-relationships">linear relationships</TopicLink>). Its units are "units of $y$ per unit of $x$": kilometres per minute, degrees per hour, litres per second. A rate is a ratio of two changes (see <TopicLink id="math.number.ratios-proportions">ratios and rates</TopicLink>).

</Definition>

<Definition term="increasing, decreasing, constant">

On an interval of $x$-values, a graph is

- **increasing** if $y$ gets larger whenever $x$ gets larger: $x_1 < x_2$ implies $y(x_1) < y(x_2)$;
- **decreasing** if $y$ gets smaller whenever $x$ gets larger: $x_1 < x_2$ implies $y(x_1) > y(x_2)$;
- **constant** if $y$ has the same value at every $x$ in the interval.

</Definition>

<Definition term="average speed">

For a journey, the **average speed** is the total distance travelled divided by the total time taken.

</Definition>

If a graph shows the distance from a starting point along a route, the slope of a segment is the average rate of change of that distance. As long as the traveller does not turn round during the segment, the **size** of the slope is the average speed, and its **sign** says whether they are moving away ($+$) or coming back ($-$). If the graph shows the *total* distance travelled, it can never go down, and its slopes are exactly the average speeds.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Rates on Keza's journey">

| segment | time | change in distance | average rate | in km/h |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $0$–$20$ min | $+4$ km | $\tfrac{4}{20} = 0.2$ km/min | $12$ |
| B | $20$–$25$ min | $0$ km | $0$ km/min | $0$ |
| C | $25$–$35$ min | $+2$ km | $\tfrac{2}{10} = 0.2$ km/min | $12$ |
| D | $35$–$55$ min | $0$ km | $0$ km/min | $0$ |
| E | $55$–$75$ min | $-6$ km | $\tfrac{-6}{20} = -0.3$ km/min | $-18$ |

(To turn km per minute into km per hour, multiply by $60$.) A and C have the same slope, so she rode at the same average speed on both; E is steepest, $18$ km/h, and the minus sign records that she was heading home.

</Example>

<Example title="The whole trip: zero change, but not nothing">

Over the whole trip, from $0$ to $75$ minutes, the distance from home changes by $0 - 0 = 0$, so the average rate of change is $0$ km/h. But Keza rode $4 + 2 + 6 = 12$ km in $75$ minutes $= 1.25$ hours: her **average speed** was $\tfrac{12}{1.25} = 9.6$ km/h. A zero average rate of change does not mean nothing happened — only that she ended where she started.

</Example>

<Example title="A table: a hot day in Delhi">

Air temperatures on a hot day in Delhi (illustrative numbers):

| time | 6 a.m. | 9 a.m. | noon | 3 p.m. | 6 p.m. | 9 p.m. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| temperature (°C) | $29$ | $33$ | $38$ | $40$ | $37$ | $33$ |

From 6 a.m. to noon the average rate of change is $\tfrac{38 - 29}{6} = 1.5$ °C per hour. From noon to 6 p.m. it is $\tfrac{37 - 38}{6} = -\tfrac{1}{6}$ °C per hour: nearly zero, even though the temperature rose to $40$ °C and fell again in between. From 3 p.m. to 9 p.m. it is $\tfrac{33 - 40}{6} \approx -1.17$ °C per hour. The table only tells us the values at six moments; what happened between them is a guess.

</Example>

<Example title="Sketching from a description">

*"The bath fills steadily for 8 minutes. Then the tap is turned off, and Tomás gets in — the water level jumps up. He lies still for 10 minutes, gets out, and pulls the plug; the bath empties steadily in 4 minutes."*

A sketch of water depth against time: a straight rising segment for 8 minutes; a short, very steep rise when Tomás gets in; a flat segment for 10 minutes; a short, very steep drop when he gets out (back to the depth before he got in); then a straight falling segment down to $0$, steeper than the filling segment because emptying takes $4$ minutes rather than $8$. The *heights* come from the story's quantities; the *steepness* from how fast things happen.

</Example>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="What rates of change can tell us">

Let $y$ be a quantity that depends on $x$, and write $R(a, b)$ for its average rate of change between $a$ and $b$.

<Theorem name="change = rate × time">

For $a \neq b$: $\;y(b) - y(a) = R(a, b) \cdot (b - a)$. On a distance–time graph: distance covered $=$ average speed $\times$ time taken.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Multiply the definition $R(a, b) = \dfrac{y(b) - y(a)}{b - a}$ by $b - a$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="the sign of the rate">

A graph is increasing on an interval if and only if every average rate of change $R(x_1, x_2)$ with $x_1 < x_2$ in the interval is positive. Likewise, it is constant on the interval if and only if all these average rates are $0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take $x_1 < x_2$. The run $x_2 - x_1$ is positive, so the quotient $\dfrac{y(x_2) - y(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1}$ is positive exactly when the rise $y(x_2) - y(x_1)$ is positive, that is, exactly when $y(x_1) < y(x_2)$. "Increasing" says this for every such pair, and so does "all rates are positive". In the same way, the quotient is $0$ exactly when $y(x_1) = y(x_2)$, so all the rates are $0$ exactly when $y$ never changes.

</Proof>

So a horizontal segment really does mean "not changing", and a straight segment that slopes upwards means steady increase. (Decreasing works the same way, with negative rates.)

Next: how do the rates over two pieces of an interval combine? Not by simply averaging them.

<Theorem name="combining intervals">

For $a < b < c$:

$$
R(a, c) = \frac{(b - a)\,R(a, b) + (c - b)\,R(b, c)}{c - a}.
$$

The rate over the whole interval is the average of the two rates, **weighted by the lengths** of the pieces. In particular, $R(a, c)$ lies between $R(a, b)$ and $R(b, c)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The total change is the sum of the changes on the two pieces, and by "change = rate × time":

$$
y(c) - y(a) = \big(y(b) - y(a)\big) + \big(y(c) - y(b)\big) = (b - a)R(a, b) + (c - b)R(b, c).
$$

Dividing by $c - a$ gives the formula. Now suppose $R(a, b) \leq R(b, c)$ (otherwise swap the roles). Replacing $R(b, c)$ by the smaller number $R(a, b)$ makes the right-hand side no larger, so $(c - a)R(a, c) \geq (b - a)R(a, b) + (c - b)R(a, b) = (c - a)R(a, b)$. Dividing by $c - a > 0$ gives $R(a, c) \geq R(a, b)$. Replacing $R(a, b)$ by the larger $R(b, c)$ shows $R(a, c) \leq R(b, c)$ in the same way.

</Proof>

For Keza's first $35$ minutes: $R(0, 35) = \dfrac{20 \cdot 12 + 5 \cdot 0 + 10 \cdot 12}{35} = \dfrac{72}{7} \approx 10.3$ km/h — between $0$ and $12$, as it must be, but not their plain average.

<Theorem name="same average, different journeys">

Two quantities can have the same average rate of change over an interval but behave differently within it.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

One example is enough. Ana runs $100$ m at a steady $5$ m/s, so she is $5t$ metres along after $t$ seconds. Ben runs at $7$ m/s for $10$ seconds and then, tiring, at $3$ m/s. Both finish $100$ m in $20$ s, so both average $5$ m/s. But after $10$ s Ana has run $50$ m and Ben $70$ m.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="round trips">

If you travel a distance $d$ at speed $u$ and return at speed $v$ (with $d, u, v > 0$), your average speed is

$$
\frac{2d}{\frac{d}{u} + \frac{d}{v}} = \frac{2uv}{u + v},
$$

which is never more than $\tfrac{u + v}{2}$, and equals it only when $u = v$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The total distance is $2d$, and the times for the two legs are $\tfrac{d}{u}$ and $\tfrac{d}{v}$. Multiplying the top and bottom of $\tfrac{2d}{d/u + d/v}$ by $\tfrac{uv}{d}$ gives $\tfrac{2uv}{v + u}$. For the comparison,

$$
\frac{u + v}{2} - \frac{2uv}{u + v} = \frac{(u + v)^2 - 4uv}{2(u + v)} = \frac{(u - v)^2}{2(u + v)},
$$

which is never negative (a square is never negative) and is $0$ only when $u = v$.

</Proof>

The classic puzzle: drive $60$ km at $30$ km/h ($2$ hours) and back at $60$ km/h ($1$ hour). The average speed is $\tfrac{120}{3} = 40$ km/h — not $45$. You spend **more time** at the slower speed, so it counts for more.

</Section>

<Section id="instantaneous" kind="human" title="From average to instantaneous">

A stone dropped from a cliff falls faster and faster. In the idealised model with no air resistance, it has fallen $d(t) = 4.9\,t^2$ metres after $t$ seconds. The graph is **curved**, so its average rate of change depends on the interval we choose. What is the stone's speed *at* the moment $t = 2$?

Take a short interval from $2$ to $2 + h$ seconds (with $h \neq 0$). The average speed over it is

$$
\frac{4.9(2 + h)^2 - 4.9 \cdot 2^2}{h} = \frac{4.9\,(4h + h^2)}{h} = 4.9\,(4 + h) = 19.6 + 4.9\,h \;\text{ m/s}.
$$

As $h$ gets smaller and smaller — from either side — this gets as close as we like to $19.6$ m/s. That number is the stone's **instantaneous speed** at $t = 2$: what a speedometer would read. On the graph, the segments become the **tangent line**. Making this idea precise is the starting point of calculus (see <TopicLink id="math.analysis.intro-calculus">introductory calculus</TopicLink>).

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"The graph is a picture of the journey."** A distance–time graph that rises and falls is not a hill. Rising means moving away, falling means coming back, and a cyclist climbing a real hill slowly makes the graph rise *gently*. Always read the axes first.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Steeper means bigger."** Steepness measures how **fast** a quantity changes, not how large it is. Town B's graph is steeper than town A's, but A has more people until year $10$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The average speed of a round trip is the average of the two speeds."** It is total distance divided by total time: $30$ km/h out and $60$ km/h back gives $40$ km/h, not $45$, because more time is spent at the slower speed.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"An average rate of $0$ means nothing changed."** Keza's distance from home has average rate $0$ over the whole trip, yet she rode $12$ km. The average only compares the start and the end.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A flat line always means stopped."** On a *distance*–time graph, yes. On a *speed*–time graph, a flat line means moving at a **constant speed**. The same shape tells different stories on different axes.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Velocity, and distance as area">

In physics, the slope of a position–time graph is **velocity** (speed with a direction, so it can be negative), and the slope of a velocity–time graph is **acceleration**. The reverse also holds: the **area** under a velocity–time graph is the change in position. For the falling stone, the velocity grows steadily, $v = 9.8\,t$; the area under that straight line from $0$ to $t$ is a triangle, $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot t \cdot 9.8t = 4.9\,t^2$ — exactly the distance fallen. Galileo found by experiment that distances fallen grow like the square of the time, and gave a geometric argument of this kind (*Two New Sciences*, 1638).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Limits and the mean value theorem">

The instantaneous rate of change — the limit of average rates over shrinking intervals — is the **derivative**, the central idea of calculus. A beautiful theorem connects the two kinds of rate. **Mean value theorem:** if a quantity $y$ depends continuously on $x$ for $a \leq x \leq b$ and has an instantaneous rate of change at every $x$ strictly between $a$ and $b$, then at some moment $c$ between them the instantaneous rate equals the average rate $R(a, b)$. If you drive $120$ km in $2$ hours, at some instant your speedometer read exactly $60$ km/h. (The hypotheses matter: a graph with a jump or a corner can avoid its average slope.)

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Slope as direction">

Geometrically, an average rate of change is the slope of a **chord** joining two points of a graph, and the instantaneous rate is the slope of the **tangent** line, the line that touches the curve at one point and points in the curve's direction there. A graph is straight exactly when all its chords have the same slope, and it curves upwards when the slopes of its chords increase as we move to the right — as for the falling stone, whose average speeds in successive seconds are $4.9$, $14.7$, $24.5$, $34.3$ m/s.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Babylonian** astronomers, on clay tablets from between about 350 and 50 BCE, computed how far the planet Jupiter moves along the sky by a calculation that amounts to finding the area of a trapezium under a graph of its daily motion against time — an "area under a velocity–time graph", more than a millennium before such figures were drawn in Europe (as Mathieu Ossendrijver showed in 2016).
- In India, **Bhāskara II** (12th century) distinguished a planet's average daily motion from its *tātkālika gati*, its motion at a particular instant — an early idea of instantaneous rate of change.
- In 14th-century Europe, scholars at Merton College, Oxford, studied "uniformly difform" (steadily accelerating) motion, and **Nicole Oresme** in Paris drew such motion as a figure: time along a base line and speed as heights above it. With it he gave a geometric proof of the Merton "mean speed" rule: a steadily accelerating body covers the same distance as one moving the whole time at its middle speed.
- **Galileo Galilei**'s *Two New Sciences* (1638) used such figures to analyse falling bodies, and **William Playfair**'s *Commercial and Political Atlas* (1786) popularised line graphs of quantities changing over time, such as a country's trade year by year.

</Section>

- I. M. Gelfand, E. G. Glagoleva and E. E. Shnol, *Functions and Graphs* (English edition 1990) — how to read and sketch graphs, from a great teacher.
- Edward R. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983) — on drawing graphs that tell the truth.
- Mathieu Ossendrijver, "Ancient Babylonian astronomers calculated Jupiter's position from the area under a time-velocity graph", *Science* 351 (2016).
