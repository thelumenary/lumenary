<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="How many primes are there below a million?">

Euclid proved that the primes never run out. But how thinly are they spread? Around 1792, the teenage Carl Friedrich Gauss counted the primes in blocks of a thousand from tables and observed that near $x$ about one number in $\log x$ is prime (natural logarithm). So the number of primes up to $x$ should be roughly
$$
\pi(x) \approx \frac{x}{\log x} \qquad\text{or, better,}\qquad \pi(x) \approx \operatorname{li}(x) = \int_0^x \frac{dt}{\log t}.
$$
For $x = 10^6$: $\pi(x) = 78498$, $x/\log x \approx 72382$, and $\operatorname{li}(x) \approx 78628$. Proving that $\pi(x)/(x/\log x) \to 1$ — the **prime number theorem** — took a century and a new idea: Bernhard Riemann's 1859 memoir, which studied the primes through the complex function
$$
\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_{p} \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}} \qquad (\operatorname{Re} s > 1),
$$
and showed that the distribution of primes is governed by the zeros of $\zeta$. The theorem was proved in 1896 by Jacques Hadamard and Charles de la Vallée Poussin. Riemann also conjectured that all the non-trivial zeros lie on the line $\operatorname{Re} s = \tfrac12$; this **Riemann hypothesis** is still open, and it is equivalent to the statement that $\pi(x) = \operatorname{li}(x) + O(\sqrt x \log x)$ — the primes are as regular as they could possibly be.

**Analytic number theory** answers arithmetic questions with analysis: generating functions (Dirichlet series), complex integration, Fourier analysis and estimates. This lesson assumes <TopicLink id="math.number.elementary-number-theory">elementary number theory</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.analysis.complex-analysis">complex analysis</TopicLink> (holomorphic functions, analytic continuation, the residue theorem).

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Three ideas">

1. **Unique factorisation is an Euler product.** Expanding $\prod_p (1 + p^{-s} + p^{-2s} + \cdots)$ produces each $n^{-s}$ exactly once, because each $n$ factors uniquely into primes. So the analytic properties of $\zeta(s)$ — a pole at $s = 1$, where $\sum 1/n$ diverges — say something about primes: there must be infinitely many, and $\sum_p 1/p$ must diverge.
2. **Logarithmic derivatives count primes.** Taking $-\frac{d}{ds}\log$ of the Euler product gives $-\zeta'(s)/\zeta(s) = \sum_n \Lambda(n) n^{-s}$, where $\Lambda(n) = \log p$ if $n = p^k$ and $0$ otherwise. The poles of $-\zeta'/\zeta$ are the pole and the zeros of $\zeta$, and complex integration (Perron's formula) turns them into terms of $\psi(x) = \sum_{n \leq x}\Lambda(n)$: the pole at $s = 1$ gives the main term $x$, and each zero $\rho$ gives a term $-x^\rho/\rho$.
3. **Zeros are frequencies.** If $\rho = \beta + i\gamma$, then $x^\rho = x^\beta e^{i\gamma\log x}$ oscillates in $\log x$ with frequency $\gamma$ and amplitude $x^\beta$. The primes are the "sound" and the zeros its "spectrum" (an analogy; the precise statement is von Mangoldt's explicit formula, Theorem 9). Zeros with $\beta = 1$ would destroy the prime number theorem; zeros with $\beta = \frac12$ give the smallest possible error terms.

A heuristic that gets many predictions right is **Cramér's model**: pretend each $n \geq 3$ is prime independently with probability $1/\log n$. It predicts $\pi(x) \approx \operatorname{li}(x)$ with fluctuations of size about $\sqrt x$ (up to logarithms) — consistent with the Riemann hypothesis. It is a model, not a theorem, and it is known to be wrong in some details (Maier, 1985).

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="arithmetic functions and Dirichlet convolution">

An **arithmetic function** is a map $f : \N_{\geq 1} \to \C$. The **Dirichlet convolution** is $(f * g)(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d)\, g(n/d)$. The function $f$ is **multiplicative** if $f(mn) = f(m)f(n)$ whenever $\gcd(m, n) = 1$ (and $f(1) = 1$), and **completely multiplicative** if this holds for all $m, n$. Important examples: $\varepsilon(n) = [n = 1]$ (the identity for $*$), $\mathbf{1}(n) = 1$, $\operatorname{id}(n) = n$, $\log(n)$, Euler's $\varphi$, the divisor function $d = \mathbf{1} * \mathbf{1}$, the **Möbius function** $\mu(n) = (-1)^k$ if $n$ is a product of $k$ distinct primes and $\mu(n) = 0$ if $n$ has a square factor, and the **von Mangoldt function** $\Lambda(n) = \log p$ if $n = p^k$ ($k \geq 1$) and $0$ otherwise.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Dirichlet series">

The **Dirichlet series** of $f$ is $D(f, s) = \sum_{n \geq 1} f(n)\, n^{-s}$, $s = \sigma + it$. If it converges absolutely at $s_0$, it converges absolutely and uniformly on $\operatorname{Re} s \geq \operatorname{Re} s_0$ and defines a holomorphic function on $\operatorname{Re} s > \operatorname{Re} s_0$. The **Riemann zeta function** is $\zeta(s) = D(\mathbf{1}, s)$ for $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="prime-counting functions">

$\pi(x) = \#\{p \leq x\}$, $\theta(x) = \sum_{p \leq x}\log p$, $\psi(x) = \sum_{n \leq x}\Lambda(n) = \sum_{p^k \leq x}\log p$, and $\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_0^x dt/\log t$ (principal value at $t = 1$). We write $f \sim g$ if $f/g \to 1$, $f = O(g)$ or $f \ll g$ if $|f| \leq Cg$ for some constant $C$, and $f \asymp g$ if $f \ll g \ll f$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Dirichlet characters and L-functions">

A **Dirichlet character** modulo $q$ is a homomorphism $\chi : (\Z/q\Z)^\times \to \C^\times$, extended to $\Z$ by $\chi(n) = \chi(n \bmod q)$ if $\gcd(n, q) = 1$ and $\chi(n) = 0$ otherwise. It is completely multiplicative and periodic. The **principal** character $\chi_0$ is $1$ on all $n$ coprime to $q$; there are $\varphi(q)$ characters modulo $q$. The **Dirichlet $L$-function** is $L(s, \chi) = \sum_n \chi(n) n^{-s} = \prod_p (1 - \chi(p)p^{-s})^{-1}$ for $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Examples">

<Example title="Dirichlet series of familiar functions">

For $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$ (or $> 2$ where noted), Proposition 2 below gives
$$
\sum_n \frac{\mu(n)}{n^s} = \frac{1}{\zeta(s)}, \qquad \sum_n \frac{d(n)}{n^s} = \zeta(s)^2, \qquad \sum_n \frac{\varphi(n)}{n^s} = \frac{\zeta(s - 1)}{\zeta(s)}\ (\operatorname{Re} s > 2), \qquad \sum_n \frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^s} = -\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)},
$$
from the convolution identities $\mu * \mathbf{1} = \varepsilon$, $d = \mathbf{1} * \mathbf{1}$, $\varphi * \mathbf{1} = \operatorname{id}$ and $\Lambda * \mathbf{1} = \log$ (formalised below). For instance $\Lambda * \mathbf{1} = \log$ says $\sum_{d \mid 12}\Lambda(d) = \Lambda(2) + \Lambda(3) + \Lambda(4) = 2\log 2 + \log 3 = \log 12$.

</Example>

<Example title="Characters modulo 4 and modulo 5">

Modulo $4$ there are two characters: $\chi_0$ and $\chi_4$ with $\chi_4(1) = 1$, $\chi_4(3) = -1$. Then
$$
L(1, \chi_4) = 1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \frac17 + \cdots = \frac{\pi}{4},
$$
the series of Mādhava and Leibniz. Modulo $5$, $(\Z/5)^\times$ is cyclic of order $4$, generated by $2$, and a character is determined by $\chi(2) \in \{1, i, -1, -i\}$: two characters are real ($\chi_0$ and the Legendre symbol $\left(\frac{\cdot}{5}\right)$) and two are complex conjugates.

| $n \bmod 5$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\chi_0$ | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $\chi_1$ | $1$ | $i$ | $-i$ | $-1$ |
| $\chi_2 = \left(\frac{\cdot}{5}\right)$ | $1$ | $-1$ | $-1$ | $1$ |
| $\chi_3 = \overline{\chi_1}$ | $1$ | $-i$ | $i$ | $-1$ |

</Example>

<Example title="Special values of ζ">

Euler showed $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$ (the Basel problem, 1734) and more generally $\zeta(2k) = (-1)^{k+1}B_{2k}(2\pi)^{2k}/(2(2k)!)$ with Bernoulli numbers $B_{2k}$. The continued function also has values at negative integers: $\zeta(0) = -\tfrac12$, $\zeta(-1) = -\tfrac{1}{12}$, and $\zeta(-2k) = 0$ for $k \geq 1$ (the **trivial zeros**). These are values of the analytic continuation — the series $\sum n^{-s}$ diverges for $\operatorname{Re} s \leq 1$. The first non-trivial zero is $\rho_1 = \tfrac12 + 14.134725\ldots\, i$.

</Example>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="The main theorems">

<Theorem name="Proposition 1 (the ring of arithmetic functions; Möbius inversion)">

Under pointwise addition and Dirichlet convolution, arithmetic functions form a commutative ring with identity $\varepsilon$; $f$ is invertible if and only if $f(1) \neq 0$. Convolutions of multiplicative functions are multiplicative. Moreover $\mu * \mathbf{1} = \varepsilon$, that is, $\sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d) = 0$ for $n > 1$, and consequently
$$
g(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d) \ \text{ for all } n \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(n) = \sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d)\, g(n/d) \ \text{ for all } n.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Commutativity and associativity follow from writing $(f * g)(n) = \sum_{ab = n} f(a)g(b)$ and $((f * g) * h)(n) = \sum_{abc = n} f(a)g(b)h(c)$; distributivity and $f * \varepsilon = f$ are immediate. If $f(1) \neq 0$, the equations $(f * g)(1) = 1$ and $(f * g)(n) = 0$ for $n > 1$ determine $g(1) = 1/f(1)$ and then, recursively, $g(n) = -\frac{1}{f(1)}\sum_{d \mid n, d > 1} f(d)g(n/d)$. If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, divisors of $mn$ are uniquely products $d_1 d_2$ with $d_1 \mid m$, $d_2 \mid n$, which gives multiplicativity of $f * g$. Since $\mu * \mathbf{1}$ is multiplicative, it suffices to check prime powers: $\sum_{d \mid p^k}\mu(d) = 1 - 1 + 0 + \cdots = 0$ for $k \geq 1$. Finally, if $g = f * \mathbf{1}$ then $g * \mu = f * (\mathbf{1} * \mu) = f * \varepsilon = f$, and conversely if $f = g * \mu$ then $f * \mathbf{1} = g$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Proposition 2 (Dirichlet series multiply)">

If $D(f, s)$ and $D(g, s)$ converge absolutely, then so does $D(f * g, s)$, and $D(f * g, s) = D(f, s)\, D(g, s)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$D(f, s)D(g, s) = \sum_{a, b} f(a)g(b)(ab)^{-s}$ is an absolutely convergent double series, so it may be summed in any order; grouping the terms with $ab = n$ gives $\sum_n (f * g)(n) n^{-s}$, and the same grouping of $\sum|f(a)||g(b)||(ab)^{-s}|$ bounds $\sum_n |(f * g)(n)||n^{-s}|$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (Euler product; Euler's theorem on Σ 1/p)">

For $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$, $\zeta(s) = \prod_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}$, the product converging absolutely; in particular $\zeta(s) \neq 0$ there. More generally $D(f, s) = \prod_p \big(\sum_{k \geq 0} f(p^k)p^{-ks}\big)$ for multiplicative $f$ with $D(|f|, \sigma)$ convergent. Consequently $\sum_p 1/p = \infty$, and there are infinitely many primes.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Fix $s$ with $\sigma = \operatorname{Re} s > 1$ and let $P(N) = \prod_{p \leq N}(1 - p^{-s})^{-1} = \prod_{p \leq N}\sum_{k \geq 0} p^{-ks}$. Multiplying out finitely many absolutely convergent series, $P(N) = \sum_{n \in S_N} n^{-s}$ where $S_N$ is the set of $n$ all of whose prime factors are $\leq N$ — each such $n$ appears exactly once by unique factorisation. Every $n \leq N$ is in $S_N$, so $|\zeta(s) - P(N)| \leq \sum_{n > N} n^{-\sigma} \to 0$. The product converges absolutely because $\sum_p |p^{-s}| < \infty$, and a convergent product of non-zero factors with this property is non-zero. The same argument works for multiplicative $f$.

For the divergence, let $1 < \sigma < 2$. Taking logarithms, $\log\zeta(\sigma) = \sum_p \sum_{k \geq 1}\frac{p^{-k\sigma}}{k} = \sum_p p^{-\sigma} + R(\sigma)$, where $0 \leq R(\sigma) \leq \sum_p \sum_{k \geq 2} p^{-k} = \sum_p \frac{1}{p(p - 1)} < 1$. Since $\zeta(\sigma) \geq \int_1^\infty x^{-\sigma}\,dx = \frac{1}{\sigma - 1} \to \infty$ as $\sigma \to 1^+$, $\sum_p p^{-\sigma}$ is unbounded, so $\sum_p p^{-1} = \infty$ by monotone convergence.

</Proof>

Differentiating the logarithm of the Euler product termwise gives $-\zeta'(s)/\zeta(s) = \sum_p \sum_k (\log p)\, p^{-ks} = \sum_n \Lambda(n) n^{-s}$, matching $\Lambda * \mathbf{1} = \log$ and Proposition 2.

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (Chebyshev's bounds)">

For all $x \geq 1$ and integers $n \geq 1$:

1. $\prod_{p \leq n} p \leq 4^n$, hence $\theta(x) \leq x\log 4$;
2. $2^n \leq (n + 1)\operatorname{lcm}(1, 2, \dots, n)$, hence $\psi(n) \geq n\log 2 - \log(n + 1)$.

Consequently $\psi(x) \asymp x$, $\theta(x) \asymp x$ and $\pi(x) \asymp x/\log x$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

(1) Induction on $n$ (Erdős's argument). For $n \leq 2$ it is clear; for even $n > 2$ the product equals that for $n - 1$. For $n = 2m + 1$: every prime $p$ with $m + 1 < p \leq 2m + 1$ divides $\binom{2m+1}{m} = \frac{(2m+1)!}{m!\,(m+1)!}$ (it divides the numerator but not the denominator), and $\binom{2m+1}{m} = \binom{2m+1}{m+1}$ are two terms of $\sum_j\binom{2m+1}{j} = 2^{2m+1}$, so $\binom{2m+1}{m} \leq 4^m$. Hence $\prod_{p \leq 2m+1} p \leq \prod_{p \leq m+1}p \cdot \binom{2m+1}{m} \leq 4^{m+1}\cdot 4^m = 4^{2m+1}$. Taking logarithms gives $\theta(n) \leq n\log 4$, and $\theta(x) = \theta(\lfloor x \rfloor)$.

(2) The exponent of a prime $p$ in $\operatorname{lcm}(1, \dots, n)$ is the largest $k$ with $p^k \leq n$, so $\log\operatorname{lcm}(1, \dots, n) = \sum_{p^k \leq n}\log p = \psi(n)$. Each binomial coefficient $\binom{n}{j}$ divides $\operatorname{lcm}(1, \dots, n)$: by Kummer's theorem the exponent of $p$ in $\binom{n}{j}$ is the number of carries when adding $j$ and $n - j$ in base $p$, which is at most the number of base-$p$ digits of $n$ beyond the first, i.e. at most $\lfloor\log n/\log p\rfloor$. So $2^n = \sum_{j=0}^n \binom{n}{j} \leq (n + 1)\operatorname{lcm}(1, \dots, n)$; take logarithms.

For the consequences: $\theta \leq \psi$, and $\psi(x) - \theta(x) = \sum_{k \geq 2}\theta(x^{1/k}) \ll \sqrt x\log x$ (there are at most $\log_2 x$ non-zero terms, each $\leq \theta(\sqrt x) \leq \sqrt x\log 4$), so both are $\asymp x$. Then $\pi(x) \geq \theta(x)/\log x \gg x/\log x$, and $\theta(x) \geq \sum_{\sqrt x < p \leq x}\log p \geq \frac12\log x\,(\pi(x) - \sqrt x)$ gives $\pi(x) \ll x/\log x$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (analytic continuation to Re s > 0)">

For $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$,
$$
\zeta(s) = \frac{s}{s - 1} - s\int_1^\infty \frac{\{x\}}{x^{s+1}}\,dx,
$$
where $\{x\} = x - \lfloor x \rfloor$. The right-hand side is meromorphic on $\operatorname{Re} s > 0$ with a single simple pole, at $s = 1$, of residue $1$. So $\zeta$ continues meromorphically to $\operatorname{Re} s > 0$, and $\zeta(s) - \frac{1}{s - 1}$ is holomorphic there.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$, partial summation gives
$$
\sum_{n \geq 1} n^{-s} = \sum_{n \geq 1} n\big(n^{-s} - (n + 1)^{-s}\big) = \sum_{n \geq 1} n \cdot s\int_n^{n+1} x^{-s-1}\,dx = s\int_1^\infty \lfloor x \rfloor x^{-s-1}\,dx,
$$
(the boundary terms $N\cdot(N+1)^{-s} \to 0$). Writing $\lfloor x \rfloor = x - \{x\}$ and $s\int_1^\infty x^{-s}\,dx = \frac{s}{s - 1}$ gives the formula. Since $0 \leq \{x\} < 1$, the integral converges absolutely and locally uniformly on $\operatorname{Re} s > 0$, so it is holomorphic there. Finally $\frac{s}{s - 1} = 1 + \frac{1}{s - 1}$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 6 (the functional equation; proof sketched)">

The **completed zeta function** $\Lambda(s) = \pi^{-s/2}\,\Gamma(s/2)\,\zeta(s)$ extends to a meromorphic function on $\C$ whose only poles are simple poles at $s = 0$ and $s = 1$, and
$$
\Lambda(1 - s) = \Lambda(s).
$$
Hence $\zeta$ is meromorphic on $\C$ with a single simple pole at $s = 1$; $\zeta(-2k) = 0$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots$ (the poles of $\Gamma(s/2)$ must cancel); and all other zeros — the **non-trivial zeros** — lie in the **critical strip** $0 \leq \operatorname{Re} s \leq 1$, symmetric about the real axis and about the **critical line** $\operatorname{Re} s = \tfrac12$.

</Theorem>

*Proof sketch* (Riemann's second proof). Let $\vartheta(t) = \sum_{n \in \Z} e^{-\pi n^2 t}$ for $t > 0$. Poisson summation applied to the Gaussian $e^{-\pi x^2 t}$, whose Fourier transform is $t^{-1/2}e^{-\pi \xi^2/t}$, gives $\vartheta(1/t) = \sqrt{t}\,\vartheta(t)$. For $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$, $\int_0^\infty e^{-\pi n^2 t}t^{s/2}\,\frac{dt}{t} = \pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)n^{-s}$, so $\Lambda(s) = \int_0^\infty \frac{\vartheta(t) - 1}{2}\,t^{s/2}\,\frac{dt}{t}$. Splitting the integral at $t = 1$ and using $\vartheta(1/t) = \sqrt t\,\vartheta(t)$ on $(0, 1)$ gives
$$
\Lambda(s) = -\frac{1}{s} - \frac{1}{1 - s} + \int_1^\infty \frac{\vartheta(t) - 1}{2}\left(t^{s/2} + t^{(1-s)/2}\right)\frac{dt}{t},
$$
in which the integral is entire (the integrand decays like $e^{-\pi t}$) and the whole expression is visibly symmetric under $s \mapsto 1 - s$. Zeros of $\zeta$ in $\operatorname{Re} s < 0$ come from poles of $\Gamma(s/2)$ only, and $\zeta \neq 0$ on $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$ (Theorem 3), so by symmetry the non-trivial zeros lie in $0 \leq \operatorname{Re} s \leq 1$. Details: Davenport, Chapter 8; Titchmarsh, Chapter II. Mathlib proves the functional equation (below). $\square$

<Theorem name="Theorem 7 (no zeros on the line Re s = 1)">

$\zeta(1 + it) \neq 0$ for every real $t$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

At $t = 0$ there is a pole. For $\sigma > 1$, the Euler product gives $\log\zeta(s) = \sum_p\sum_{k \geq 1}\frac{p^{-ks}}{k} = \sum_{n \geq 2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{\log n}n^{-s}$, so
$$
\log|\zeta(\sigma + it)| = \operatorname{Re}\log\zeta(\sigma + it) = \sum_{n \geq 2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{\log n}\,n^{-\sigma}\cos(t\log n).
$$
Therefore, with $\theta_n = t\log n$,
$$
\log\left|\zeta(\sigma)^3\,\zeta(\sigma + it)^4\,\zeta(\sigma + 2it)\right| = \sum_{n \geq 2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{\log n}\,n^{-\sigma}\big(3 + 4\cos\theta_n + \cos 2\theta_n\big) \geq 0,
$$
because $3 + 4\cos\theta + \cos 2\theta = 2(1 + \cos\theta)^2 \geq 0$ (Mertens' trick, formalised below). So $|\zeta(\sigma)^3\zeta(\sigma + it)^4\zeta(\sigma + 2it)| \geq 1$ for all $\sigma > 1$.

Suppose $\zeta(1 + it) = 0$ with $t \neq 0$. As $\sigma \to 1^+$: $\zeta(\sigma)^3 \sim (\sigma - 1)^{-3}$ (simple pole of residue $1$, Theorem 5); $\zeta(\sigma + it)^4 = O\big((\sigma - 1)^4\big)$ (a zero at $1 + it$, and $\zeta$ is holomorphic there); and $\zeta(\sigma + 2it)$ stays bounded, because $\zeta$ is holomorphic at $1 + 2it \neq 1$. So the product is $O(\sigma - 1) \to 0$, contradicting the lower bound $1$.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why $3$, $4$ and $1$? A zero at $1 + it$ vanishes to order at least one and is raised to the fourth power, which beats the triple pole at $\sigma = 1$; non-negativity of $3 + 4\cos\theta + \cos 2\theta$ keeps the product away from $0$. Any non-negative cosine polynomial $a_0 + a_1\cos\theta + a_2\cos 2\theta + \cdots$ with non-negative coefficients and $a_1 > a_0$ would do; $3$-$4$-$1$ is the classical choice.

</Narrator>

<Theorem name="Theorem 8 (the prime number theorem; proof outlined)">

$$
\psi(x) \sim x, \qquad \theta(x) \sim x, \qquad \pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x} \sim \operatorname{li}(x).
$$

</Theorem>

*Outline of a proof* (D. J. Newman's, as presented by Zagier, 1997).

1. $\theta(x) = O(x)$ — Theorem 4.
2. The function $\Phi(s) = \sum_p (\log p)\,p^{-s}$ differs from $-\zeta'(s)/\zeta(s)$ by $\sum_p \frac{(\log p)\, p^{-s}}{p^s - 1}$, which is holomorphic for $\operatorname{Re} s > \frac12$. By Theorems 5 and 7, $-\zeta'/\zeta$ has a simple pole with residue $1$ at $s = 1$ and no other poles on $\operatorname{Re} s \geq 1$. So $\Phi(s) - \frac{1}{s - 1}$ extends holomorphically to a neighbourhood of $\operatorname{Re} s \geq 1$.
3. **Newman's analytic theorem**: if $f$ is bounded and locally integrable on $[0, \infty)$ and its Laplace transform $g(z) = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-zt}\,dt$ extends holomorphically to a neighbourhood of $\operatorname{Re} z \geq 0$, then $\int_0^\infty f(t)\,dt$ converges (and equals $g(0)$). The proof is a single clever contour integral.
4. Apply it to $f(t) = \theta(e^t)e^{-t} - 1$, bounded by step 1, whose Laplace transform is $\frac{\Phi(z + 1)}{z + 1} - \frac{1}{z}$, holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re} z \geq 0$ by step 2. So $\int_1^\infty \frac{\theta(x) - x}{x^2}\,dx$ converges.
5. If $\theta(x) \geq \lambda x$ for some $\lambda > 1$ and arbitrarily large $x$, then since $\theta$ is increasing, $\int_x^{\lambda x}\frac{\theta(t) - t}{t^2}\,dt \geq \int_1^\lambda \frac{\lambda - u}{u^2}\,du > 0$ for those $x$, contradicting convergence; similarly for $\lambda < 1$. So $\theta(x) \sim x$.
6. Partial summation: $\pi(x) = \frac{\theta(x)}{\log x} + \int_2^x \frac{\theta(t)}{t\log^2 t}\,dt$, and the integral is $O(x/\log^2 x)$, so $\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$; also $\operatorname{li}(x) \sim x/\log x$ by L'Hôpital.

Complete proof: Zagier, *Newman's short proof of the prime number theorem*, Amer. Math. Monthly 104 (1997). The classical proofs use the **zero-free region** $\sigma > 1 - c/\log(|t| + 2)$ (de la Vallée Poussin, 1899), which gives the error term $\psi(x) = x + O\big(x e^{-c'\sqrt{\log x}}\big)$. $\square$

<Theorem name="Theorem 9 (von Mangoldt's explicit formula; stated)">

For $x > 1$ not a prime power,
$$
\psi(x) = x - \sum_\rho \frac{x^\rho}{\rho} - \log 2\pi - \frac12\log\left(1 - x^{-2}\right),
$$
where $\rho$ runs over the non-trivial zeros of $\zeta$ with multiplicity, the sum taken as $\lim_{T \to \infty}\sum_{|\operatorname{Im}\rho| < T}$.

</Theorem>

This is proved by integrating $-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)}\frac{x^s}{s}$ around a large rectangle and collecting residues: $x$ from the pole at $s = 1$, $-x^\rho/\rho$ from each zero, $-\log 2\pi = -\zeta'(0)/\zeta(0)$ from $s = 0$, and $-\frac12\log(1 - x^{-2}) = \sum_k \frac{x^{-2k}}{2k}$ from the trivial zeros (Davenport, Chapter 17). It explains everything:

- If every zero has real part at most $\Theta$, then $\psi(x) = x + O(x^\Theta\log^2 x)$; conversely a zero with real part $\beta$ forces errors of size $x^{\beta - \varepsilon}$ infinitely often. The prime number theorem is equivalent to "no zeros on $\operatorname{Re} s = 1$".
- The **Riemann hypothesis** ($\Theta = \frac12$) is equivalent to $\psi(x) = x + O(\sqrt x\log^2 x)$, and to $\pi(x) = \operatorname{li}(x) + O(\sqrt x\log x)$ (von Koch, 1901).
- Each pair of zeros $\frac12 \pm i\gamma$ contributes the wave $-2\operatorname{Re}\frac{x^\rho}{\rho} = -\frac{4\sqrt x\,\big(\cos(\gamma\log x) + 2\gamma\sin(\gamma\log x)\big)}{1 + 4\gamma^2}$, of amplitude about $2\sqrt x/\gamma$; the visualisation below adds these waves one by one.

**What is known and what is open.** *Proved:* infinitely many zeros lie on the critical line (Hardy, 1914), in fact a positive proportion (Selberg, 1942), more than $40\%$ (Conrey, 1989); every zero with $0 < \operatorname{Im}\rho \leq 3 \cdot 10^{12}$ is on the line (Platt and Trudgian, 2021); $\pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x)$ changes sign infinitely often (Littlewood, 1914). *Open:* the Riemann hypothesis itself; even the existence of some $\Theta < 1$ with no zeros in $\operatorname{Re} s > \Theta$; the twin prime conjecture; Goldbach's conjecture for even numbers.

<Theorem name="Theorem 10 (Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions)">

If $\gcd(a, q) = 1$, there are infinitely many primes $p \equiv a \pmod q$; in fact $\sum_{p \equiv a \ (q)} 1/p = \infty$.

</Theorem>

*Proof for $q = 4$, and the general strategy.* For $\sigma > 1$ and a character $\chi$ mod $q$, the Euler product of $L(s, \chi)$ gives, as in Theorem 3,
$$
\log L(\sigma, \chi) = \sum_p \frac{\chi(p)}{p^\sigma} + O(1) \qquad (\sigma \to 1^+).
$$
Take $q = 4$. For $\chi_0$, $L(\sigma, \chi_0) = (1 - 2^{-\sigma})\zeta(\sigma) \to \infty$, so $\sum_{p \text{ odd}} p^{-\sigma} \to \infty$. For $\chi_4$, the alternating series $L(\sigma, \chi_4) = 1 - 3^{-\sigma} + 5^{-\sigma} - \cdots$ converges for $\sigma > 0$ and is continuous in $\sigma$, and $L(1, \chi_4) = \frac{\pi}{4} > 0$, so $\log L(\sigma, \chi_4)$ stays bounded as $\sigma \to 1^+$. Adding and subtracting,
$$
\sum_{p \equiv 1 \ (4)}\frac{1}{p^\sigma} = \frac12\left(\sum_{p \text{ odd}}\frac{1}{p^\sigma} + \sum_p\frac{\chi_4(p)}{p^\sigma}\right) \to \infty, \qquad \sum_{p \equiv 3 \ (4)}\frac{1}{p^\sigma} = \frac12\left(\sum_{p \text{ odd}}\frac{1}{p^\sigma} - \sum_p\frac{\chi_4(p)}{p^\sigma}\right) \to \infty.
$$
For general $q$, the orthogonality relation $\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi \overline{\chi(a)}\chi(n) = [n \equiv a \bmod q]$ (for $\gcd(a, q) = 1$) gives
$$
\sum_{p \equiv a \ (q)}\frac{1}{p^\sigma} = \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi \overline{\chi(a)}\log L(\sigma, \chi) + O(1).
$$
The principal character contributes $\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\log\frac{1}{\sigma - 1} + O(1) \to \infty$, and the theorem follows once $L(1, \chi) \neq 0$ for every $\chi \neq \chi_0$ (each such $L(s, \chi)$ is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re} s > 0$ by partial summation). For complex $\chi$ this follows from $\prod_\chi L(\sigma, \chi) \geq 1$ (a $3$-$4$-$1$-type argument); for real $\chi$ it is the deep point, proved by Dirichlet via his class number formula ($L(1, \chi) = \frac{\pi h}{\sqrt{|d|}}$ for imaginary quadratic characters with $|d| > 4$, and $h \geq 1$). Full proof: Apostol, Chapter 7; Davenport, Chapters 1–4. Mathlib proves the theorem (below). $\square$

With more work (the zero-free region for $L$-functions) one gets the **prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions**: $\pi(x; q, a) \sim \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\frac{x}{\log x}$ — the primes are equidistributed among the $\varphi(q)$ admissible classes.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Billions of zeros have been checked, so the Riemann hypothesis is essentially proved."** More than $10^{13}$ zeros are known to lie on the critical line, but that is a finite amount of evidence about infinitely many zeros, and number theory has famous examples of patterns that fail only at enormous heights: $\pi(x) < \operatorname{li}(x)$ holds for every $x$ ever computed, yet Littlewood proved that it fails infinitely often. Numerical verification is evidence, not proof; the Riemann hypothesis is open.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$ means $\pi(x) - x/\log x \to 0$."** It means the *ratio* tends to $1$. The difference grows without bound: it is $6116$ at $x = 10^6$ and is of order $x/\log^2 x$. Even the much better approximation $\operatorname{li}(x)$ has an error that is unbounded (by Littlewood) and, under the Riemann hypothesis, of order at most $\sqrt x\log x$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots = -\frac{1}{12}$ because $\zeta(-1) = -\frac{1}{12}$."** The series $\sum n^{-s}$ converges only for $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$; $\zeta(-1) = -\frac{1}{12}$ is the value at $-1$ of the unique analytic continuation, not a sum of the divergent series. (Regularisation methods in physics do assign $-\frac{1}{12}$ to it, but that is a different, carefully specified procedure.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Primes in progressions are as easy as primes: just repeat Euclid's argument."** Euclid-style arguments exist for some progressions — $4k + 3$, $4k + 1$, and more generally exactly the classes $a \bmod q$ with $a^2 \equiv 1 \pmod q$ (Schur, 1912; Murty, 1988) — but not for all. Dirichlet needed $L$-functions and the non-vanishing of $L(1, \chi)$. (Selberg found an elementary proof in 1949, but it is not a variation on Euclid.)

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Viewpoints">

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Fourier duality between primes and zeros">

The explicit formula is a Fourier-type duality, made precise by Weil: schematically, for a nice even test function $g$ with Fourier transform $h$,
$$
\sum_\gamma h(\gamma) = h\!\left(\tfrac{i}{2}\right) + h\!\left(-\tfrac{i}{2}\right) - \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_n \frac{\Lambda(n)}{\sqrt n}\big(g(\log n) + g(-\log n)\big) + (\text{archimedean term}),
$$
summing over the ordinates of non-trivial zeros on one side and prime powers on the other — the analogue of the Poisson summation formula that proves the functional equation. Analysis supplies the tools: Mellin and Laplace transforms, contour integration, Tauberian theorems (Wiener–Ikehara gives the prime number theorem from Theorem 7 alone), and **sieve methods**, which bound primes in sets where $L$-functions say nothing. Modern highlights mix these: Yitang Zhang (2013) proved bounded gaps between primes, and Maynard and Tao (2013) found a simpler sieve; the best bound, $246$, is due to the Polymath project.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="Primes as a random-looking set">

Cramér's model (each $n$ prime with probability $1/\log n$) is a heuristic that predicts the size of prime gaps (Cramér conjectured $\limsup (p_{n+1} - p_n)/\log^2 p_n = 1$) and the Riemann-hypothesis-sized error term. Some probabilistic statements are theorems: the **Erdős–Kac theorem** (1940) says that the number of distinct prime factors $\omega(n)$ of a random $n \leq x$ is asymptotically normal with mean and variance $\log\log x$ — a central limit theorem for arithmetic. The Möbius function behaves like a random sign: the prime number theorem is equivalent to $\sum_{n \leq x}\mu(n) = o(x)$, and the Riemann hypothesis to $\sum_{n \leq x}\mu(n) = O(x^{1/2 + \varepsilon})$, the square-root cancellation of a random walk. Sarnak's **Möbius disjointness** conjecture and Chowla's conjecture make "$\mu$ is random" precise; Tao and Teräväinen have proved cases of them.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Zeros and random matrices">

In 1972 Hugh Montgomery computed (assuming the Riemann hypothesis, and for a restricted class of test functions) the pair correlation of the zeros $\frac12 + i\gamma$: normalised spacings are distributed with density $1 - \left(\frac{\sin\pi u}{\pi u}\right)^2$. Freeman Dyson recognised this as the pair correlation of eigenvalues of large random Hermitian matrices (the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble), which models energy levels of heavy nuclei and quantum chaotic systems. Andrew Odlyzko's computations of zeros near the $10^{20}$-th zero (1980s onwards) match GUE statistics with striking precision. This suggests the **Hilbert–Pólya** idea that the $\gamma$'s are the eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator, which would imply the Riemann hypothesis. All of this is conjecture supported by numerical evidence and partial theorems; no such operator is known. Random matrix theory also predicts moments of $\zeta$ on the critical line (Keating and Snaith, 2000), again conjecturally.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="The Riemann hypothesis for curves is a theorem">

For a smooth projective curve $C$ of genus $g$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$, the zeta function $Z(C, T) = \exp\big(\sum_m \#C(\mathbb{F}_{q^m})\,T^m/m\big)$ is a rational function $\frac{P(T)}{(1 - T)(1 - qT)}$ with $\deg P = 2g$, it satisfies a functional equation, and **all zeros of $P$ have absolute value $q^{-1/2}$** — the exact analogue of the Riemann hypothesis, proved by Weil in the 1940s (Hasse had done $g = 1$ in 1933) and generalised to all varieties by Deligne (1974). Geometrically, $\#C(\mathbb{F}_{q^m}) = q^m + 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{2g}\alpha_i^m$ is a Lefschetz fixed-point formula for the Frobenius map acting on cohomology, and $|\alpha_i| = \sqrt q$ is a positivity statement (the Hodge index theorem on $C \times C$). In the analogy, $\operatorname{Spec}\Z$ is a "curve" whose missing cohomology theory would prove the Riemann hypothesis; this analogy has guided research for eighty years but is not a proof strategy anyone has completed (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.algebraic-geometry">algebraic geometry</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.number.arithmetic-geometry">arithmetic geometry</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Characters are Fourier analysis on a finite group">

Dirichlet characters are the characters (one-dimensional representations) of the finite abelian group $(\Z/q\Z)^\times$, and the orthogonality relations used in Theorem 10 are Fourier analysis on that group: the indicator function of a residue class is $\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi\overline{\chi(a)}\chi$. Replacing $(\Z/q)^\times$ by Galois groups leads to **Artin $L$-functions** $L(s, \rho)$ of Galois representations $\rho$, whose analytic properties encode the splitting of primes in number fields (Chebotarev's density theorem generalises Dirichlet's theorem). The quadratic character $\chi_d$ satisfies $\zeta_{\Q(\sqrt d)}(s) = \zeta(s)L(s, \chi_d)$, so $L(1, \chi_d) \neq 0$ is a statement about class numbers (see <TopicLink id="math.number.algebraic-number-theory">algebraic number theory</TopicLink>). The Langlands programme predicts that all such $L$-functions are automorphic (see <TopicLink id="math.number.langlands-programme">the Langlands programme</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Mādhava of Sangamagrāma** (Kerala, c. 1400) found the series $\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \cdots$, rediscovered by Gregory and Leibniz; it is $L(1, \chi_4)$. **Leonhard Euler** found the Euler product and proved that $\sum 1/p$ diverges (1737), and evaluated $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$ (1734).
- **Gauss** (from about 1792, described in an 1849 letter to Encke) and **Legendre** (1798) conjectured the prime number theorem. **Dirichlet** (1837) proved his theorem on primes in progressions, inventing characters and $L$-functions. **Pafnuty Chebyshev** (1848–52) proved $\pi(x) \asymp x/\log x$ with explicit constants and Bertrand's postulate.
- **Bernhard Riemann**'s eight-page memoir *Ueber die Anzahl der Primzahlen unter einer gegebenen Grösse* (1859) introduced $\zeta$ as a complex function, proved the functional equation, sketched the explicit formula and stated the Riemann hypothesis. **Hans von Mangoldt** proved the explicit formula (1895); **Jacques Hadamard** and **Charles de la Vallée Poussin** independently proved the prime number theorem (1896).
- **G. H. Hardy** proved that infinitely many zeros lie on the critical line, and **J. E. Littlewood** that $\pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x)$ changes sign infinitely often (both 1914). **Srinivasa Ramanujan** and Hardy developed the circle method (1918), which **I. M. Vinogradov** used to prove that every sufficiently large odd number is a sum of three primes (1937); **Harald Helfgott** completed this ternary Goldbach theorem (2013). **Chen Jingrun** proved that every sufficiently large even number is a prime plus a product of at most two primes (1973).
- **Atle Selberg** and **Paul Erdős** found elementary proofs of the prime number theorem (1948–49). **Ben Green** and **Terence Tao** proved that the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions (2004), and **Yitang Zhang** proved bounded gaps between primes (2013), improved by **James Maynard**, Tao and the Polymath project.

</Section>
