<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Two tanks">

At a school in Lilongwe, two rainwater tanks stand side by side. In the morning the first holds $500$ litres and is being used to water the vegetable garden, losing $20$ litres a minute. The second holds $100$ litres and is being filled from the roof gutters at $30$ litres a minute. When will the two tanks hold the same amount?

After $t$ minutes the first tank holds $500 - 20t$ litres and the second $100 + 30t$ litres. We want the value of $t$ that makes

$$
500 - 20t = 100 + 30t.
$$

We could guess: after $5$ minutes the tanks hold $400$ and $250$ litres; after $10$ minutes, $300$ and $400$. So the answer lies somewhere between. Guessing works here, but it is slow, it is hopeless when the answer is $\tfrac{37}{11}$, and it can never tell us that there is **no** answer. This lesson develops a method that always works — and proves *why* it works.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Keeping the balance">

An equation is like a balance in equilibrium: the two sides weigh the same. If we take the same amount off both pans, or double both pans, the balance stays level. Try it: each box weighs $x$ units, and the goal is to get one box alone on one side.

<BalanceScale left={{ x: 3, units: 1 }} right={{ x: 1, units: 9 }} title="x on both sides" subtitle="Get one x alone. Every legal move keeps the scale level." />

<Narrator who="chloe">

Did you notice that every move can be **undone**? If you took $1$ from both sides, adding $1$ to both sides takes you back. That is the secret of the whole method. A move that can be undone never adds or loses a solution — and we are about to prove it.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Equations, solutions and equivalence">

<Definition term="equation and solution">

An **equation** in a variable $x$ is a statement that two <TopicLink id="math.algebra.variables-expressions">algebraic expressions</TopicLink> are equal, such as $3x + 1 = x + 9$. A **solution** is a value of $x$ that makes the statement true. The **solution set** is the set of all solutions. To **solve** an equation means to find its solution set — all of the solutions, and nothing else.

</Definition>

<Definition term="equivalent equations">

Two equations are **equivalent** if they have exactly the same solutions.

</Definition>

<Definition term="linear equation">

A **linear equation** in $x$ is one that can be written as $ax + b = cx + d$ for numbers $a, b, c, d$: the variable appears only to the first power, multiplied by numbers.

</Definition>

So $5 - 2x = 11$ and $\tfrac{x}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{x - 1}{4}$ are linear; $x^2 = 2x$ and $\tfrac{1}{x} = 3$ are not.

<Definition term="inequality">

An **inequality** compares two expressions with $<$, $\leq$, $>$ or $\geq$. Its solutions are the values of the variable that make it true. For example, the solutions of $x + 2 > 5$ are all the numbers greater than $3$ — infinitely many.

</Definition>

Throughout, the variable can be any **rational number** (see <TopicLink id="math.number.rational-numbers">rational numbers</TopicLink>) unless we say otherwise. This matters: $2x = 1$ has the solution $\tfrac{1}{2}$ among the rational numbers but no solution among the <TopicLink id="math.number.integers">integers</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Why the balance method works">

<Theorem name="moves that give equivalent equations">

For all numbers $p$, $q$ and $c$:

1. $p = q$ if and only if $p + c = q + c$;
2. if $c \neq 0$, then $p = q$ if and only if $cp = cq$.

Consequently, adding the same number to both sides of an equation, or multiplying both sides by the same **non-zero** number, gives an **equivalent** equation.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

1. If $p = q$, then $p + c$ and $q + c$ are the same number, so they are equal. Conversely, if $p + c = q + c$, add $-c$ to both sides: $p = (p + c) + (-c) = (q + c) + (-c) = q$.
2. If $p = q$, then $cp = cq$. Conversely, if $cp = cq$ and $c \neq 0$, multiply both sides by $\tfrac{1}{c}$, which exists because $c \neq 0$: $p = \tfrac{1}{c}(cp) = \tfrac{1}{c}(cq) = q$.

For an equation in $x$, apply this to each value of $x$ separately, with $p$ and $q$ the values of the two sides: $x$ makes the old equation true exactly when it makes the new one true.

</Proof>

Subtracting $c$ is adding $-c$, and dividing by $c \neq 0$ is multiplying by $\tfrac{1}{c}$, so these moves are covered too. We may also add an *expression* such as $-x$ to both sides: for each value of $x$, that just adds a number.

The proof shows exactly where $c \neq 0$ is used: to **undo** the multiplication. Multiplying by $0$ cannot be undone. The equation $x = 3$ has one solution, but $0 \cdot x = 0 \cdot 3$ is true for every $x$ — multiplying by zero has thrown the information away.

So **solving an equation is building a chain of equivalent equations**, ending with one whose solutions we can read off. Because every link is an "if and only if", the last equation has exactly the same solutions as the first. Checking the answer by substitution is still a good habit: it catches arithmetic slips.

<Theorem name="the three outcomes for ax = b">

For numbers $a$ and $b$, the equation $ax = b$ has

- exactly one solution, $x = \tfrac{b}{a}$, if $a \neq 0$;
- no solution, if $a = 0$ and $b \neq 0$;
- every number as a solution, if $a = 0$ and $b = 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $a \neq 0$, multiplying both sides by $\tfrac{1}{a}$ gives the equivalent equation $x = \tfrac{b}{a}$, whose only solution is $\tfrac{b}{a}$. If $a = 0$, the left side $0 \cdot x$ is $0$ for every $x$, so the equation says $0 = b$: never true if $b \neq 0$, always true if $b = 0$.

</Proof>

Every linear equation can be brought into this form. Subtracting $cx + b$ from both sides of $ax + b = cx + d$ gives the equivalent equation

$$
(a - c)\,x = d - b.
$$

<Theorem name="no linear equation has exactly two solutions">

If two different numbers $x_1 \neq x_2$ both solve $ax + b = cx + d$, then every number solves it.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Both numbers solve the standard form: $(a - c)x_1 = d - b$ and $(a - c)x_2 = d - b$. Subtracting, $(a - c)(x_1 - x_2) = 0$. Since $x_1 - x_2 \neq 0$, we can divide by it, so $a - c = 0$, and then $d - b = (a - c)x_1 = 0$. So the standard form reads $0 \cdot x = 0$, which every number satisfies.

</Proof>

A linear equation therefore has exactly one solution, no solution, or infinitely many — nothing in between.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="The variable on both sides">

Solve $3x + 1 = x + 9$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
3x + 1 &= x + 9 &&\\
2x + 1 &= 9 &&\text{subtract } x \text{ from both sides}\\
2x &= 8 &&\text{subtract } 1\\
x &= 4 &&\text{multiply by } \tfrac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check: $3 \cdot 4 + 1 = 13$ and $4 + 9 = 13$. ✓

</Example>

<Example title="The two tanks">

$500 - 20t = 100 + 30t$. Add $20t$ to both sides: $500 = 100 + 50t$. Subtract $100$: $400 = 50t$. Divide by $50$: $t = 8$. After $8$ minutes both tanks hold $340$ litres ($500 - 160 = 100 + 240$).

</Example>

<Example title="Negative coefficients and brackets">

- $5 - 2x = 11$. Subtract $5$: $-2x = 6$. Divide by $-2$: $x = -3$. Check: $5 - 2(-3) = 5 + 6 = 11$. ✓
- $2(x - 3) - (x - 5) = 7$. Expand carefully — the minus sign in front of the second bracket multiplies *both* terms: $2x - 6 - x + 5 = 7$, so $x - 1 = 7$ and $x = 8$. Check: $2 \cdot 5 - 3 = 7$. ✓

</Example>

<Example title="Fractions">

Solve $\dfrac{x}{3} + \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{x - 1}{4}$.

Multiply both sides by $12$, the least common multiple of $3$, $2$ and $4$ — and multiply **every term**:

$$
12 \cdot \frac{x}{3} + 12 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 12 \cdot \frac{x - 1}{4}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 4x + 6 = 3x - 3
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = -9.
$$

Check: $\tfrac{-9}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2} = -\tfrac{5}{2}$ and $\tfrac{-10}{4} = -\tfrac{5}{2}$. ✓

</Example>

<Example title="No solution, and every number a solution">

- $2(x + 3) = 2x + 5$ expands to $2x + 6 = 2x + 5$. Subtracting $2x$ leaves $6 = 5$, which is false whatever $x$ is. **No solution.**
- $3(x - 2) + x = 4x - 6$ expands to $4x - 6 = 4x - 6$. Subtracting $4x$ leaves $-6 = -6$, true whatever $x$ is. **Every number is a solution**: the equation is an identity.

When the variable disappears, do not panic — read the statement that remains.

</Example>

<Example title="Modelling: three consecutive numbers">

Three consecutive integers add up to $96$. What are they? Call the smallest $n$; the others are $n + 1$ and $n + 2$. Then $n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 96$, so $3n + 3 = 96$, $3n = 93$ and $n = 31$. The numbers are $31, 32, 33$ — and indeed they add to $96$. Choosing *what the variable stands for* is the most important step in modelling.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="inequalities" kind="proof" title="Inequalities, and why they flip">

Inequalities are solved with the same moves — with one twist. We use the basic facts about order: $p < q$ means that $q - p$ is **positive**; the sum and the product of two positive numbers are positive; and the product of a positive and a negative number is negative.

<Theorem name="moves for inequalities">

For all numbers $p$, $q$ and $c$:

1. $p < q$ if and only if $p + c < q + c$;
2. if $c > 0$, then $p < q$ if and only if $cp < cq$;
3. if $c < 0$, then $p < q$ if and only if $cp > cq$ — **the inequality reverses**.

The same holds with $\leq$ in place of $<$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

1. $(q + c) - (p + c) = q - p$, so one difference is positive exactly when the other is.
2. If $c > 0$ and $q - p > 0$, then $cq - cp = c(q - p)$ is a product of positive numbers, so it is positive: $cp < cq$.
3. If $c < 0$ and $q - p > 0$, then $-c > 0$, so $(-c)(q - p) > 0$. But $(-c)(q - p) = cp - cq$. So $cp - cq > 0$, which says $cq < cp$.

Each move can be undone (by adding $-c$, or multiplying by $\tfrac{1}{c}$, which has the same sign as $c$), which gives the "if and only if".

</Proof>

Here is the picture behind part 3. Multiplying by $-1$ reflects the number line in $0$: $2 < 5$, but $-2 > -5$, because the reflection swaps left and right. Multiplying by any negative number is a stretch followed by this reflection.

<Example title="Solving an inequality">

Solve $7 - 3x \leq 19$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
7 - 3x &\leq 19 &&\\
-3x &\leq 12 &&\text{subtract } 7\\
x &\geq -4 &&\text{divide by } -3 \text{, which reverses the inequality}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check with test values: $x = 0$ gives $7 \leq 19$, true; $x = -4$ gives $19 \leq 19$, true; $x = -5$ gives $22 \leq 19$, false. ✓

</Example>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Multiplying both sides by the same thing is always safe."** Only if the thing cannot be zero. Multiplying $x = 3$ by $0$ gives $0 = 0$, true for every $x$. Multiplying by an expression that can be zero can add solutions: $(x - 2)x = (x - 2) \cdot 3$ is also solved by $x = 2$. Dividing by such an expression can lose them: "dividing" $x^2 = 2x$ by $x$ gives $x = 2$ and loses the solution $x = 0$. Safe moves are the ones you can undo.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Forgetting to reverse the inequality.** Dividing $-3x \leq 12$ by $-3$ gives $x \geq -4$, not $x \leq -4$. Test a value to check: $x = 0$ satisfies $7 - 3x \leq 19$, so the answer must include $0$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Doing the move to only one term.** From $2x + 3 = 11$, dividing "both sides" by $2$ gives $x + \tfrac{3}{2} = \tfrac{11}{2}$, not $x + 3 = \tfrac{11}{2}$. A move applies to a **whole side**. Likewise, when clearing fractions, multiply *every* term by the common denominator.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$-(x - 5) = -x - 5$."** The minus sign multiplies both terms in the bracket: $-(x - 5) = -x + 5$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$0$ as an answer means no solution."** The equation $4x = 0$ has the solution $x = 0$. "No solution" means that *no* number works, as in $0 \cdot x = 5$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Meeting points and reflections">

Solving $ax + b = cx + d$ is finding where two lines in the <TopicLink id="math.geom.coordinate-plane">coordinate plane</TopicLink> cross. Two lines either cross once, never (parallel), or coincide — which is the geometric face of the theorem "one, none or infinitely many". The flip of an inequality is geometry too: $x \mapsto -x$ is the reflection of the number line in $0$, and a reflection turns "left of" into "right of". The next lesson, <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-relationships">linear relationships</TopicLink>, builds on these pictures.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Why we need inverses">

The proof that the moves work used two things: every number $c$ has an additive inverse $-c$, and every **non-zero** number has a multiplicative inverse $\tfrac{1}{c}$. A number system with both is called a **field**; the rational numbers are one. In the integers, $2$ has no multiplicative inverse, and $2x = 1$ has no integer solution. In clock arithmetic with $7$ hours, though, $3 \cdot 5 = 15$ leaves remainder $1$, so $5$ acts as $\tfrac{1}{3}$, and the "linear equation" $3x = 1$ is solved by $x = 5$. The trails below explore this.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="logic" title="Implies versus if and only if">

Each step of a solution should be an equivalence ($\Leftrightarrow$), not just an implication ($\Rightarrow$). Squaring both sides, or multiplying by an expression that can be zero, is only an implication: every solution of the old equation solves the new one, but the new one may have extra solutions (logicians would say the new equation is *weaker*). That is why such steps must always be followed by a check in the original equation. Lean makes the difference visible: our worked example is proved with `↔`, so its answer is both necessary and sufficient.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- The **Rhind papyrus** (Egypt, copied by the scribe Ahmes around 1550 BCE) contains linear problems such as Problem 24: "a quantity and its seventh, added together, become 19". It was solved by **false position**: guess $7$, which gives $7 + 1 = 8$; since $8$ must become $19$, scale the guess by $\tfrac{19}{8}$, giving $16 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{8}$.
- Chapter 7 of the Chinese *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* (compiled by about the 1st century CE), "Excess and deficit", solves linear problems by a method of **double false position**: two guesses, one too big and one too small, determine the answer exactly.
- In 9th-century Baghdad, **al-Khwārizmī** named two of the operations of this lesson. *Al-jabr* ("restoring") adds equal amounts to both sides to remove a subtracted term; *al-muqābala* ("balancing") cancels equal terms from both sides. Brahmagupta in India (628 CE) also gave a general rule for solving linear equations.
- The equals sign was introduced by the Welsh mathematician **Robert Recorde** in *The Whetstone of Witte* (1557), "bicause noe .2. thynges, can be moare equalle" than two parallel lines. The signs $<$ and $>$ appear in **Thomas Harriot**'s *Artis analyticae praxis*, published in 1631 after his death.

</Section>
