<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Where exactly?">

“The treasure is buried somewhere in the field” is not much help. “Walk 30 paces east from the old baobab tree, then 20 paces north” is. Two numbers, measured from an agreed starting point in two agreed directions, pin down a place exactly.

People use this idea everywhere. A chess move is written as a letter and a number, such as e4. A seat in a cinema has a row and a number. A ship's position is given by latitude and longitude: Quito lies almost exactly on the equator, at latitude $0^\circ$. Many cities, from La Plata in Argentina to Chandigarh in India, are laid out in grids of streets, so a delivery rider thinks in “blocks east” and “blocks north”.

Mathematics takes this everyday idea and makes it precise. The result, the **coordinate plane**, lets us draw numbers and calculate with pictures. It is one of the most useful inventions in the whole of mathematics.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Two number lines at right angles">

Take a number line and lay it horizontally: this is the **$x$-axis**. Take a second number line and stand it up vertically, crossing the first at $0$: this is the **$y$-axis**. The crossing point is the **origin**. Every point of the plane can now be reached from the origin by moving some distance left or right (along the $x$-axis) and then some distance up or down (parallel to the $y$-axis).

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="coordinate plane">

The **coordinate plane** is formed by two perpendicular number lines, the **$x$-axis** (horizontal) and the **$y$-axis** (vertical), which meet at their zeros. The meeting point is the **origin**, $O = (0, 0)$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="coordinates">

The **coordinates** $(a, b)$ of a point $P$ are its signed horizontal and vertical positions relative to the origin: $a$ is the **$x$-coordinate** (positive to the right, negative to the left) and $b$ is the **$y$-coordinate** (positive up, negative down). An **ordered pair** is a pair of numbers in which the order matters: $(a, b) = (c, d)$ exactly when $a = c$ and $b = d$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="quadrants">

The axes split the rest of the plane into four **quadrants**: I where $x > 0$ and $y > 0$; II where $x \lt 0$ and $y > 0$; III where $x \lt 0$ and $y \lt 0$; IV where $x > 0$ and $y \lt 0$. Points on an axis are in no quadrant.

</Definition>

<Definition term="absolute value">

The **absolute value** $|a|$ is the distance of $a$ from $0$ on the number line: $|a| = a$ if $a \ge 0$, and $|a| = -a$ if $a \lt 0$. So $|5| = 5$, $|-5| = 5$ and $|0| = 0$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="reflection in an axis">

The **reflection** of a point $P$ in a line $\ell$ is the point $P'$ on the other side of $\ell$ such that $\ell$ crosses the segment $PP'$ at right angles, exactly halfway. A point on $\ell$ is its own reflection.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Plotting with negative numbers and fractions">

To plot $D = (-2.5, -3)$: from the origin go $2.5$ units **left** (the $x$-coordinate is negative), then $3$ units **down**. $D$ is in quadrant III. The point $(\tfrac{3}{4}, -6)$ is in quadrant IV, and $(0, -2)$ lies on the $y$-axis, in no quadrant.

</Example>

<Example title="Reading a distance along a horizontal line">

How far is $(-3, 2)$ from $(4, 2)$? Both points are 2 units up, so the segment joining them is horizontal. From $x = -3$ to $x = 0$ is 3 units; from $0$ to $4$ is 4 more. The distance is $7 = |-3 - 4| = |4 - (-3)|$. It is **not** $4 - 3 = 1$: the points are on opposite sides of the $y$-axis.

</Example>

<Example title="A vertical distance">

From $(1, -4)$ to $(1, 3)$ the segment is vertical, and its length is $|-4 - 3| = 7$.

</Example>

<Example title="Distance along the grid">

A delivery rider in a city of square blocks travels from $(-3, 2)$ to $(4, -1)$ along the streets. She must go $|-3 - 4| = 7$ blocks east and $|2 - (-1)| = 3$ blocks south: $10$ blocks, whichever route she picks (as long as she never doubles back). This **grid distance** $|a - c| + |b - d|$ is also called the *taxicab distance*.

</Example>

<Example title="Reflections">

Reflect $A = (3, 2)$ in the $x$-axis: it lands at $(3, -2)$, 2 units **below** the axis. Reflect it in the $y$-axis: $(-3, 2)$. Reflect it in both: $(-3, -2)$, the same as turning $A$ half way round the origin.

</Example>

<Example title="Plotting a relationship">

A kettle's water starts at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and warms by $15$ degrees each minute. Plotting (minutes, temperature) gives $(0, 20)$, $(1, 35)$, $(2, 50)$, $(3, 65)$, $(4, 80)$: the points lie on a straight line. Turning a table into points in the plane makes patterns visible — the starting point for <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-relationships">linear relationships</TopicLink>.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Why it all works">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (one point, one ordered pair)">

Every point of the plane has exactly one pair of coordinates, and every ordered pair of numbers $(a, b)$ is the address of exactly one point. Two points are the same exactly when their $x$-coordinates are equal **and** their $y$-coordinates are equal.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Given a point $P$, draw the line through $P$ parallel to the $y$-axis. It crosses the $x$-axis at exactly one point, which is some number $a$ on that number line. Likewise the line through $P$ parallel to the $x$-axis crosses the $y$-axis at exactly one number $b$. So $P$ has the coordinates $(a, b)$, and no others.

Conversely, given $(a, b)$, the vertical line through $a$ and the horizontal line through $b$ are perpendicular, so they cross at exactly one point. That point has coordinates $(a, b)$.

So points and ordered pairs match up one-to-one, and two points are equal exactly when their pairs are equal, that is, when both coordinates agree.

</Proof>

In particular $(3, 2) \neq (2, 3)$: their $x$-coordinates differ.

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (distance along a line)">

The distance between $(a, b)$ and $(c, b)$ is $|a - c|$. The distance between $(a, b)$ and $(a, d)$ is $|b - d|$. Also $|a - c| = |c - a|$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The points $(a, b)$ and $(c, b)$ lie on the horizontal line at height $b$, which is a copy of the $x$-axis number line. On a number line the distance between $a$ and $c$ is the larger minus the smaller. If $a \ge c$ that is $a - c$, which equals $|a - c|$ because $a - c \ge 0$. If $a \lt c$ it is $c - a = -(a - c)$, which equals $|a - c|$ because $a - c \lt 0$. Either way the distance is $|a - c|$; and because the distance does not depend on which point we call first, $|a - c| = |c - a|$. The vertical case is the same, using the $y$-coordinates.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (reflections in the axes)">

Reflecting $(a, b)$ in the $x$-axis gives $(a, -b)$; reflecting it in the $y$-axis gives $(-a, b)$. Reflecting twice in the same axis brings every point back, and reflecting in both axes, in either order, gives $(-a, -b)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $P = (a, b)$. The line through $P$ perpendicular to the $x$-axis is the vertical line at $a$, so the reflection $P'$ also has $x$-coordinate $a$. The $x$-axis must cross $PP'$ halfway, so $P'$ is as far below the axis as $P$ is above it (or vice versa): its $y$-coordinate is $-b$. (If $b = 0$, then $P$ is on the axis and $P' = P = (a, -0)$.) The $y$-axis case is the same with the roles of the coordinates swapped.

Reflecting $(a, -b)$ in the $x$-axis gives $(a, -(-b)) = (a, b)$, the starting point. Reflecting in the $y$-axis and then the $x$-axis gives $(-a, b) \mapsto (-a, -b)$; the other order gives $(a, -b) \mapsto (-a, -b)$. The same point.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (reflections and quadrants)">

Reflection in the $x$-axis swaps quadrants I and IV, and II and III. Reflection in the $y$-axis swaps I and II, and III and IV. The points that reflection in the $x$-axis leaves where they are are exactly the points of the $x$-axis.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Reflection in the $x$-axis keeps the sign of the $x$-coordinate and reverses the sign of the $y$-coordinate. Quadrants I and IV are exactly the two quadrants with $x > 0$, differing only in the sign of $y$; so are II and III with $x \lt 0$. For the last claim: $(a, -b) = (a, b)$ exactly when $-b = b$, that is $2b = 0$, that is $b = 0$. The $y$-axis case is the same with the coordinates swapped.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (reflections keep distances)">

Reflecting two points in an axis does not change the horizontal distance, the vertical distance, or the grid distance between them.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Reflect $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ in the $x$-axis. The horizontal distance is still $|a - c|$. The vertical distance becomes $|(-b) - (-d)| = |d - b| = |b - d|$ by Theorem 2. So the grid distance $|a - c| + |b - d|$ is unchanged too. The $y$-axis is similar.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Theorem 5 is a first glimpse of a big idea. A reflection moves every point, but it keeps *distances* — so it cannot change the size or shape of a figure, only flip it over. Moves like that are called **rigid motions**, and they are the subject of <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">a later lesson</TopicLink>.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="taxicab" kind="human" title="Taxicab geometry: a different distance">

In a city of square blocks, the grid distance $|a - c| + |b - d|$ is the distance that matters to a taxi driver: you cannot drive through buildings. It obeys the same basic rule as ordinary distance, the **triangle inequality**: going from $P$ to $R$ by way of a third point $Q$ is never shorter than going directly.

<Theorem name="Theorem 6 (triangle inequality for grid distance)">

For any points $P$, $Q$, $R$: $\;\text{grid}(P, R) \le \text{grid}(P, Q) + \text{grid}(Q, R)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

On a number line, $|u + v| \le |u| + |v|$ for all numbers $u$ and $v$: if $u$ and $v$ have the same sign, both sides are equal; if they have opposite signs, they partly cancel on the left. Apply this with $u = a - e$ and $v = e - c$ for the $x$-coordinates $a, e, c$ of $P, Q, R$: $|a - c| \le |a - e| + |e - c|$. Do the same for the $y$-coordinates and add the two inequalities.

</Proof>

Taxicab geometry has surprises. The set of points at grid distance $3$ from the origin — a taxicab “circle” — is not round at all: it is a square standing on one corner, with corners at $(3, 0)$, $(0, 3)$, $(-3, 0)$ and $(0, -3)$. Ordinary straight-line distance, the kind a bird would fly, needs the <TopicLink id="math.geom.pythagorean-theorem">Pythagorean theorem</TopicLink>, which comes next.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Swapping the coordinates: plotting $(3, 2)$ at $(2, 3)$.** The first number is always the horizontal ($x$) position, the second the vertical ($y$) position — “along the corridor, then up the stairs”. Swapping them moves the point to its mirror image in the line $y = x$, which is a different point unless the two coordinates are equal.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Counting grid lines instead of unit intervals.** From $x = -3$ to $x = 4$ you pass $8$ grid lines ($-3, -2, \dots, 4$) but take only $7$ unit steps. Distance counts the **gaps** between grid lines, not the lines themselves — like fence panels and fence posts.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“The distance from $-3$ to $4$ is $4 - 3 = 1$.”** Ignoring the sign gives the wrong answer when the points lie on opposite sides of an axis. Subtract the coordinates themselves and take the absolute value: $|4 - (-3)| = 7$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“Reflecting in the $x$-axis changes the $x$-coordinate.”** It is the other way round: reflecting in the $x$-axis flips the point *across* the $x$-axis, so it changes the sign of $y$ and keeps $x$. Picture the axis as a mirror lying along the $x$-direction.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Points you can add">

Once points are pairs of numbers, we can calculate with them. Adding $(a, b) + (c, d) = (a + c, b + d)$ means “do one journey, then the other”; multiplying by a number, $k \cdot (a, b) = (ka, kb)$, stretches a journey. With these operations the plane becomes the **vector space** $\R^2$, and reflections become simple formulas: the reflection in the $x$-axis is the rule $(a, b) \mapsto (a, -b)$. This is the doorway to <TopicLink id="math.geom.vectors">vectors</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">linear algebra</TopicLink>, where whole geometric transformations are written as tables of numbers called matrices.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Which distance?">

Grid distance and straight-line distance are two different ways to measure the same plane, and they give two different geometries. Reflections in the axes, half turns and swapping the coordinates keep both kinds of distance. But a turn through $45^\circ$ keeps straight-line distance and changes grid distance: the point $(1, 0)$, at grid distance 1 from the origin, turns into a point at grid distance $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$. Felix Klein's insight (1872) was that a geometry is described by the transformations that keep its measurements — see <TopicLink id="math.geom.transformation-geometry">transformation geometry</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="The plane as a set of pairs">

For any two sets $A$ and $B$, the **Cartesian product** $A \times B$ is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \in A$ and $b \in B$. The coordinate plane *is* the Cartesian product $\R \times \R = \R^2$ of the real number line with itself, and the grid of points with whole-number coordinates is $\Z \times \Z$. Theorem 1 says that the points of the geometric plane and the elements of $\R \times \R$ match up exactly. The name “Cartesian” honours Descartes (in Latin, *Cartesius*). See <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">sets</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Maps.** Claudius Ptolemy's *Geography* (Alexandria, 2nd century CE) located about 8000 places by latitude and longitude — two numbers per place. In China, the cartographer Pei Xiu (3rd century CE) is credited with principles of map-making that included a rectangular grid for measuring distances.
- **Graphs.** In the 14th century, Nicole Oresme in France drew pictures of how a quantity, such as speed, changes over time, using horizontal and vertical lengths — an early form of a graph.
- **Coordinate geometry.** In the 1630s **René Descartes** (*La Géométrie*, 1637) and **Pierre de Fermat** (whose *Introduction to Plane and Solid Loci* circulated in manuscript around the same time) independently showed how to describe curves by equations relating two coordinates, uniting algebra and geometry. Neither used our modern picture with two perpendicular axes and negative coordinates on both; that settled into its familiar form over the following century.
- **Distances.** Around 1900 Hermann Minkowski studied geometries with unusual distances, including the grid distance; the name “taxicab geometry” came later.

</Section>
