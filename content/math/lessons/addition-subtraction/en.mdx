<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Mangoes at the market">

Priya helps her uncle at his fruit stall in a busy market in Chennai, India. The mangoes arrive in crates. Each crate holds **ten** mangoes.

In the morning, one customer buys 8 mangoes and another buys 5. How many mangoes did they buy altogether? Later, Priya counts 52 mangoes on the stall and sells 27 of them. How many are left?

Priya could count every mango one by one. But there are faster ways — and they all come from a few big ideas about **putting together**, **taking away**, and **tens and ones**.

**Subtracting** has two meanings.

- **Taking away:** you have 7 beads and give away 3. How many are left? $7 - 3 = 4$.
- **Comparing:** Amina has 12 shells and Lucas has 7. How many **more** does Amina have? Pair them up: 7 of Amina's shells have partners, and 5 do not. So Amina has 5 more: $12 - 7 = 5$.

Adding and taking away are like doing and undoing. If you put 3 beads in a bag and then take 3 beads out, you are back where you started.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="addition">

If one collection has $a$ members and another, with no members in common, has $b$ members, then $a + b$ is the number of members when we put them together.

</Definition>

<Definition term="subtraction">

When $b$ is not bigger than $a$, $a - b$ is the number left when we take $b$ members away from a collection of $a$ members. It is also the **difference** between two amounts: how many more one has than the other.

</Definition>

<Definition term="the equals sign">

The equals sign $=$ means **has the same value as**. A number sentence with $=$ is true when both sides are worth the same.

</Definition>

<Definition term="tens and ones">

In a two-digit number, the **left digit counts tens** and the **right digit counts ones**. So 52 means 5 tens and 2 ones: $52 = 50 + 2$.

</Definition>

<Narrator who="gideon">

Notice "no members in common" in the first big idea. If a red, round button is in both groups, you must not count it twice! You saw why in the lesson on <TopicLink id="math.number.counting">counting</TopicLink>: each thing gets exactly one number word.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Example title="Turn-around facts and doubles">

$2 + 9$ is the same as $9 + 2$, and counting on 2 from 9 is quicker than counting on 9 from 2. So start with the bigger number: $9 + 2 = 11$.

Doubles are easy to remember: $6 + 6 = 12$. Then a **near double** is one more: $6 + 7 = 13$.

<Example title="A fact family">

The numbers 3, 4 and 7 belong together:

$$
3 + 4 = 7 \qquad 4 + 3 = 7 \qquad 7 - 4 = 3 \qquad 7 - 3 = 4.
$$

Use a fact family to subtract. For $15 - 7$, ask: "7 and what make 15?" Since $7 + 8 = 15$, we know $15 - 7 = 8$.

</Example>

<Section id="why" kind="proof" title="Why the rules work">

**The turn-around rule.** Put 3 red beads and 4 blue beads in a row. Read the row from the left and you say $3 + 4$. Walk round to the other side and read it from the left again: now you say $4 + 3$. It is the **same row of beads**, so it has the same number of beads. That is why $3 + 4 = 4 + 3$ — and the same is true for any two numbers.

**Making ten.** When adding three numbers, it doesn't matter which two we add first: $(8 + 2) + 3 = 8 + (2 + 3)$. Both mean "all the beads in three little piles". Making ten uses this to swap a hard sum, $8 + 5$, for an easy one, $10 + 3$.

**Adding zero.** Putting together a group with an empty group changes nothing: $a + 0 = a$.

**Taking away undoes adding.** A bag has 9 beads in it. Put in 4 more, then take 4 beads out. You have taken away exactly as many as you put in, so 9 are left: $(9 + 4) - 4 = 9$.

**Subtraction does not turn around.** $5 - 3 = 2$: take 3 beads from 5 and 2 are left. But $3 - 5$? You cannot take 5 beads from a bag with only 3. So $5 - 3$ and $3 - 5$ are not the same.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Can we *ever* take 5 away from 3? Not with beads. But mathematicians found a way: they invented numbers below zero. That story is in the lesson on <TopicLink id="math.number.integers">integers</TopicLink>.

</Narrator>

**Why the tens decide.** The biggest number with 4 tens is 49. The smallest number with 5 tens is 50. So *every* number with 4 tens is smaller than *every* number with 5 tens. That is why we compare the tens first.

<ForGrownUps title="proofs for grown-ups">

Following Wu (2011), define $a + b$ as the number of members of $A \cup B$, where $A$ and $B$ are collections with $a$ and $b$ members and **no member in common**. (This is well defined because counting does not depend on the order or on which collections we pick — see the counting lesson.)

<Theorem name="Commutativity and associativity">

For all whole numbers $a, b, c$: $\;a + b = b + a$ and $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Choose pairwise disjoint collections $A, B, C$ with $a, b, c$ members. Then $a + b$ counts $A \cup B$ and $b + a$ counts $B \cup A$. These are the same collection (a thing is in one exactly when it is in the other), so they have the same number of members. Similarly $(a + b) + c$ counts $(A \cup B) \cup C$ and $a + (b + c)$ counts $A \cup (B \cup C)$, and both are the collection of things in at least one of $A$, $B$, $C$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Taking away undoes adding">

For all whole numbers $a, b$: $\;(a + b) - b = a$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Start with $A \cup B$, which has $a + b$ members, and take away the $b$ members of $B$. Since $A$ and $B$ have no member in common, exactly the members of $A$ remain, and there are $a$ of them.

</Proof>

For $b \le a$, $a - b$ is the unique whole number $c$ with $b + c = a$; for $b > a$ no such whole number exists, which is precisely why the integers are invented. The Lean section below proves the same laws a second way, from the definition of addition by counting on, $a + (b + 1) = (a + b) + 1$, using induction (Dedekind 1888, Peano 1889). For place value, every whole number $n$ is $10q + r$ for exactly one pair $q, r$ with $0 \le r \le 9$ (division with remainder); a two-digit numeral "$qr$" names $10q + r$.

</ForGrownUps>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"The answer always comes after the equals sign."** The equals sign means *has the same value as*, not *here comes the answer*. So $7 = 3 + 4$ is true, and so is $3 + 4 = 4 + 3$. In a puzzle like $9 + \square = 14$, the box is not 23!

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Subtraction turns around, like addition."** $3 + 5 = 5 + 3$, but $5 - 3$ is not the same as $3 - 5$. Taking 3 away from 5 leaves 2; taking 5 away from 3 cannot be done with counting numbers.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"52 means five and two."** The 5 is in the tens place, so it means 5 tens, which is 50. Fifty-two is $50 + 2$, and it is much bigger than 25.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Always take the smaller digit from the bigger one."** For $52 - 27$, it is tempting to do $7 - 2 = 5$ in the ones and get 35. But we must take 7 ones **from** 2 ones, which needs a ten to be broken first. The answer is 25. Checking by adding catches the mistake: $35 + 27 = 62$, not 52.

</Misconception>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="For grown-ups: other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="A commutative monoid, waiting to become a group">

The whole numbers with $+$ and $0$ satisfy $a + b = b + a$, $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ and $a + 0 = a$: they form a **commutative monoid**. Subtraction is only partly defined, because most elements have no additive inverse among the whole numbers. Adjoining inverses gives the integers, a **group** — the smallest one containing the whole numbers. "Add 3" and "take away 3" are the first example children meet of an action and its inverse. See <TopicLink id="math.cat.actions-and-undoing">doing and undoing</TopicLink>, <TopicLink id="math.number.integers">integers</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.algebra.algebraic-structures">monoids, groups and rings</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Ten is a choice">

Place value works in any base $b \ge 2$: every whole number can be written in exactly one way as a sum of powers of $b$ with digits from $0$ to $b - 1$ (repeated division with remainder). Base ten probably comes from our fingers. The Maya counted in twenties, Babylonian astronomers in sixties — a choice that survives in our 60 minutes to the hour — and computers work in base two. The comparison rule "most significant digit first" and the carrying rule work in every base for the same reason they work in base ten.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="Numbers with places, around the world">

- In **Mesopotamia**, about 4,000 years ago, Babylonian scribes wrote numbers with place value in base sixty.
- In **China**, counting rods were laid out in columns for ones, tens, hundreds, …, with an empty space for zero. Rod arithmetic is described in old texts such as the *Sunzi Suanjing*, written roughly 1,600 years ago.
- The **Maya** of Central America wrote numbers with place value, counting mostly in twenties, and used a sign for zero.
- In **ancient Egypt**, hieroglyphic numerals were *not* place value: one symbol for one, another for ten, another for a hundred, repeated as often as needed.
- The base-ten digits used across much of the world today, with place value and zero, were developed in **India**. They became known in the Islamic world through writers such as **al-Khwārizmī** in Baghdad (9th century), and in Europe partly through **Fibonacci**'s *Liber Abaci* (1202).
- The signs $+$ and $-$ appeared in print in a German arithmetic book by Johannes Widmann in 1489. The equals sign was invented by the Welsh mathematician **Robert Recorde** in *The Whetstone of Witte* (1557), who chose two parallel lines "bicause noe .2. thynges, can be moare equalle".
