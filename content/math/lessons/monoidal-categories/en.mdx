<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Side by side">

Composition $g \circ f$ puts two processes **in sequence**. Much of mathematics and physics also needs to put things **in parallel**: two independent choices form a pair $(x, y) \in X \times Y$; a quantum system made of two subsystems has state space $V \otimes W$; two electrical circuits sit next to each other on a board; two strands of a braid hang side by side; two programs run on separate inputs. Each situation has a binary operation on objects *and* on morphisms, $f \otimes g : A \otimes B \to A' \otimes B'$, together with a "do nothing" object $I$.

Such an operation is almost never associative on the nose. The sets $(X \times Y) \times Z$ and $X \times (Y \times Z)$ are different (their elements are $((x, y), z)$ and $(x, (y, z))$), though canonically isomorphic. A **monoidal category** keeps these canonical isomorphisms as data and asks for exactly the right laws between them: Mac Lane's **pentagon** and **triangle**. The surprise is that two small diagrams are enough to make *every* formal diagram built from these canonical isomorphisms commute — the coherence theorem. Asking for "commutativity up to isomorphism" leads to **braidings**, the **hexagon** axioms, and a genuine fork in the road: braidings that square to the identity (symmetric) and braidings that do not, the latter governed by Artin's braid groups and found in quantum groups, knot invariants and anyonic physics.

This lesson builds on <TopicLink id="math.cat.natural-transformations">natural transformations</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.cat.products-coproducts">products and coproducts</TopicLink>.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Strings, boxes and bracketings">

Draw an object as a vertical **string** and a morphism $f : A \to B$ as a **box** on a string, read from bottom to top. Composition stacks boxes vertically; the tensor product places strings and boxes **side by side**; the unit $I$ is drawn as *no string at all*. Functoriality of $\otimes$, the **interchange law**

$$
(g \otimes g') \circ (f \otimes f') = (g \circ f) \otimes (g' \circ f'),
$$

says that boxes on different strings slide freely past each other in height. A **braiding** $c_{A,B} : A \otimes B \to B \otimes A$ is drawn as a crossing of two strings — and whether $c_{B,A} \circ c_{A,B}$ is the identity is the question of whether a doubly-crossed pair of strings can be pulled straight.

Bracketings are the other half of the picture. A word $A_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes A_n$ can be bracketed in $C_{n-1}$ ways (the Catalan numbers $1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, \dots$), and single applications of the associator connect them into a graph: for $n = 4$ it is a **pentagon**, for $n = 5$ the 1-skeleton of a 3-dimensional polytope with 14 vertices and 21 edges (Stasheff's associahedron; the underlying order is Tamari's lattice). Coherence says that if the pentagon commutes, then every 2-dimensional face of every associahedron does, so any two paths of associators between two bracketings agree.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Monoidal, braided and symmetric">

<Definition term="monoidal category">

A **monoidal category** $(\mathcal{C}, \otimes, I, \alpha, \lambda, \rho)$ is a category $\mathcal{C}$ with a functor $\otimes : \mathcal{C} \times \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{C}$, an object $I$ (the **unit**), and natural isomorphisms

$$
\alpha_{A,B,C} : (A \otimes B) \otimes C \xrightarrow{\ \cong\ } A \otimes (B \otimes C), \qquad \lambda_A : I \otimes A \xrightarrow{\ \cong\ } A, \qquad \rho_A : A \otimes I \xrightarrow{\ \cong\ } A
$$

(the **associator** and the left and right **unitors**) such that the pentagon and triangle diagrams below commute for all objects. It is **strict** if $\alpha$, $\lambda$, $\rho$ are identities.

</Definition>

<Definition term="monoidal functor">

A **(strong) monoidal functor** $(F, \phi, \phi_0) : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ is a functor with natural isomorphisms $\phi_{A,B} : FA \otimes FB \to F(A \otimes B)$ and $\phi_0 : I \to FI$ compatible with the associators and unitors. If $\phi, \phi_0$ are merely morphisms it is **lax monoidal**. A **monoidal equivalence** is a strong monoidal functor that is an equivalence of categories.

</Definition>

<Definition term="braiding; symmetric monoidal category">

A **braiding** on a monoidal category is a natural isomorphism $c_{A,B} : A \otimes B \to B \otimes A$ satisfying the two **hexagon axioms**; with the associators suppressed they read

$$
c_{A, B \otimes C} = (B \otimes c_{A,C}) \circ (c_{A,B} \otimes C), \qquad c_{A \otimes B, C} = (c_{A,C} \otimes B) \circ (A \otimes c_{B,C}),
$$

i.e. moving $A$ past $B \otimes C$ is moving it past $B$ then past $C$, and likewise on the other side. A braided monoidal category is **symmetric** if $c_{B,A} \circ c_{A,B} = \mathrm{id}_{A \otimes B}$ for all $A, B$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="monoid object">

A **monoid object** in a monoidal category is an object $M$ with morphisms $\mu : M \otimes M \to M$ and $\eta : I \to M$ such that $\mu \circ (\mu \otimes M) = \mu \circ (M \otimes \mu) \circ \alpha_{M,M,M}$ and $\mu \circ (\eta \otimes M) = \lambda_M$, $\mu \circ (M \otimes \eta) = \rho_M$. A morphism of monoid objects is a morphism commuting with $\mu$ and $\eta$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="closed monoidal category">

A monoidal category is **(right) closed** if every functor $- \otimes A$ has a right adjoint $[A, -]$, the **internal hom**: $\mathcal{C}(B \otimes A, C) \cong \mathcal{C}(B, [A, C])$ naturally in $B$ and $C$.

</Definition>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Examples">

<Example title="Cartesian: (Set, ×, 1)">

$X \otimes Y = X \times Y$, $I = \{*\}$, $\alpha((x, y), z) = (x, (y, z))$, $\lambda(*, x) = x$, $\rho(x, *) = x$, and the braiding $c(x, y) = (y, x)$. More generally any category with finite products is monoidal under $\times$ with the terminal object as unit (a **cartesian** monoidal category), and symmetric. It is closed: $[Y, Z] = Z^Y$ and currying is the adjunction.

</Example>

<Example title="Cocartesian: (Set, ⊔, ∅)">

$X \otimes Y = X \sqcup Y$ (disjoint union, e.g. $X \times \{0\} \cup Y \times \{1\}$), $I = \varnothing$; the associator and unitors are the evident relabellings, and the braiding swaps the tags $0, 1$. Any category with finite coproducts is monoidal under $\sqcup$ with the initial object as unit. This structure is *not* closed: $- \sqcup Y$ does not preserve the initial object (for $Y \neq \varnothing$), so it has no right adjoint.

</Example>

<Example title="Linear: (Vect_k, ⊗, k)">

$V \otimes W$ is the tensor product over a field $k$, $I = k$, $\alpha((u \otimes v) \otimes w) = u \otimes (v \otimes w)$, $\lambda(c \otimes v) = cv$, and the braiding $c(v \otimes w) = w \otimes v$, all defined on pure tensors and extended linearly (well defined by the universal property). It is symmetric and closed with $[V, W] = \mathrm{Hom}_k(V, W)$. Here $\otimes$ is **not** a categorical product: a linear map $U \to V \otimes W$ is not a pair of maps, and there are no natural projections $V \otimes W \to V$. The same holds for $(\cat{Ab}, \otimes_{\Z}, \Z)$, for $R$-modules over a commutative ring, and for $(\cat{Hilb}, \otimes, \C)$.

</Example>

<Example title="Endofunctors: a strict, non-symmetric monoidal category">

For any category $\mathcal{C}$, the functor category $[\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{C}]$ is strict monoidal with $F \otimes G = F \circ G$ and $I = \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}$; on natural transformations $\otimes$ is horizontal composition. It is usually not braided: $F \circ G \not\cong G \circ F$ in general. Monoid objects here are exactly **monads** (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.monads">monads</TopicLink>).

</Example>

<Example title="Graded vector spaces with a twisted associator">

Let $G$ be a group and $\omega : G^3 \to k^\times$ a function. $\mathrm{Vec}_G^\omega$ is the category of finite-dimensional $G$-graded vector spaces with $(V \otimes W)_g = \bigoplus_{hh' = g} V_h \otimes W_{h'}$, unit the one-dimensional space $\delta_e$ in degree $e$, and associator acting on $V_a \otimes V_b \otimes V_c$ as multiplication by $\omega(a, b, c)$. On the simple objects $\delta_a$ the pentagon axiom becomes the **3-cocycle condition**

$$
\omega(b, c, d)\, \omega(a, bc, d)\, \omega(a, b, c) = \omega(ab, c, d)\, \omega(a, b, cd),
$$

and the triangle axiom (with identity unitors) the normalisation $\omega(a, e, c) = 1$. For $G = \Z/n$ and $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$, the cochains $\omega_p(a, b, c) = \zeta^{\,p\, a\, \mathrm{carry}(b, c)}$, where $\mathrm{carry}(b, c) = 1$ if $b + c \ge n$ (for representatives $0 \le b, c \lt n$) and $0$ otherwise, are 3-cocycles. *Proof:* write the condition additively in exponents. The number of wrap-arounds in computing $b + c + d$ does not depend on the bracketing, so $\mathrm{carry}(b, c) + \mathrm{carry}(b + c, d) = \mathrm{carry}(c, d) + \mathrm{carry}(b, c + d)$; substituting, the two sides differ by $p\,(a + b - [a + b])\,\mathrm{carry}(c, d) = p\,n\,\mathrm{carry}(a, b)\,\mathrm{carry}(c, d) \equiv 0 \pmod n$. These cochains represent all of $H^3(\Z/n, \C^\times) \cong \Z/n$: up to changing the tensor-product isomorphisms by a 2-cochain, there are exactly $n$ associators on $\Z/n$-graded vector spaces with $\delta_a \otimes \delta_b = \delta_{a+b}$.

</Example>

<Example title="Representations">

For a group $G$, finite-dimensional representations form a symmetric monoidal category $\mathrm{Rep}(G)$ with $g \cdot (v \otimes w) = gv \otimes gw$ and the trivial representation as unit. For a Hopf algebra $H$ the comultiplication makes $\mathrm{Rep}(H)$ monoidal, and an $R$-matrix (a quasi-triangular structure) makes it braided. For the quantum group $U_q(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$ at generic $q$ the resulting braiding is **not** symmetric (see the second experiment).

</Example>

<Example title="Braids">

The braid category $\mathcal{B}$ has objects $0, 1, 2, \dots$, morphisms $n \to n$ the elements of Artin's braid group $B_n$ (none between different $n$), and $m \otimes n = m + n$ by placing braids side by side. The crossing $\sigma \in B_2$ is a braiding $1 \otimes 1 \to 1 \otimes 1$. Since $B_2 \cong \Z$ is generated by $\sigma$, $\sigma^2 \neq \mathrm{id}$: $\mathcal{B}$ is braided but not symmetric. By a theorem of Joyal and Street, $\mathcal{B}$ is the free braided monoidal category on one object.

</Example>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Theorems">

<Theorem name="Proposition 1 ((Set, ×, 1) is symmetric monoidal)">

With the structure maps above, $(\cat{Set}, \times, \{*\})$ satisfies the pentagon, triangle and hexagon axioms, and $c_{Y,X} \circ c_{X,Y} = \mathrm{id}$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Naturality of $\alpha, \lambda, \rho, c$ is immediate from the formulas. Pentagon: both composites send $(((w, x), y), z)$ to $(w, (x, (y, z)))$ — the upper path via $((w, x), (y, z))$, the lower via $((w, (x, y)), z)$ and $(w, ((x, y), z))$. Triangle: both sides send $((x, *), y)$ to $(x, y)$. Hexagon (first form, associators included): both sides send $((x, y), z)$ to $(y, (z, x))$; the second is similar. Symmetry: $c(c(x, y)) = c(y, x) = (x, y)$. The same element-chasing proves the claims for $(\cat{Set}, \sqcup, \varnothing)$, and for $(\cat{Vect}_k, \otimes, k)$ it suffices to check on pure tensors $u \otimes v \otimes w$, which span.

</Proof>

The next result is the first sign that "commutativity" is forced one dimension up.

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (Eckmann–Hilton)">

Let $X$ be a set with two binary operations $\cdot$ and $\ast$, with units $1_\cdot$ and $1_\ast$, satisfying the **interchange law** $(a \ast b) \cdot (c \ast d) = (a \cdot c) \ast (b \cdot d)$. Then $1_\cdot = 1_\ast$, the two operations coincide, and they are commutative (and associative).

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Put $a = d = 1_\ast$, $b = c = 1_\cdot$: the left side is $(1_\ast \ast 1_\cdot) \cdot (1_\cdot \ast 1_\ast) = 1_\cdot \cdot 1_\cdot = 1_\cdot$ and the right side is $(1_\ast \cdot 1_\cdot) \ast (1_\cdot \cdot 1_\ast) = 1_\ast \ast 1_\ast = 1_\ast$. Call the common unit $1$. Then

$$
a \cdot b = (a \ast 1) \cdot (1 \ast b) = (a \cdot 1) \ast (1 \cdot b) = a \ast b, \qquad a \cdot b = (1 \ast a) \cdot (b \ast 1) = (1 \cdot b) \ast (a \cdot 1) = b \ast a = b \cdot a .
$$

Associativity follows similarly: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = (a \ast b) \cdot (1 \ast c) = (a \cdot 1) \ast (b \cdot c) = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (End(I) is commutative; Kelly, Kelly–Laplaza)">

In any monoidal category, $\lambda_I = \rho_I : I \otimes I \to I$, and the monoid $\mathrm{End}(I)$ is commutative.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

That $\lambda_I = \rho_I$ follows from the pentagon and triangle axioms (Kelly 1964; the commutativity of $\mathrm{End}(I)$ is recorded by Kelly and Laplaza, 1980); a proof is in Etingof–Gelaki–Nikshych–Ostrik, *Tensor Categories*, §2.2, and Mathlib proves it as `unitors_equal`. Write $u = \lambda_I = \rho_I$. For $f, g \in \mathrm{End}(I)$, naturality of $\lambda$ and $\rho$ gives

$$
u \circ (I \otimes g) = g \circ u, \qquad u \circ (f \otimes I) = f \circ u ,
$$

and the interchange law gives $(f \otimes I) \circ (I \otimes g) = f \otimes g = (I \otimes g) \circ (f \otimes I)$. Hence

$$
g \circ f \circ u = g \circ u \circ (f \otimes I) = u \circ (I \otimes g) \circ (f \otimes I) = u \circ (f \otimes I) \circ (I \otimes g) = f \circ u \circ (I \otimes g) = f \circ g \circ u ,
$$

and since $u$ is invertible, $g \circ f = f \circ g$.

</Proof>

This is Eckmann–Hilton in disguise: on $\mathrm{End}(I)$, composition and $\otimes$ (transported along $u$) are two unital operations satisfying interchange. For example, $\mathrm{End}(I) = k$ in $\cat{Vect}_k$, and in $[\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{C}]$ it is the commutative monoid of natural transformations $\mathrm{id} \Rightarrow \mathrm{id}$ (the centre of $\mathcal{C}$).

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (Mac Lane's coherence theorem, 1963)">

(a) In a monoidal category, every formal diagram built from components of $\alpha$, $\lambda$, $\rho$, their inverses, identities and $\otimes$ commutes. Precisely: in the free monoidal category on a set of objects, any two parallel morphisms are equal. (b) Every monoidal category is monoidally equivalent to a strict monoidal category.

</Theorem>

*Proof sketch and reference.* For (a), send every bracketed word to its normal form (all brackets to the right, units deleted); one shows that the canonical map from a word to its normal form is well defined, using the pentagon to commute any two associator moves and the triangle to handle units. Then any two canonical paths $w \to w'$ both factor through the normal form, hence agree. Mac Lane's original argument contains this; a careful account is in Mac Lane, *CWM*, VII.2 (coherence) and XI.3 (strictification), and Mathlib formalises (a) (`FreeMonoidalCategory.subsingleton_hom`). For (b), replace $\mathcal{C}$ by the category of finite lists of objects of $\mathcal{C}$, with concatenation as a strict tensor product and morphisms computed after tensoring out (EGNO, §2.8). Coherence is about **formal** diagrams: in $(\cat{Set}, \times)$ the swap $X \times X \to X \times X$ and the identity are both "canonical", yet different — they come from different formal expressions.

For braided categories the analogue is that formal diagrams commute *when their underlying braids are equal* (Joyal–Street); for symmetric ones, when their underlying permutations are equal.

<Theorem name="Proposition 5 (monoid objects)">

Monoid objects in $(\cat{Set}, \times, 1)$ are monoids; in $(\cat{Ab}, \otimes_{\Z}, \Z)$ they are rings; in $(\cat{Vect}_k, \otimes, k)$ they are $k$-algebras; in $([\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{C}], \circ, \mathrm{id})$ they are monads; in $(\cat{Set}, \sqcup, \varnothing)$ every object has exactly one monoid structure.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

In $\cat{Set}$, $\mu : M \times M \to M$ is a binary operation and $\eta : \{*\} \to M$ picks an element; the axioms, evaluated on elements, are associativity and the unit laws. In $\cat{Ab}$, a homomorphism $\mu : M \otimes M \to M$ is the same as a **biadditive** map $M \times M \to M$ (universal property of $\otimes$), i.e. a multiplication distributing over addition, and $\eta : \Z \to M$ is determined by $1 := \eta(1)$; the axioms (checked on pure tensors) are associativity and the unit laws, so $M$ is a ring. Replacing $\Z$ by $k$ gives $k$-algebras. In endofunctors, $\mu : T \circ T \Rightarrow T$ and $\eta : \mathrm{id} \Rightarrow T$, and the axioms are the monad laws. In $(\cat{Set}, \sqcup, \varnothing)$, $\eta : \varnothing \to M$ is unique and the unit laws force $\mu : M \sqcup M \to M$ to be the identity on each copy (the **codiagonal**); it is associative.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Proposition 6 (monoids in monoids are commutative)">

A monoid object in the cartesian monoidal category $(\cat{Mon}, \times, 1)$ is a commutative monoid, and its two multiplications coincide.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

A monoid object in $\cat{Mon}$ is a monoid $(M, \cdot, 1)$ whose multiplication $\ast : M \times M \to M$ and unit are monoid homomorphisms. "$\ast$ is a homomorphism" is exactly the interchange law $(a \cdot c) \ast (b \cdot d) = (a \ast b) \cdot (c \ast d)$, so Eckmann–Hilton applies: $\ast = \cdot$ and both are commutative.

</Proof>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Every braided monoidal category is symmetric."** A braiding need not square to the identity. In the braid category $\sigma^2 \neq \mathrm{id}$ because $B_2 \cong \Z$; in $\mathrm{Rep}(U_q(\mathfrak{sl}_2))$ at generic $q$ the R-matrix satisfies $R^2 = 1 + (q - q^{-1})R \neq 1$. Symmetric is a property; braided is extra structure, and a monoidal category may carry several braidings.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The monoidal product is the cartesian product."** $\otimes$ in $\cat{Vect}_k$, $\cat{Ab}$ or $\cat{Hilb}$ is not a product: there are no natural projections $V \otimes W \to V$ and no natural diagonal $V \to V \otimes V$ (copying $v + w$ would give $(v + w) \otimes (v + w) \neq v \otimes v + w \otimes w$ — the linear-algebra core of the quantum no-cloning theorem). Cartesian monoidal categories are a special case, characterised by natural diagonals and projections.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Coherence means every diagram commutes."** Only *formal* diagrams built from $\alpha, \lambda, \rho$ commute. If two different formal expressions happen to evaluate to the same object (e.g. $X \otimes X$), the canonical maps between them may differ: the symmetry of $X \times X$ is not the identity.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"By strictification we may always assume the associator is the identity and the category is skeletal."** Every monoidal category is equivalent to a strict one, but not always to one that is both strict and skeletal: an observation of Isbell (discussed in Mac Lane's book) shows that the skeleton of $(\cat{Set}, \times)$ cannot be made strict. Likewise in $\mathrm{Vec}_{\Z/n}^{\omega_p}$ with $p \not\equiv 0 \pmod n$ the associator cannot be made the identity while keeping one object per isomorphism class: a strict model needs many isomorphic copies of each simple object.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Viewpoints">

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Loops, configuration spaces and E_n">

Eckmann and Hilton found their argument for **homotopy groups**: for $n \ge 2$, $\pi_n(X)$ has two composition laws (concatenate in the first or the second coordinate of $[0,1]^n$) satisfying interchange, so $\pi_n(X)$ is abelian, while $\pi_1$ need not be. The same mechanism stratifies monoidal structures: a monoidal category is an "$E_1$-algebra" in categories (objects can be multiplied along a line), a braided monoidal category an $E_2$-algebra (in the plane, where points can circle each other), and for $n \ge 3$ an $E_n$-structure on a 1-category is just a symmetric monoidal structure. The braid group $B_n$ is the fundamental group of the space of $n$ unordered distinct points in $\R^2$ (Fox–Neuwirth 1962); in $\R^3$ that fundamental group is the symmetric group $S_n$, because a double exchange can be undone in three dimensions. This is the topological reason why braidings are "2-dimensional" and symmetries "3-dimensional and up" (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.fundamental-group">the fundamental group</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.geom.homotopy-theory">homotopy theory</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="String diagrams and knot invariants">

Joyal and Street (1991) proved that string diagrams are a sound and complete calculus: an equation between morphisms of the free monoidal category on a set of generating boxes holds if and only if the corresponding planar diagrams are isotopic (in the plane, keeping endpoints fixed). For braided categories the diagrams live in 3-dimensional space and isotopy is of braids and tangles; for symmetric categories strings may pass through each other. Adding duals and a twist gives **ribbon categories**, whose diagrams are framed tangles; Reshetikhin and Turaev (1990) turned this into invariants of framed links and 3-manifolds from quantum groups — the Jones polynomial arises from the R-matrix of $U_q(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$ in the second experiment (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.knot-theory">knot theory</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.geom.low-dimensional-topology">low-dimensional topology</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Composite systems, no-cloning and anyons">

In quantum mechanics the state space of a composite system is $\mathcal{H}_1 \otimes \mathcal{H}_2$, and entangled states are exactly the elements that are not pure tensors — a phenomenon impossible in a cartesian category, where every map into $A \times B$ is a pair. Abramsky and Coecke (2004) recast quantum protocols such as teleportation as calculations in symmetric monoidal categories with duals, drawn with string diagrams (see <TopicLink id="math.applied.quantum-mechanics">quantum mechanics</TopicLink>). Exchange statistics are a braiding: in three spatial dimensions exchanging two identical particles twice is trivial, giving bosons and fermions (the symmetric case, with the sign rule of super vector spaces for fermions); in two spatial dimensions it need not be, and quasiparticles called **anyons** are described by braided (modular) tensor categories (Leinaas and Myrheim 1977; Wilczek 1982). Kitaev (2003) proposed using non-abelian anyons for fault-tolerant quantum computation.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Cohomology, Hopf algebras and Tannaka duality">

The associators on $G$-graded vector spaces with $\delta_g \otimes \delta_h = \delta_{gh}$ are classified by $H^3(G, k^\times)$, so group cohomology measures exactly how non-associative (up to coherent isomorphism) such a category can be; braidings on them are classified by Eilenberg–Mac Lane's abelian cohomology. For an algebra $H$, monoidal structures on $\mathrm{Rep}(H)$ compatible with the forgetful functor correspond to bialgebra structures, and braidings to R-matrices (Drinfeld). Conversely, **Tannaka–Krein duality** recovers a group from its symmetric monoidal category of representations with the forgetful functor; Deligne (2002) showed that, over an algebraically closed field of characteristic 0, symmetric tensor categories of moderate growth are representation categories of affine supergroups (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.representation-theory">representation theory</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="categories" title="One object, one dimension up">

A monoidal category is a bicategory with one object: objects of $\mathcal{C}$ become 1-cells, $\otimes$ becomes composition of 1-cells, and morphisms become 2-cells. Iterating: a braided monoidal category is a tricategory with one object and one 1-cell, and the Eckmann–Hilton argument explains why one-object, one-1-cell 2-categories are commutative monoids. Monoidal categories are also the natural bases for **enrichment**: replacing hom-sets by hom-objects in a monoidal category $\mathcal{V}$ gives additive categories, 2-categories and Lawvere's metric spaces (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.enriched-categories">enriched categories</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.cat.higher-categories">higher categories</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- Products of spaces, tensor products of modules and convolution of functions were used long before the axioms; the interchange law appeared in **Beno Eckmann** and **Peter Hilton**'s work on group-like structures (1962), and the bracketings of a product were studied by **Dov Tamari** (1951) and, as cell complexes, by **Jim Stasheff** (1963) in his theory of $A_\infty$-spaces.
- **Jean Bénabou** (1963, "catégories avec multiplication") and **Saunders Mac Lane** (1963, "Natural associativity and commutativity") introduced monoidal categories; Mac Lane proved the coherence theorem and found the pentagon and hexagon. **G. Max Kelly** (1964) showed that some of Mac Lane's axioms were redundant, and with **Samuel Eilenberg** developed closed categories (1966).
- **André Joyal** and **Ross Street** introduced braided monoidal categories (circulated 1986, published 1993) and the geometry of string diagrams (1991). **Vladimir Drinfeld** and **Michio Jimbo** introduced quantum groups (1985–86), and **Nicolai Reshetikhin** and **Vladimir Turaev** (1990–91) built link and 3-manifold invariants from their braided categories of representations, following **Vaughan Jones**'s discovery of his polynomial (1984).
- **Samson Abramsky** and **Bob Coecke** (2004) began categorical quantum mechanics; **Alexei Kitaev** (2003) and **Michael Freedman** and collaborators connected braided categories to topological quantum computation.

</Section>
