How many sticks does a row of $n$ squares need? Each of them finds a rule:

- **Amara:** "Start with one stick on the left. Every square then adds 3 sticks." That gives $3n + 1$.
- **Kenji:** "The first square needs 4. Each of the other $n - 1$ squares needs 3." That gives $4 + 3(n - 1)$.
- **Lucía:** "Every square has 4 sides, but $n - 1$ sides are shared and were counted twice." That gives $4n - (n - 1)$.

They try $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ and get $4, 7, 10, 13, 16$ every time. Are the three rules *always* the same — for a row of a thousand squares, or a million? Trying values cannot settle that, because there are infinitely many values to try. In this lesson we learn the language that lets us answer such questions once and for all: **variables** and **algebraic expressions**.

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Letters that stand for numbers">

In $3n + 1$, the letter $n$ is a **variable**: a name for a number that we have not fixed. It is like the box in $3 \times \square + 1$ from your work on <TopicLink id="math.algebra.expressions-and-unknowns">expressions and unknowns</TopicLink>, except that a letter can appear several times and always means the same number each time it appears.

An expression such as $3n + 1$ is a **recipe**: give it a number, follow the instructions, and a number comes out. Putting a number in place of the variable is called **substituting** or **evaluating**.

<Example title="Evaluating with a negative number">

Evaluate $2x^2 - 5x$ when $x = -3$. Put the number in brackets first, then follow the order of operations (powers, then multiplication, then addition and subtraction):

$$
2x^2 - 5x = 2 \cdot (-3)^2 - 5 \cdot (-3) = 2 \cdot 9 + 15 = 33.
$$

The brackets matter: $(-3)^2 = 9$, but $-3^2$ means $-(3^2) = -9$.

</Example>

<Narrator who="chloe">

A variable is not a code for an object. In $3a + 2$, the letter $a$ is not "apples": it is a *number* — perhaps the number of apples, or a price, or a temperature. And a variable is allowed to be negative (see <TopicLink id="math.number.integers">integers</TopicLink>), or zero, or a fraction, unless we say otherwise.

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The vocabulary of algebra">

<Definition term="variable">

A **variable** is a letter that stands for any number from a given set — for example, any integer, or any rational number. Different letters *may* stand for the same number; the same letter always stands for the same number within one expression.

</Definition>

<Definition term="algebraic expression">

An **algebraic expression** is built from numbers and variables using addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and powers, with brackets to show the order. The parts joined by $+$ or $-$ are its **terms**; the number multiplying the variables in a term is its **coefficient**; a term with no variable is a **constant**. In $4x^2 - 7x + 2$ the terms are $4x^2$, $-7x$ and $2$, and the coefficient of $x$ is $-7$.

</Definition>

We write products without a dot: $3n$ means $3 \cdot n$, and $ab$ means $a \cdot b$.

<Definition term="like terms">

Two terms are **like terms** if they contain the same variables raised to the same powers. $3x$ and $-5x$ are like terms; $3x$ and $3x^2$ are not; neither are $2ab$ and $2a$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="equivalent expressions">

Two expressions are **equivalent** (on a given set of numbers) if they have the **same value for every value** of their variables in that set. We then write them with an equals sign, as an **identity**: $4 + 3(n - 1) = 3n + 1$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="power">

For a whole number $n \geq 1$, the **power** $a^n$ is the product of $n$ factors, each equal to $a$: $\;a^n = a \cdot a \cdots a$. The number $a$ is the **base** and $n$ the **exponent**. For $a \neq 0$ we define $a^0 = 1$ (we shall see why).

</Definition>

<Note>

The set matters. The expressions $\tfrac{x}{x}$ and $1$ are equivalent on the non-zero numbers, but not on all numbers: at $x = 0$ the first one has no value at all.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Writing, simplifying, expanding, factorising">

<Example title="Writing expressions for situations">

- A taxi in Lima charges a fixed 5 soles plus 2 soles per kilometre. A ride of $k$ kilometres costs $5 + 2k$ soles.
- A rectangle with sides $\ell$ and $w$ has perimeter $\ell + w + \ell + w = 2\ell + 2w$.
- The temperature in Ulaanbaatar was $t$ degrees at dawn and rose by $9$ degrees by noon: it is now $t + 9$ degrees. If $t = -23$, that is $-14$ degrees.

</Example>

<Example title="Combining like terms">

$$
4x + 3y - x + 5y - 2 = (4 - 1)x + (3 + 5)y - 2 = 3x + 8y - 2.
$$

We may only combine **like** terms: $3x + 8y$ cannot be squeezed into one term (it is *not* $11xy$), just as 3 metres plus 8 kilograms is not 11 of anything.

</Example>

<Example title="Expanding with the distributive law">

The **distributive law** $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ multiplies every term inside the bracket:

- $3(2x - 5) = 6x - 15$.
- $-2(x - 4) = -2x + 8$ — the $-2$ multiplies *both* terms, and $(-2)(-4) = +8$.
- $5 - (x - 3) = 5 - x + 3 = 8 - x$.

To multiply two brackets, use the law twice. A grid keeps track of the four products:

| $\times$ | $x$ | $3$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $x$ | $x^2$ | $3x$ |
| $2$ | $2x$ | $6$ |

so $(x + 3)(x + 2) = x^2 + 3x + 2x + 6 = x^2 + 5x + 6$.

</Example>

<Example title="Factorising: the distributive law backwards">

**Factorising** writes a sum as a product. Look for a common factor of all the terms:

- $6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)$, because $3$ divides both $6x$ and $9$.
- $12x + 18 = 6(2x + 3)$. (Writing $3(4x + 6)$ is correct but not *complete*: $4x + 6$ still has the common factor $2$.)
- $x^2 - 2x = x(x - 2)$.

You can always check a factorisation by expanding it again.

</Example>

<Example title="Non-examples: tempting but wrong">

- $3x + 2y$ is not $5xy$. Try $x = y = 1$: both expressions give $5$, so the test passes! But $x = 1$, $y = 2$ gives $7$ for $3x + 2y$ and $10$ for $5xy$.
- $x + x = 2x$, not $x^2$: at $x = 3$, $x + x = 6$ but $x^2 = 9$.
- $(x + 3)^2 \neq x^2 + 9$: at $x = 1$ the left side is $16$ and the right side is $10$.

Notice that in the first non-example a single test *passed* by luck. One passing test proves nothing; one failing test is enough to show two expressions are **not** equivalent.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Why the rules work">

Every rule for rearranging expressions comes from a few basic laws of arithmetic, which hold for **all** numbers: commutativity ($ab = ba$, $a + b = b + a$), associativity, and the distributive law $a(b + c) = ab + ac$. Because the laws hold for every number, anything we derive from them holds for every number too. That is how we can answer a question about infinitely many values in a few lines.

<Theorem name="combining like terms">

For all numbers $a$, $b$ and $x$: $\;ax + bx = (a + b)x$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By commutativity, $ax + bx = xa + xb$, and by the distributive law $xa + xb = x(a + b) = (a + b)x$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="the three stick-counting rules agree">

For every number $n$: $\;4 + 3(n - 1) = 3n + 1\;$ and $\;4n - (n - 1) = 3n + 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By the distributive law, $3(n - 1) = 3n - 3$, so $4 + 3(n - 1) = 4 + 3n - 3 = 3n + 1$. For Lucía's rule, subtracting $n - 1$ means subtracting $n$ and adding $1$: $4n - (n - 1) = 4n - n + 1 = 3n + 1$.

</Proof>

So Amara, Kenji and Lucía will agree for every row of squares there could ever be. Notice what the proof did *not* do: it did not try any values.

<Theorem name="square of a sum">

For all numbers $a$ and $b$: $\;(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Use the distributive law twice, then combine like terms (using $ba = ab$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
(a + b)^2 &= (a + b)(a + b) \\
&= a(a + b) + b(a + b) \\
&= a^2 + ab + ba + b^2 \\
&= a^2 + 2ab + b^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Proof>

The theorem tells us exactly how wrong the tempting rule $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$ is: it leaves out $2ab$. So the tempting rule gives the right answer only when $2ab = 0$.

<Theorem name="when the tempting rule happens to work">

For integers $a$ and $b$: $\;(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2\;$ if and only if $\;a = 0$ or $b = 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By the square of a sum, the equation says $a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = a^2 + b^2$, that is, $2ab = 0$, that is, $ab = 0$. A product of two non-zero integers is never $0$ (its size is at least $1 \cdot 1$), so $ab = 0$ exactly when $a = 0$ or $b = 0$.

</Proof>

The same kind of argument shows exactly when $2x$ and $x^2$ — two expressions that are often confused — take the same value.

<Theorem name="2x and x² agree only at 0 and 2">

For an integer $x$: $\;2x = x^2\;$ if and only if $\;x = 0$ or $x = 2$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$2x = x^2$ means $x^2 - 2x = 0$. Factorising, $x(x - 2) = 0$. A product of integers is $0$ exactly when one of the factors is $0$, so $x = 0$ or $x - 2 = 0$, that is, $x = 2$. Conversely, $2 \cdot 0 = 0 = 0^2$ and $2 \cdot 2 = 4 = 2^2$.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="exponents" kind="proof" title="Powers and the laws of exponents">

Powers are shorthand for repeated multiplication: $2^5 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 32$. Counting factors gives three laws.

<Theorem name="laws of exponents">

For all numbers $a$, $b$ and all whole numbers $m, n \geq 1$:

1. $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$,
2. $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$,
3. $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

1. $a^m \cdot a^n$ is $m$ factors of $a$ followed by $n$ more. By associativity the brackets do not matter, so this is a product of $m + n$ factors of $a$, which is $a^{m+n}$.
2. $(a^m)^n$ is $n$ blocks, each consisting of $m$ factors of $a$: $m \cdot n$ factors in all, so it is $a^{mn}$.
3. $(ab)^n = (ab)(ab)\cdots(ab)$ with $n$ blocks. By commutativity and associativity we may reorder the $2n$ factors as $(a \cdots a)(b \cdots b) = a^n b^n$.

</Proof>

<Example title="Using the laws — and avoiding the traps">

- $2^3 \cdot 2^4 = 2^7 = 128$. It is **not** $2^{12}$ (that would be multiplying the exponents) and **not** $4^7$ (the bases are not multiplied).
- $(2^3)^4 = 2^{12} = 4096$, while $2^{3^4} = 2^{81}$ is enormously bigger: the brackets matter.
- $(3x)^2 = 9x^2$, not $3x^2$.
- $2^6 + 2^6 = 2 \cdot 2^6 = 2^7$, not $2^{12}$: the laws are about **products** of powers, not sums.
- $(-2)^3 = -8$ and $(-2)^4 = 16$: an odd power of a negative number is negative, an even power is positive.

</Example>

**Why $a^0 = 1$?** The laws above were proved for exponents $1, 2, 3, \dots$. If we want the first law to keep working when an exponent is $0$, then $a^1 \cdot a^0 = a^{1+0} = a^1$, so $a \cdot a^0 = a$. For $a \neq 0$ we can divide both sides by $a$, and the only possible value is $a^0 = 1$. So $a^0 = 1$ is not an arbitrary rule: it is the only choice that keeps the laws true.

<Note>

What about $0^0$? Many mathematicians (and Lean) define $0^0 = 1$ too, because it makes formulas such as the binomial theorem work without exceptions. Some school books leave it undefined. Both are consistent; the argument above simply cannot decide the value when $a = 0$.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="testing" kind="human" title="Testing is not proving">

Testing values is a good way to *check* algebra, and it is all you need to show that two expressions are **not** equivalent: one counterexample is enough. But passing tests can never prove an equivalence on its own. Here is a striking example.

<Theorem name="four tests can mislead">

The expressions $x + x(x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)$ and $x$ agree for $x = 0, 1, 2, 3$, but they are not equivalent.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For $x = 0, 1, 2$ or $3$, one of the factors $x$, $x - 1$, $x - 2$, $x - 3$ is zero, so the product is $0$ and the first expression equals $x$. But for $x = 4$ the first expression is $4 + 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 28 \neq 4$.

</Proof>

Someone who tested only $0, 1, 2, 3$ would be fooled. By changing the factors, you can build two expressions that agree on a thousand test values and still differ at the next one.

<Note>

There is a surprising twist, which you will prove in the lesson on <TopicLink id="math.algebra.polynomials">polynomials</TopicLink>: if two polynomial expressions in one variable both have degree at most $d$, and they agree at $d + 1$ different values, then they are equivalent. For instance, $4 + 3(n - 1)$ and $3n + 1$ have degree $1$, so agreeing at $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ already forces them to agree everywhere. But notice what does the proving here: the **theorem**, and the knowledge of the degree — not the tests on their own.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$."** This forgets the two rectangles $ab$ in the area picture. The correct identity is $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, and the tempting rule is right only when $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. One counterexample settles it: $(1 + 1)^2 = 4$, but $1^2 + 1^2 = 2$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$2x$ and $x^2$ are the same."** $2x = x + x$ is *doubling*; $x^2 = x \cdot x$ is *squaring*. At $x = 3$ they are $6$ and $9$. They agree only at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$ — which is exactly why a quick test with $x = 2$ can mislead.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A variable must be positive" or "different letters must have different values."** A variable stands for *any* number of the stated kind. In $x + y$, nothing stops $x$ from being $-4$, or $y$ from being equal to $x$. When we need a variable to be positive, or two variables to differ, we must say so.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$3a$ means three apples."** Letters stand for *numbers*, not objects. If $a$ is the price of an apple in rupees, $3a$ is the price of three apples. Reading letters as objects leads to errors such as "$3a + 2b = 5ab$".

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$-x^2$ and $(-x)^2$ are the same."** The power is done before the minus sign: $-x^2 = -(x^2)$, which is never positive, while $(-x)^2 = x^2$, which is never negative. At $x = 3$: $-9$ and $9$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Algebra as area">

For positive numbers, a product $ab$ is the area of an $a$ by $b$ rectangle, and the distributive law $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ says that a rectangle cut in two has the total area of its pieces. The area picture of $(a + b)^2$ above is very old: Proposition 4 of Book II of Euclid's *Elements* (Alexandria, around 300 BCE) states, in the language of lines and squares, that the square on a line cut in two equals the squares on the two parts together with twice the rectangle contained by them. The picture is a proof for positive lengths; the algebraic proof extends it to all numbers.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="The same rules, in other number systems">

Our proofs used only a short list of laws: commutativity, associativity, the distributive law, and the behaviour of $0$ and $1$. Any number system obeying those laws — mathematicians call it a **commutative ring** (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.rings">rings</TopicLink>) — inherits every identity in this lesson for free. One example is clock arithmetic, where we keep only the remainder after dividing by a fixed number (see <TopicLink id="math.number.modular-arithmetic">modular arithmetic</TopicLink>). There is a delightful twist: working with remainders on division by $2$, the term $2ab$ is always $0$, so there the "tempting rule" $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$ is actually **true**. Whether a rule holds depends on which laws your numbers obey.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="sets" title="An expression is a function">

An expression in $x$, together with the set its variable ranges over, defines a <TopicLink id="math.found.functions">function</TopicLink>: $x \mapsto 2x^2 - 5x$. Two expressions are equivalent exactly when they define the **same** function — the same set of input–output pairs. So "$\tfrac{x}{x}$ and $1$ are equivalent on the non-zero numbers, but not on all numbers" is a statement about the domain of a function, and graphing an expression is drawing that set of pairs.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- Around the 3rd century CE, **Diophantus** of Alexandria wrote his *Arithmetica* with a special abbreviation for the unknown number and for its powers — a step from writing algebra in full sentences towards symbols.
- In India, mathematicians from **Brahmagupta** (*Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, 628 CE) onwards handled several unknowns at once, naming the first *yāvat-tāvat* ("as much as") and further ones after colours such as *kālaka* ("black") and *nīlaka* ("blue"); **Bhāskara II**'s *Bījagaṇita* (1150) writes them with abbreviated syllables.
- In Baghdad around 820 CE, **Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī** called the unknown *shayʾ*, "thing", and wrote all his algebra in words. The word *algebra* comes from the *al-jabr* in the title of his book.
- In China, **Li Ye** (*Ceyuan haijing*, 1248) and **Zhu Shijie** (1303) wrote polynomials with the *tiān yuán* ("celestial element") method: a column of counting-rod numerals in which the position of each coefficient showed the power of the unknown.
- In France, **François Viète** (1591) used letters both for unknowns (vowels) and for known quantities (consonants), so that one calculation solved a whole family of problems. **René Descartes**' *La Géométrie* (1637) introduced the convention we still use — $x, y, z$ for unknowns, $a, b, c$ for known quantities — and raised exponents such as $x^3$.

</Section>
