<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Three injera, four friends">

Hana and three friends in Addis Ababa have **3** injera to share equally. That is $3$ flatbreads for $4$ people, so nobody can have a whole one.

Hana has an idea. She cuts **each** injera into $4$ equal pieces. Now there are $12$ pieces, and each friend takes $3$ of them. Each piece is **one quarter** of an injera, so each friend gets **three quarters**:

$$
3 \div 4 = \tfrac{3}{4}.
$$

In <TopicLink id="math.number.whole-number-arithmetic">whole-number arithmetic</TopicLink>, $3 \div 4$ was "$0$ remainder $3$". Fractions are how we answer "how much?" exactly, when the answer is not a whole number. In this lesson you will see that a fraction is a **number** in its own right, with its own place on the number line.

</Section>

So a fraction is not only "part of a pizza". It is a **length**, and a **point** on the number line, just like $1$, $2$ and $3$. Some fractions are bigger than $1$: five quarters, $\tfrac54$, is one whole and one more quarter, which we can write as the **mixed number** $1\tfrac14$. Whole numbers are fractions too: $2 = \tfrac21 = \tfrac42 = \tfrac84$.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The words we need">

<Definition term="unit fraction">

Let $b$ be a whole number bigger than $0$. Cut the stretch from $0$ to $1$ into $b$ equal parts. The length of each part is the **unit fraction** $\tfrac{1}{b}$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="fraction">

For whole numbers $a$ and $b$ with $b > 0$, the fraction $\tfrac{a}{b}$ is the length of $a$ parts of size $\tfrac{1}{b}$, laid end to end. The bottom number $b$ is the **denominator**: it names the size of the parts. The top number $a$ is the **numerator**: it counts how many parts.

</Definition>

<Definition term="equivalent fractions">

Two fractions are **equivalent** when they mark the same point on the number line, that is, when they are the same number. There is a quick test: $\tfrac{a}{b}$ and $\tfrac{c}{d}$ are equivalent exactly when $a \times d = c \times b$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="decimal">

Decimals continue place value to the right of the ones. The first place after the decimal point counts **tenths**, the next **hundredths**, the next **thousandths**. So $0.37$ means $3$ tenths and $7$ hundredths, which is $\tfrac{37}{100}$.

</Definition>

The denominator can never be $0$: nobody can cut a whole into $0$ equal parts.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Families of equivalent fractions">

Cut every half into $2$ pieces and you get quarters: $\tfrac12 = \tfrac24$. Cut every half into $3$ pieces and you get sixths: $\tfrac12 = \tfrac36$. So

$$
\tfrac12 = \tfrac24 = \tfrac36 = \tfrac48 = \tfrac{50}{100} = \cdots
$$

Going the other way, $\tfrac68 = \tfrac34$: both $6$ and $8$ are $2$ times something, so we can join pairs of eighths into quarters. A fraction is in **simplest form** (or **lowest terms**) when no whole number bigger than $1$ divides both the top and the bottom.

</Example>

<Example title="Comparing">

Which is bigger, $\tfrac34$ or $\tfrac23$? Their pieces are different sizes, so rewrite both with **twelfths**: $\tfrac34 = \tfrac{9}{12}$ and $\tfrac23 = \tfrac{8}{12}$. Now the pieces are the same size, and $9$ pieces are more than $8$. So $\tfrac34 > \tfrac23$.

With the **same numerator**, the bigger denominator means *smaller* pieces: $\tfrac15 > \tfrac18$, because fifths are bigger than eighths.

</Example>

<Example title="Adding and subtracting">

- $\tfrac12 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac36 + \tfrac26 = \tfrac56$.
- $\tfrac34 - \tfrac16 = \tfrac{9}{12} - \tfrac{2}{12} = \tfrac{7}{12}$.
- $\tfrac25 + \tfrac45 = \tfrac65 = 1\tfrac15$. Same denominator, so just add the numerators.

</Example>

<Example title="Decimals and fractions">

- $0.7 = \tfrac{7}{10}$, $\quad 0.07 = \tfrac{7}{100}$, $\quad 0.007 = \tfrac{7}{1000}$.
- $0.125 = \tfrac{125}{1000} = \tfrac18$, because $8 \times 125 = 1000$.
- $\tfrac{3}{20} = \tfrac{15}{100} = 0.15$: multiply top and bottom by $5$ to make hundredths.
- Which is bigger, $0.3$ or $0.25$? Write $0.3$ as $0.30$: that is $30$ hundredths, and $0.25$ is $25$ hundredths. So $0.3 > 0.25$.
- In Canada and the United States, a $25$-cent coin is called a **quarter**. A dollar is $100$ cents, so the coin is $\tfrac{25}{100} = \tfrac14$ of a dollar, which is $0.25$ dollars.

</Example>

<Example title="A whole number times a fraction">

$4 \times \tfrac23$ means $4$ groups of $2$ thirds, which is $8$ thirds: $\tfrac83 = 2\tfrac23$. In general, $n \times \tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{n \times a}{b}$. The size of the pieces stays the same, and there are $n$ times as many of them.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="why" kind="proof" title="Why the rules work">

### Why $\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{n \times a}{n \times b}$

Start with $\tfrac{a}{b}$: the whole is cut into $b$ equal parts, and we take $a$ of them. Now cut **every** part into $n$ smaller equal pieces. The whole now has $n \times b$ pieces. Each old part became $n$ new pieces, so our $a$ old parts are $n \times a$ new pieces. We cut, but we did not move anything, so the length is the same:

$$
\frac{a}{b} = \frac{n \times a}{n \times b} \qquad \text{for every whole number } n > 0.
$$

### Why the cross-multiplication test works

To compare $\tfrac{a}{b}$ and $\tfrac{c}{d}$, rewrite both with the same pieces, using the rule above. Multiply the first by $d$ on top and bottom, and the second by $b$:

$$
\frac{a}{b} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times d}, \qquad \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c \times b}{d \times b}.
$$

Now both fractions count pieces of the **same size**, $\tfrac{1}{b \times d}$. Two counts of same-sized pieces give the same length exactly when the counts are equal. So $\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{c}{d}$ exactly when $a \times d = c \times b$. In the same way, $\tfrac{a}{b} < \tfrac{c}{d}$ exactly when $a \times d < c \times b$.

<Example title="Using the test">

Is $\tfrac{6}{9} = \tfrac{10}{15}$? Check: $6 \times 15 = 90$ and $10 \times 9 = 90$. Yes! Is $\tfrac23 = \tfrac34$? $2 \times 4 = 8$ but $3 \times 3 = 9$. No: since $8 < 9$, $\tfrac23 < \tfrac34$.

</Example>

### Why we add with a common denominator

We can only add **counts of the same thing**: $3$ sixths and $2$ sixths are $5$ sixths, just as $3$ apples and $2$ apples are $5$ apples. Halves and thirds are different-sized pieces, so we first re-cut both into sixths. In general,

$$
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times d} + \frac{c \times b}{b \times d} = \frac{a \times d + c \times b}{b \times d}.
$$

<Theorem name="the tempting mistake">

$\tfrac12 + \tfrac13$ is **not** $\tfrac25$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Adding $\tfrac13$ to $\tfrac12$ must give something **bigger** than $\tfrac12$. But $\tfrac25$ is **smaller** than $\tfrac12$, because $2 \times 2 = 4$ is less than $1 \times 5 = 5$. So $\tfrac25$ cannot be the answer. (The correct answer is $\tfrac56$.)

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

I love this proof: we didn't even need to know the right answer to see that $\tfrac25$ is wrong. A sum of two positive numbers is bigger than each of them, and $\tfrac25$ fails that test.

</Narrator>

### Why $0.3$ is bigger than $0.25$

The digits of a decimal are counts of tenths, hundredths and thousandths. To compare, use the same place values: $0.3 = 0.30 = \tfrac{30}{100}$ and $0.25 = \tfrac{25}{100}$. Thirty hundredths are more than twenty-five hundredths. Comparing "$25$ with $3$" compares hundredths with tenths, which are different-sized pieces.

### Which fractions have a decimal that stops?

A decimal with $3$ places is a number of thousandths, like $0.375 = \tfrac{375}{1000}$. So a fraction has a decimal that **stops** exactly when it can be rewritten with denominator $10$, $100$, $1000$ or another **power of ten**.

- $\tfrac38$ can: $8 \times 125 = 1000$, so $\tfrac38 = \tfrac{375}{1000} = 0.375$.
- $\tfrac{7}{20}$ can: $20 \times 5 = 100$, so $\tfrac{7}{20} = \tfrac{35}{100} = 0.35$.
- $\tfrac13$ cannot!

<Theorem name="one third never stops">

There is no power of ten $10, 100, 1000, \dots$ that $\tfrac13$ can be rewritten over. So the decimal of $\tfrac13$ goes on forever: $0.333\ldots$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose $\tfrac13 = \tfrac{m}{1000}$, or over some other power of ten. By the cross-multiplication test, $1000 = 3 \times m$, so $1000$ would be a multiple of $3$. But $1000 = 999 + 1$, and $999 = 3 \times 333$. So $1000$ is one more than a multiple of $3$, and it is not a multiple of $3$ itself. The same is true of every power of ten: $10 = 9 + 1$, $100 = 99 + 1$, $10000 = 9999 + 1$, and so on.

</Proof>

<ForGrownUps title="the general rule">

A fraction in lowest terms has a decimal that stops exactly when its denominator has no **prime factors** other than $2$ and $5$ (see <TopicLink id="math.number.factors-primes">factors and primes</TopicLink>). One direction is easy: if the denominator is made of $2$s and $5$s, multiply top and bottom by enough $2$s and $5$s to pair them all up into $10$s. The other direction needs the fact that prime factorisations are unique, since the only prime factors of $10^k$ are $2$ and $5$. This is proved in <TopicLink id="math.number.divisibility-gcd">divisibility and unique factorisation</TopicLink>. Lowest terms matter: $\tfrac{3}{12}$ has a $3$ in its denominator, yet $\tfrac{3}{12} = \tfrac14 = 0.25$ stops.

</ForGrownUps>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"Add the tops and add the bottoms": $\tfrac12 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac25$.** The answer $\tfrac25$ is smaller than $\tfrac12$, so it cannot be a sum that includes $\tfrac12$. Re-cut into same-sized pieces first: $\tfrac36 + \tfrac26 = \tfrac56$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A bigger denominator means a bigger fraction."** The denominator tells you how many pieces the whole is cut into. More pieces means *smaller* pieces, so $\tfrac18 < \tfrac15$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$0.25 > 0.3$, because $25 > 3$."** The $25$ counts hundredths and the $3$ counts tenths. Write $0.3$ as $0.30$ to compare hundredths with hundredths: $30 > 25$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Fractions are only parts of shapes."** Shapes are a good start, but a fraction is a **number**. It has a place on the number line, it can be bigger than $1$ ($\tfrac54$), and it can be the answer to a division ($3 \div 4 = \tfrac34$).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Adding the same number to the top and bottom gives an equivalent fraction."** $\tfrac12$ is not $\tfrac{1 + 1}{2 + 1} = \tfrac23$. Equivalent fractions come from **multiplying** top and bottom by the same number.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Fractions are points and lengths">

A geometer sees $\tfrac{a}{b}$ as a point on a line, found by cutting the unit length into $b$ equal parts. Ancient Greek geometers knew how to do that cutting with only a straightedge and compass, using parallel lines (Euclid, *Elements*, Book VI). In this picture, equivalent fractions are one point with many names. Comparing fractions means asking which point is further to the right.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Lowest terms, and the primes 2 and 5">

A number theorist notices that simplest form is about common factors. $\tfrac{a}{b}$ is in lowest terms when the greatest common factor of $a$ and $b$ is $1$. And whether a decimal stops depends only on the primes in the denominator, because $10 = 2 \times 5$. In base twelve, which uses $12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3$, the fraction $\tfrac13$ *would* stop. See <TopicLink id="math.number.factors-primes">factors and primes</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Division always works now">

With whole numbers alone, $3 \div 4$ has no exact answer. With fractions, every division by a number other than $0$ has one: $a \div b = \tfrac{a}{b}$. Every fraction except $0$ also has a partner that multiplies with it to make $1$: $\tfrac34 \times \tfrac43 = 1$. Later, with negative numbers included, the fractions form the <TopicLink id="math.number.rational-numbers">rational numbers</TopicLink>. There, all four operations work (except division by zero), and algebraists call a system like that a **field**. Some numbers, such as $\sqrt2$, are not fractions at all (<TopicLink id="math.number.real-numbers">real numbers</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="A fraction is a whole family">

Think of all the names for one half: $\tfrac12, \tfrac24, \tfrac36, \tfrac48, \dots$ They form a set, a **family** of equivalent fractions. Every fraction belongs to exactly one family, and the families never overlap. Mathematicians say that "is equivalent to" is an **equivalence relation**. It is reflexive (each fraction is equivalent to itself), symmetric, and transitive (Lean proved `equiv_trans` above). Its families are called **equivalence classes** (<TopicLink id="math.found.equivalence-relations">relations and partitions</TopicLink>).

One way to say what the number "one half" *is*: it is the whole family. The Lean theorem `add_respects_equiv` says that adding works family by family. It does not matter which member of a family you pick.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Egypt.** Scribes wrote almost every fraction as a sum of different unit fractions. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (around 1550 BCE) begins with a table of such sums, for example $\tfrac25 = \tfrac13 + \tfrac{1}{15}$.
- **Mesopotamia.** Babylonian scribes wrote fractions in base $60$, about four thousand years ago. We still split an hour into $60$ minutes and a minute into $60$ seconds.
- **China.** *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, compiled about two thousand years ago, gives rules for adding, subtracting and comparing fractions with a common denominator. It also reduces fractions to lowest terms by repeatedly subtracting the smaller number from the larger.
- **India.** Indian mathematicians wrote a fraction as the numerator above the denominator, without a line between them. The horizontal fraction bar later appears in Arabic writing, for example in the 12th-century work of al-Ḥaṣṣār in the Maghreb.
- **Decimal fractions.** Abu'l-Ḥasan al-Uqlīdisī, writing in Damascus around 952 CE, already used decimal fractions. Jamshīd al-Kāshī in Samarkand explained them fully in his *Key to Arithmetic* (1427). In Flanders, Simon Stevin's booklet *De Thiende* (1585) spread decimals across Europe.

</Section>
