<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Two routes to the same price">

Wanjiru sells hand-woven baskets in Nairobi. A basket costs $1000$ Kenyan shillings before tax, and Kenya adds $16\%$ value-added tax (VAT). A visitor wants to know the price in US dollars. To keep the numbers simple, suppose the exchange rate is $125$ shillings to the dollar (real rates change every day).

There are two routes from “price before tax, in shillings” to “price with tax, in dollars”:

- **add VAT, then convert:** $1000 \times 1.16 = 1160$ shillings, and $1160 \div 125 = 9.28$ dollars;
- **convert, then add VAT:** $1000 \div 125 = 8$ dollars, and $8 \times 1.16 = 9.28$ dollars.

Same answer! Now Wanjiru adds a delivery fee of $200$ shillings. “Add the fee, then convert” gives $1200 \div 125 = 9.60$ dollars. But “convert, then add $200$” gives $8 + 200 = 208$ dollars — absurd. The fee has to be converted too: on the dollar route we must add $200 \div 125 = 1.60$ dollars.

Pictures of routes like these, and the question “do the routes always agree?”, are the subject of this lesson. They are called **commutative diagrams**, and they are one of the most widely used pictures in modern mathematics.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="diagram of functions">

A **diagram of functions** shows sets at its corners and functions as arrows between them. A **route** is a sequence of arrows, each starting where the previous one ends; following a route means applying its functions one after another (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.chaining-functions">function machines in sequence</TopicLink>).

</Definition>

<Definition term="commuting square">

A square of functions

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
A & \xrightarrow{\ \ f\ \ } & B \\
{\scriptstyle h}\downarrow & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle g} \\
C & \xrightarrow{\ \ k\ \ } & D
\end{array}
$$

**commutes** if the two routes from $A$ to $D$ give the same output for every input:

$$
g\big(f(a)\big) = k\big(h(a)\big) \quad \text{for every } a \in A.
$$

</Definition>

<Definition term="commuting triangle">

A triangle with $f : A \to B$, $g : B \to C$ and $h : A \to C$ **commutes** if the two-step route equals the direct one: $g(f(a)) = h(a)$ for every $a \in A$. More generally, a diagram **commutes** when any two routes with the same start and the same end give the same function.

</Definition>

<Note>

“For every input” is essential. To show that a square commutes we need an argument that covers all inputs. To show that it does **not** commute, one input where the routes differ is enough.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="preserving" kind="human" title="Preserving an operation">

A carpenter in Kumasi measures two planks: $1.2$ m and $0.85$ m. Laid end to end they make $2.05$ m, which is $205$ cm. Measured in centimetres they are $120$ cm and $85$ cm, which make $205$ cm. Converting and adding can be done in either order:

$$
100 \cdot (a + b) = 100a + 100b.
$$

This is a commuting square too — but its left-hand corners hold **pairs** of lengths $(a, b)$:

<CommutativeDiagram
  objects={[
    { id: "A", tex: "\\text{pairs of lengths in m}", row: 0, col: 0 },
    { id: "B", tex: "\\text{lengths in m}", row: 0, col: 1.3 },
    { id: "C", tex: "\\text{pairs of lengths in cm}", row: 1, col: 0 },
    { id: "D", tex: "\\text{lengths in cm}", row: 1, col: 1.3 },
  ]}
  arrows={[
    { from: "A", to: "B", tex: "\\text{add}" },
    { from: "A", to: "C", tex: "\\times 100 \\text{ each}", labelSide: "right" },
    { from: "B", to: "D", tex: "\\times 100" },
    { from: "C", to: "D", tex: "\\text{add}", labelSide: "right" },
  ]}
  caption="Add then convert, or convert both then add: a function preserves addition when this square commutes."
/>

<Definition term="preserving addition">

A function $f$ **preserves addition** if $f(a + b) = f(a) + f(b)$ for all inputs $a$ and $b$ — that is, if “add, then apply $f$” equals “apply $f$ to both, then add”.

</Definition>

Some functions preserve addition and many do not:

- $x \mapsto 100x$ (metres to centimetres) preserves addition: that is the distributive law.
- $x \mapsto x + 1$ does **not**: with $a = b = 0$, “add then apply” gives $1$ but “apply then add” gives $1 + 1 = 2$.
- $x \mapsto x^2$ does not: $(1 + 1)^2 = 4$ but $1^2 + 1^2 = 2$.
- $x \mapsto 100x$ preserves addition but **not multiplication**: $100 \cdot (1 \cdot 1) = 100$, while $(100 \cdot 1) \cdot (100 \cdot 1) = 10\,000$. That is why $1\ \mathrm{m}^2 = 10\,000\ \mathrm{cm}^2$, not $100\ \mathrm{cm}^2$: an area is a product of two lengths.
- Celsius to Fahrenheit, $C \mapsto 1.8\,C + 32$, does not preserve addition: $10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ give $50$ and $68$, which add to $118$, but $30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is $86\,^\circ\mathrm{F}$. (The *differences* of temperatures do convert by $\times 1.8$ alone.)
- “Take the last digit” preserves addition **if the bottom route adds digits with wrap-around** (keeping only the last digit of the sum): the last digit of $47 + 38 = 85$ is $5$, and $7 + 8 = 15$ also ends in $5$.
- Squares can even change the operation. $2^{a + b} = 2^a \cdot 2^b$: “add, then raise $2$ to that power” equals “raise $2$ to each power, then **multiply**”.

</Section>

<Section id="proof" kind="proof" title="Proofs">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (metres to centimetres preserves addition)">

For all numbers $a$ and $b$: $\;100(a + b) = 100a + 100b$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

This is the distributive law $c(a + b) = ca + cb$ with $c = 100$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (which straight-line rules preserve addition?)">

Let $m$ and $c$ be integers. The function $x \mapsto mx + c$ on $\Z$ preserves addition exactly when $c = 0$. In particular, $x \mapsto x + 1$ does not preserve addition.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The two routes give $m(a + b) + c = ma + mb + c$ and $(ma + c) + (mb + c) = ma + mb + 2c$.

If $c = 0$, these are equal for all $a, b$.

If the function preserves addition, the routes agree in particular for $a = b = 0$, which gives $c = 2c$, so $c = 0$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (pasting squares)">

Suppose that in the diagram

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
A & \xrightarrow{\ \ f\ \ } & B & \xrightarrow{\ \ g\ \ } & C \\
{\scriptstyle p}\downarrow & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle q} & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle r} \\
D & \xrightarrow{\ \ f'\ \ } & E & \xrightarrow{\ \ g'\ \ } & F
\end{array}
$$

the left square commutes, $q(f(x)) = f'(p(x))$ for all $x \in A$, and the right square commutes, $r(g(y)) = g'(q(y))$ for all $y \in B$. Then the outer rectangle commutes: $r(g(f(x))) = g'(f'(p(x)))$ for all $x \in A$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take any $x \in A$. The right square, applied to the input $y = f(x) \in B$, gives $r(g(f(x))) = g'(q(f(x)))$. The left square gives $q(f(x)) = f'(p(x))$, so

$$
r\big(g(f(x))\big) = g'\big(q(f(x))\big) = g'\big(f'(p(x))\big).
$$

</Proof>

In words: we slide the route across the rectangle one square at a time, and each slide is allowed because that square commutes.

<Note>

**The converse is false.** The outer rectangle can commute even if the left square does not. Use integers at every corner, let $f$, $g$, $f'$ and $p$ be “do nothing”, let $q$ be “add $1$”, and let $r$ and $g'$ send every number to $0$. Both outer routes send everything to $0$, so the rectangle commutes, but the left square would need $x + 1 = x$.

</Note>

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (addition-preserving functions are multiplications)">

If $f : \Z \to \Z$ preserves addition, then $f(x) = f(1) \cdot x$ for every integer $x$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

First, $f(0) = f(0 + 0) = f(0) + f(0)$, so $f(0) = 0$.

Next, $f(n + 1) = f(n) + f(1)$ for every $n$. Starting from $f(0) = 0$ and adding $f(1)$ at each step, $f(1) = 1 \cdot f(1)$, $f(2) = 2 \cdot f(1)$, $f(3) = 3 \cdot f(1)$, and so on: $f(n) = n \cdot f(1)$ for every natural number $n$. (Strictly, this “and so on” is a proof by **induction**.)

Finally, $f(x) + f(-x) = f(x + (-x)) = f(0) = 0$, so $f(-x) = -f(x)$. For a negative integer $-n$ this gives $f(-n) = -n \cdot f(1)$.

</Proof>

So among all functions from $\Z$ to $\Z$, the ones that preserve addition are exactly the multiplications $x \mapsto mx$. A single value, $f(1)$, determines everything.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**“The routes agree for one input, so the diagram commutes.”** The routes $x \mapsto (x + 1)^2$ and $x \mapsto x^2 + 1$ agree at $x = 0$, but at $x = 1$ they give $4$ and $2$. A diagram commutes only if the routes agree for **every** input, which needs an argument (or, on a finite set, a check of every case).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“If all the arrows can be drawn, the diagram commutes.”** Drawing the arrows only says that the routes **exist**. Whether they **agree** is a separate question: the square with “add $1$, then double” and “double, then add $1$” is perfectly drawable and never commutes.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“A commutative diagram means the functions commute with each other.”** The word is borrowed from “commutative” as in $ab = ba$, but a commuting square says that two routes agree, $g(f(a)) = k(h(a))$, and its four functions can all be different. Only the special square with $f, g$ on one route and $g, f$ on the other says that $f$ and $g$ can be swapped.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**“A unit conversion preserves everything.”** Converting metres to centimetres preserves addition but not multiplication: $1\ \mathrm{m}^2$ is $10\,000\ \mathrm{cm}^2$. And Celsius to Fahrenheit does not even preserve addition, because of the $+32$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Maps that respect structure">

Functions that preserve an operation are called **homomorphisms** (“same shape”), and they are how algebraists compare one number system with another (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.structure-preserving-maps">structure-preserving maps</TopicLink>). Some of them change the operation: $2^{a + b} = 2^a \cdot 2^b$ turns addition into multiplication, and logarithms go back, $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$. John Napier's tables of logarithms (1614) and the slide rules invented in the 1620s used this square for three centuries to turn hard multiplications into easy additions. Theorem 4 is a first classification theorem: the homomorphisms from $(\Z, +)$ to itself are exactly the multiplications.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Casting out nines">

A number and the sum of its digits leave the same remainder on division by $9$ (because $10, 100, 1000, \dots$ all leave remainder $1$). And “take the remainder on division by $9$” preserves multiplication, if we multiply remainders and reduce again. So the square “multiply, then reduce” = “reduce both, then multiply and reduce” gives a quick check of arithmetic. For $47 \times 23 = 1081$: the digit sums give $4 + 7 = 11 \to 2$ and $2 + 3 = 5$, and $2 \times 5 = 10 \to 1$; also $1 + 0 + 8 + 1 = 10 \to 1$. They match. A claimed answer of $1071$ (digit sum $9 \to 0$) would be caught. But the check cannot catch every error — $1801$ passes too. The method was known to medieval arithmeticians writing in Arabic and is explained in Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* (1202); see also <TopicLink id="math.number.modular-arithmetic">clock arithmetic</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.applied.check-digits">check digits</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Units and scales">

Physical laws must not depend on the units we measure in, which is a commuting-square requirement: compute in metres and convert, or convert and compute in feet — the answers must match. Conversions between length units are multiplications, so they preserve addition of lengths. Temperature scales are different: Celsius and Fahrenheit have different zero points, so the conversion $C \mapsto 1.8\,C + 32$ preserves neither addition nor doubling. That is why “twice as hot” makes no sense in °C or °F, and why physicists use the kelvin scale, whose zero is absolute zero, when they need to multiply or divide temperatures.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Maps of maps">

A map of a city at scale $1 : 50\,000$ shrinks every distance by the same factor, so it preserves adding distances along a route: walk $2$ cm and then $3$ cm on the map, and you have walked $5 \times 50\,000$ cm $= 2.5$ km in the city. Scaling preserves angles as well, so triangles on the map are **similar** to triangles in the city (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">rigid motions, congruence and similarity</TopicLink>). But it does not preserve areas: a park of $1$ cm² on the map is $0.25$ km² in real life, because both of its dimensions are scaled.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="The language of category theory">

Commutative diagrams are the everyday language of category theory, where they state equations between composites of arrows (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.categories">categories</TopicLink>). A famous example is the **naturality square**. Suppose a translation $\eta$ turns objects of one kind into objects of another, and $f$ is a change on the first side with a matching change $f'$ on the other side. Then $\eta$ is “natural” if translating and changing can be done in either order:

Our VAT square is an example in spirit: converting currency is natural with respect to adding a percentage, but not with respect to adding a fixed fee unless the fee is converted too. And the pasting theorem is used constantly: big diagrams are proved to commute by breaking them into small squares and triangles, each of which commutes.

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- The idea that a calculation can be checked by doing it “downstairs”, with remainders, is old. Casting out nines was used by medieval arithmeticians writing in Arabic and explained by Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci) in *Liber Abaci* (1202).
- **John Napier** published his tables of logarithms in 1614, and the slide rule followed in the 1620s, with **William Oughtred** among its inventors. Both rest on a square that turns multiplication into addition.
- Diagrams of arrows that commute became a standard tool in algebraic topology in the 1940s and 1950s. **Samuel Eilenberg** and **Saunders Mac Lane** introduced categories in 1945, and Eilenberg and **Norman Steenrod**'s book *Foundations of Algebraic Topology* (1952) used commutative diagrams throughout, helping to make them part of every mathematician's toolkit.

</Section>
