<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Questions about whole numbers">

On Christmas Day 1640, Pierre de Fermat wrote to Marin Mersenne that every prime of the form $4k + 1$ is a sum of two squares — $5 = 1 + 4$, $13 = 4 + 9$, $29 = 4 + 25$ — while no prime of the form $4k + 3$ is. He gave no proof, and Leonhard Euler supplied one a century later. Why should the remainder on division by $4$ decide such a thing?

Some questions look even more innocent. Is $37$ a perfect square modulo $101$, that is, does $101$ divide $x^2 - 37$ for some $x$? One can try all $101$ residues. Euler, Adrien-Marie Legendre and finally Carl Friedrich Gauss found something much better, a hidden symmetry between primes: whether $p$ is a square modulo $q$ is tied to whether $q$ is a square modulo $p$. Gauss called this *theorema aureum*, the golden theorem, and gave eight proofs.

The oldest question here is Pell's equation $x^2 - dy^2 = 1$. Brahmagupta studied it in 628, and Bhāskara II solved $x^2 - 61y^2 = 1$ in the twelfth century with his *chakravāla* method. The smallest solution is

$$
x = 1\,766\,319\,049, \qquad y = 226\,153\,980 .
$$

In 1657 Fermat set the same equation as a challenge to European mathematicians, apparently unaware it had been solved in India five hundred years earlier.

This lesson proves the central results of classical number theory beyond <TopicLink id="math.number.congruences">congruences</TopicLink>. The tools are the group theory of <TopicLink id="math.algebra.groups">groups</TopicLink> (the unit group modulo $p$ is cyclic), a clever count of lattice points (for reciprocity), the Gaussian integers of <TopicLink id="math.algebra.rings">rings</TopicLink> (for sums of two squares), and approximation of irrationals by fractions (for Pell).

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Powers going round the clock">

Take the powers of $3$ modulo $7$: $3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1$. They visit every non-zero residue before returning to $1$. So every non-zero residue is a power of $3$, and $3$ is a **primitive root** modulo $7$. The powers of $2$ only reach $2, 4, 1$. Modulo $8$ nothing works: $3^2 = 9 \equiv 1$, $5^2 \equiv 1$, $7^2 \equiv 1$, so every unit has order at most $2$.

When a primitive root $g$ exists, the multiplicative structure becomes additive. Every unit is $g^k$, multiplying units adds exponents, and $a = g^k$ is a perfect square exactly when $k$ is even. So modulo an odd prime exactly half of the non-zero residues are squares. Modulo $7$ they are $1, 2, 4$, the even powers of $3$. A whole theory of quadratic residues grows from this observation, and its crowning result relates the squares modulo $p$ to those modulo $q$.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Primitive roots, residues and arithmetic functions">

Throughout, $p$ and $q$ denote primes, $\varphi$ is Euler's totient, and $(\Z/n\Z)^\times$ is the group of units modulo $n$, of order $\varphi(n)$. For $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, $\operatorname{ord}_n(a)$ is the order of $a$ in this group, the least $k \ge 1$ with $a^k \equiv 1 \pmod n$.

<Definition term="primitive root">

A **primitive root** modulo $n$ is an integer $g$ with $\operatorname{ord}_n(g) = \varphi(n)$, that is, a generator of $(\Z/n\Z)^\times$. A primitive root exists iff $(\Z/n\Z)^\times$ is cyclic.

</Definition>

<Definition term="quadratic residue and Legendre symbol">

For an odd prime $p$ and $p \nmid a$, $a$ is a **quadratic residue** modulo $p$ if $x^2 \equiv a \pmod p$ has a solution, and a **non-residue** otherwise. The **Legendre symbol** is

$$
\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) = \begin{cases} 0 & p \mid a, \\ 1 & a \text{ a quadratic residue mod } p, \\ -1 & a \text{ a non-residue mod } p. \end{cases}
$$

</Definition>

<Definition term="arithmetic functions">

An **arithmetic function** is a function $f : \Z_{\gt 0} \to \C$. It is **multiplicative** if $f(1) = 1$ and $f(mn) = f(m)f(n)$ whenever $\gcd(m, n) = 1$. The **Dirichlet convolution** is $(f * g)(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d)\,g(n/d)$. Important examples are $\mathbf{1}(n) = 1$, $\operatorname{id}(n) = n$, $\varepsilon(n) = [n = 1]$, the divisor functions $\tau = \mathbf 1 * \mathbf 1$ and $\sigma = \operatorname{id} * \mathbf 1$, Euler's $\varphi$, and the **Möbius function** $\mu$: $\mu(n) = (-1)^r$ if $n$ is a product of $r$ distinct primes, and $\mu(n) = 0$ if $n$ has a square factor.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Pell's equation">

For a positive integer $d$ that is not a square, **Pell's equation** is $x^2 - dy^2 = 1$ in integers. Its solutions with $y \ne 0$ are **non-trivial**. Identify $(x, y)$ with the real number $x + y\sqrt d$. The **norm** $N(x + y\sqrt d) = (x + y\sqrt d)(x - y\sqrt d) = x^2 - dy^2$ is multiplicative.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Primitive roots and indices">

Modulo $11$, $\operatorname{ord}(2) = 10$, since $2^5 = 32 \equiv -1 \ne 1$ and $2^2 \ne 1$. So $2$ is a primitive root, and its powers $2, 4, 8, 5, 10, 9, 7, 3, 6, 1$ give every unit an **index** (discrete logarithm). For instance $\operatorname{ind}(7) = 7$. To solve $x^3 \equiv 7 \pmod{11}$, write $x = 2^k$: then $3k \equiv 7 \pmod{10}$, so $k \equiv 9$ and $x = 2^9 \equiv 6$. Check: $6^3 = 216 = 19 \cdot 11 + 7$.

</Example>

<Example title="Squares modulo 13">

The squares of $1, \dots, 6$ modulo $13$ are $1, 4, 9, 3, 12, 10$. These are the six quadratic residues, and $x$ and $-x$ have the same square, so the list is complete. The non-residues are $2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11$. Note that $-1 \equiv 12$ is a residue ($5^2 = 25 \equiv -1$), as it must be for $13 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

</Example>

<Example title="Arithmetic functions">

$\varphi(12) = 4$, $\tau(12) = 6$, $\sigma(12) = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 12 = 28$, and $\mu(12) = 0$, $\mu(30) = -1$, $\mu(15) = 1$. Since these functions are multiplicative, it suffices to know them on prime powers: $\varphi(p^k) = p^k - p^{k-1}$, $\tau(p^k) = k + 1$, $\sigma(p^k) = \frac{p^{k+1} - 1}{p - 1}$. For example $\sigma(12) = \sigma(4)\sigma(3) = 7 \cdot 4 = 28$.

</Example>

<Example title="A Pell equation solved by hand">

For $d = 2$: $3^2 - 2 \cdot 2^2 = 1$, so $3 + 2\sqrt2$ is a solution. Its powers $(3 + 2\sqrt2)^2 = 17 + 12\sqrt2$, $(3 + 2\sqrt2)^3 = 99 + 70\sqrt2$, … are solutions too, because the norm is multiplicative. The fractions $\frac32, \frac{17}{12}, \frac{99}{70}, \frac{577}{408}$ approximate $\sqrt2$ extremely well. Pell's equation is about the best rational approximations to $\sqrt d$.

</Example>

<Section id="primitive-roots" kind="proof" title="Primitive roots">

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (the units modulo a prime form a cyclic group)">

For every prime $p$, $(\Z/p\Z)^\times$ is cyclic of order $p - 1$. More precisely, for each $d \mid p - 1$ there are exactly $\varphi(d)$ elements of order $d$; in particular there are $\varphi(p - 1)$ primitive roots.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $\psi(d)$ be the number of elements of order $d$. By Lagrange's theorem every order divides $p - 1$, so $\sum_{d \mid p-1}\psi(d) = p - 1$. Suppose $\psi(d) \ge 1$, and let $a$ have order $d$. Then $1, a, \dots, a^{d-1}$ are $d$ distinct roots of $x^d - 1$. Over the field $\Z/p\Z$ a polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots (factor theorem plus no zero divisors), so these are *all* the roots. Every element of order $d$ is therefore a power $a^k$, and $a^k$ has order $d$ iff $\gcd(k, d) = 1$. Hence $\psi(d) = \varphi(d)$ whenever $\psi(d) \ne 0$, so $\psi(d) \le \varphi(d)$ for all $d$. But $\sum_{d \mid p-1}\varphi(d) = p - 1$ (groups lesson, Proposition 4), so the inequalities are equalities. In particular $\psi(p - 1) = \varphi(p - 1) \ge 1$.

</Proof>

The same proof shows that every finite subgroup of the multiplicative group of any field is cyclic. The proof does not *construct* a primitive root, and no efficient deterministic method is known for finding one. In practice one tests $g = 2, 3, 5, \dots$ using the criterion: $g$ is a primitive root modulo $p$ iff $g^{(p-1)/q} \not\equiv 1$ for every prime $q \mid p - 1$.

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (no primitive roots modulo 2ᵏ for k ≥ 3)">

If $k \ge 3$ and $a$ is odd, then $a^{2^{k-2}} \equiv 1 \pmod{2^k}$. Since $\varphi(2^k) = 2^{k-1}$, there is no primitive root modulo $2^k$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For $k = 3$: $a = 2m + 1$ gives $a^2 = 4m(m + 1) + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, as $m(m + 1)$ is even. If $a^{2^{k-2}} = 1 + 2^k t$, squaring gives $a^{2^{k-1}} = 1 + 2^{k+1}t + 2^{2k}t^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{k+1}}$. Induct on $k$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (which moduli have primitive roots)">

A primitive root modulo $n$ exists if and only if $n = 1, 2, 4, p^k$ or $2p^k$, where $p$ is an odd prime and $k \ge 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*None otherwise.* If $n = 2^k$ with $k \ge 3$, use Theorem 2. Otherwise $n = m m'$ with $\gcd(m, m') = 1$ and $m, m' \gt 2$, so $\varphi(m)$ and $\varphi(m')$ are both even. By the Chinese remainder theorem (<TopicLink id="math.algebra.quotients">quotients</TopicLink>), $(\Z/n)^\times \cong (\Z/m)^\times \times (\Z/m')^\times$. Every element then has order dividing $\operatorname{lcm}(\varphi(m), \varphi(m')) \le \varphi(m)\varphi(m')/2 \lt \varphi(n)$.

*Odd prime powers.* Let $g$ be a primitive root modulo $p$. If $g^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$, replace $g$ by $g + p$: by the binomial theorem $(g + p)^{p-1} \equiv g^{p-1} + (p-1)g^{p-2}p \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$. So we may assume $g^{p-1} = 1 + pt$ with $p \nmid t$. By induction, using the binomial theorem and $p$ odd, $(1 + pt)^{p^{j}} = 1 + p^{j+1}t_j$ with $p \nmid t_j$. The order of $g$ modulo $p^k$ divides $\varphi(p^k) = (p - 1)p^{k-1}$ and is a multiple of $p - 1$ (reduce modulo $p$), so it is $(p-1)p^j$ for some $j$. It is not $(p-1)p^{j}$ for $j \lt k - 1$, since $g^{(p-1)p^{k-2}} = 1 + p^{k-1}t_{k-2} \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p^k}$. So $g$ is a primitive root modulo every $p^k$.

*$2p^k$, $2$ and $4$.* $(\Z/2p^k)^\times \cong (\Z/p^k)^\times$ (the factor $(\Z/2)^\times$ is trivial), and an odd primitive root modulo $p^k$ is a primitive root modulo $2p^k$. Modulo $2$ and $4$, $1$ and $3$ are primitive roots.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="reciprocity" kind="proof" title="Quadratic residues and reciprocity">

Fix an odd prime $p$.

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (Euler's criterion)">

For every integer $a$, $\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) \equiv a^{(p-1)/2} \pmod p$. Consequently there are exactly $\frac{p-1}{2}$ quadratic residues and $\frac{p-1}{2}$ non-residues, and the Legendre symbol is completely multiplicative: $\left(\frac{ab}{p}\right) = \left(\frac ap\right)\left(\frac bp\right)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $p \mid a$ both sides are $0$. Otherwise let $g$ be a primitive root (Theorem 1) and $a \equiv g^k$. Then $a$ is a square iff $k$ is even: $g^k = (g^j)^2$ means $k \equiv 2j \pmod{p - 1}$, and $p - 1$ is even. On the other hand $g^{(p-1)/2} \equiv -1$, because it is a root of $x^2 - 1$ other than $1$ (as $g$ has order $p - 1$). So $a^{(p-1)/2} = (g^{(p-1)/2})^k \equiv (-1)^k$. This is $1$ for even $k$ and $-1$ for odd $k$. Exactly half of $k = 0, \dots, p-2$ are even. Multiplicativity follows since $(ab)^{(p-1)/2} = a^{(p-1)/2}b^{(p-1)/2}$, and the values $\pm 1$ are distinct modulo $p \gt 2$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Corollary 5 (first supplementary law)">

$\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right) = (-1)^{(p-1)/2}$: $-1$ is a square modulo $p$ iff $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

</Theorem>

<Theorem name="Lemma 6 (Gauss's lemma)">

Let $p \nmid a$, and let $\mu$ be the number of $k \in \{1, \dots, \frac{p-1}{2}\}$ for which the least positive residue of $ka$ exceeds $\frac p2$. Then $\left(\frac ap\right) = (-1)^\mu$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $h = \frac{p-1}{2}$. Write the residues of $a, 2a, \dots, ha$ as $s_1, \dots, s_\lambda$ (those below $\frac p2$) and $t_1, \dots, t_\mu$ (those above). The $h$ numbers $s_1, \dots, s_\lambda, p - t_1, \dots, p - t_\mu$ all lie in $\{1, \dots, h\}$, and they are distinct. The $s$'s are distinct, and so are the $t$'s. If $s_i = p - t_j$, then $ka + la \equiv 0$ for some $1 \le k, l \le h$, so $p \mid k + l$, impossible as $2 \le k + l \le p - 1$. So they are exactly $1, \dots, h$ in some order. Multiplying, $h! \equiv \prod s_i \prod (p - t_j) \equiv (-1)^\mu \prod s_i \prod t_j \equiv (-1)^\mu\, a^h\, h! \pmod p$. Cancel $h!$ and use Euler's criterion.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Corollary 7 (second supplementary law)">

$\left(\frac{2}{p}\right) = (-1)^{(p^2-1)/8}$: $2$ is a square modulo $p$ iff $p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For $a = 2$, the residues $2k$ with $1 \le k \le h$ are just $2, 4, \dots, p - 1$. Those exceeding $\frac p2$ are the $2k$ with $k \gt \frac p4$, so $\mu = h - \lfloor \frac p4 \rfloor$. Checking $p = 8m + 1, 8m + 3, 8m + 5, 8m + 7$ gives $\mu = 2m, 2m + 1, 2m + 1, 2m + 2$. So $\mu$ is even exactly for $p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8$, and $(-1)^\mu = (-1)^{(p^2 - 1)/8}$ in each case.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 8 (law of quadratic reciprocity)">

For distinct odd primes $p$ and $q$,

$$
\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\cdot\frac{q-1}{2}} .
$$

So $\left(\frac pq\right) = \left(\frac qp\right)$ unless $p \equiv q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, in which case $\left(\frac pq\right) = -\left(\frac qp\right)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

(Eisenstein, 1844.) *Step 1: for odd $a$ with $p \nmid a$, $\mu \equiv \sum_{k=1}^{h} \lfloor \frac{ka}{p} \rfloor \pmod 2$.* With the notation of Lemma 6, $ka = p\lfloor\frac{ka}{p}\rfloor + (\text{residue})$. Summing over $k = 1, \dots, h$ gives

$$
a\sum_{k=1}^h k = p\sum_{k=1}^h \Big\lfloor \frac{ka}{p}\Big\rfloor + \sum_i s_i + \sum_j t_j .
$$

From the proof of Lemma 6, $\sum_{k=1}^h k = \sum_i s_i + \sum_j (p - t_j) = \sum_i s_i + p\mu - \sum_j t_j$. Subtracting, $(a - 1)\sum k = p\big(\sum\lfloor\frac{ka}p\rfloor - \mu\big) + 2\sum_j t_j$. Reduce modulo $2$: $a - 1$ is even and $p$ is odd, so $\sum\lfloor \frac{ka}{p}\rfloor \equiv \mu$.

*Step 2: count lattice points.* Consider the points $(x, y)$ with $1 \le x \le \frac{p-1}{2}$ and $1 \le y \le \frac{q-1}{2}$; there are $\frac{p-1}{2}\cdot\frac{q-1}{2}$ of them. None lies on the line $py = qx$, since that would need $p \mid x$. Below the line, the column at $x$ contains $\lfloor \frac{qx}{p} \rfloor$ points, so there are $\sum_x \lfloor\frac{qx}{p}\rfloor \equiv \mu(q, p)$ points below. Symmetrically, there are $\sum_y \lfloor \frac{py}{q}\rfloor \equiv \mu(p, q)$ points above. By Gauss's lemma,

$$
\left(\frac qp\right)\left(\frac pq\right) = (-1)^{\mu(q,p) + \mu(p, q)} = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\cdot\frac{q-1}{2}} .
$$

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Step back and see how little is left of the mystery. Gauss's lemma turns each Legendre symbol into the parity of the number of lattice points on one side of the line $py = qx$, and reciprocity is then the identity "points below + points above = all points", read modulo $2$. The symmetry between $p$ and $q$, so mysterious in the statement, becomes the exchange of the two axes of a rectangle.

</Narrator>

<Example title="Computing a Legendre symbol">

Is $219$ a square modulo $383$? Both numbers are small enough to check by hand, but reciprocity makes it quick. $219 = 3 \cdot 73$, so $\left(\frac{219}{383}\right) = \left(\frac{3}{383}\right)\left(\frac{73}{383}\right)$. Since $3 \equiv 383 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, $\left(\frac{3}{383}\right) = -\left(\frac{383}{3}\right) = -\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = 1$. Since $73 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $\left(\frac{73}{383}\right) = \left(\frac{383}{73}\right) = \left(\frac{18}{73}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{73}\right)\left(\frac{9}{73}\right) = 1 \cdot 1$, as $73 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$. So $\left(\frac{219}{383}\right) = 1$, and $219$ is a square modulo $383$; indeed $169^2 = 28561 = 74 \cdot 383 + 219$.

</Example>

<Section id="arithmetic-functions" kind="proof" title="Arithmetic functions and Möbius inversion">

<Theorem name="Proposition 9 (the Dirichlet ring)">

Dirichlet convolution is commutative and associative, with identity $\varepsilon$. If $f$ and $g$ are multiplicative, so is $f * g$. And $\mu * \mathbf 1 = \varepsilon$, that is, $\sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d) = [n = 1]$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$(f * g)(n) = \sum_{ab = n} f(a)g(b)$ is symmetric in $f, g$, and $((f * g) * h)(n) = \sum_{abc = n} f(a)g(b)h(c)$ is symmetric in the bracketing. Also $(f * \varepsilon)(n) = f(n)$. If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, the divisors of $mn$ are exactly the products $d e$ with $d \mid m$, $e \mid n$, each uniquely, and then $\gcd(d, e) = 1$ and $\gcd(m/d, n/e) = 1$. So $(f * g)(mn) = \sum_{d,e} f(d)f(e)g(\frac md)g(\frac ne) = (f * g)(m)(f * g)(n)$. Finally $\mu * \mathbf 1$ is multiplicative, and on a prime power $p^k$ with $k \ge 1$ it is $\mu(1) + \mu(p) = 1 - 1 = 0$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 10 (Möbius inversion)">

For arithmetic functions $f, g$: $\;g(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d)$ for all $n$ $\iff$ $f(n) = \sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d)\,g(n/d)$ for all $n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The first statement says $g = f * \mathbf 1$, the second $f = \mu * g$. If $g = f * \mathbf 1$ then $\mu * g = f * (\mathbf 1 * \mu) = f * \varepsilon = f$. If $f = \mu * g$ then $f * \mathbf 1 = g * (\mu * \mathbf 1) = g$.

</Proof>

<Example title="Euler's function and necklaces">

Gauss's identity $\sum_{d \mid n}\varphi(d) = n$ says $\varphi * \mathbf 1 = \operatorname{id}$. By inversion $\varphi = \mu * \operatorname{id}$, that is, $\varphi(n) = \sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d)\frac nd = n\prod_{p \mid n}\big(1 - \frac1p\big)$, since only square-free $d$ contribute. In the same way, the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$ is $\frac1n\sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d)q^{n/d}$ (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.fields-galois">fields and Galois theory</TopicLink>). That is also the number of aperiodic necklaces of $n$ beads in $q$ colours.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="two-squares" kind="proof" title="Sums of two squares">

<Theorem name="Theorem 11 (Fermat; proved by Euler)">

An odd prime $p$ is a sum of two squares if and only if $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*Only if.* Squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $4$, so $a^2 + b^2 \not\equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

*If.* Let $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. By Corollary 5 there is an $x$ with $p \mid x^2 + 1 = (x + i)(x - i)$ in the Gaussian integers $\Z[i]$. But $p$ divides neither factor, since $\frac xp \pm \frac 1p i \notin \Z[i]$. So $p$ is not a prime element of $\Z[i]$. Now $\Z[i]$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a UFD in which irreducible elements are prime (rings lesson, Theorems 6–9). So $p$ is not irreducible: $p = \alpha\beta$ with $\alpha, \beta$ non-units. Taking norms, $p^2 = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$ with $N(\alpha), N(\beta) \gt 1$, so $N(\alpha) = p$. Writing $\alpha = a + bi$ gives $p = a^2 + b^2$.

</Proof>

The representation is unique up to order and signs (by unique factorisation in $\Z[i]$). Combined with the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity

$$
(a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2) = (ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2,
$$

which is the multiplicativity of $N$ on $\Z[i]$, this gives the full answer. A positive integer is a sum of two squares iff every prime $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ divides it to an even power. (If $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ divides $a^2 + b^2$, then $q \mid a$ and $q \mid b$, because $-1$ is not a square modulo $q$.) Don Zagier (1990) found a famous "one-sentence" proof of Theorem 11, by an involution on a finite set with exactly one fixed point. Roger Heath-Brown's earlier argument underlies it.

</Section>

<Section id="pell" kind="proof" title="Pell's equation">

Let $d \gt 0$ be a non-square, so $\sqrt d$ is irrational. Write $\eta = x + y\sqrt d$ and $\bar\eta = x - y\sqrt d$. The set $U = \{\eta \mid x, y \in \Z,\ x^2 - dy^2 = 1\}$ is a group under multiplication. It is closed under products by the multiplicativity of the norm, and $\eta^{-1} = \bar\eta$. This is Brahmagupta's *bhāvanā*: $(x_1, y_1) \cdot (x_2, y_2) = (x_1x_2 + dy_1y_2,\ x_1y_2 + x_2y_1)$.

<Theorem name="Lemma 12 (Dirichlet's approximation theorem)">

For every irrational $\alpha$ there are infinitely many pairs of integers $(x, y)$ with $y \ge 1$ and $|x - y\alpha| \lt \frac1y$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Fix $Q \ge 1$. The $Q + 1$ numbers $\{0\alpha\}, \{1\alpha\}, \dots, \{Q\alpha\}$ (fractional parts) lie in $[0, 1)$. By the pigeonhole principle two of them, say for $0 \le j \lt k \le Q$, lie in the same interval $[\frac iQ, \frac{i+1}Q)$. Then $y = k - j \in [1, Q]$ and $x = \lfloor k\alpha \rfloor - \lfloor j\alpha \rfloor$ satisfy $|y\alpha - x| \lt \frac1Q \le \frac1y$. As $\alpha$ is irrational, $|y\alpha - x| \gt 0$. Taking $Q$ larger than $1/|y\alpha - x|$ produces a new pair each time, so there are infinitely many.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 13 (Pell's equation; Lagrange 1768)">

(a) Pell's equation has a non-trivial solution. (b) There is a least solution $\varepsilon = x_1 + y_1\sqrt d \gt 1$, the **fundamental solution**, and every solution is $\pm\varepsilon^n$ with $n \in \Z$. So $U \cong \Z/2 \times \Z$, and there are infinitely many solutions.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

(a) For each pair from Lemma 12 (with $\alpha = \sqrt d$),

$$
|x^2 - dy^2| = |x - y\sqrt d|\,|x + y\sqrt d| \lt \frac1y\big(2y\sqrt d + 1\big) \le 2\sqrt d + 1 .
$$

So infinitely many pairs give values $x^2 - dy^2$ in a finite set. None is $0$, since $\sqrt d$ is irrational. By the pigeonhole principle infinitely many pairs share the same value $N$ and the same residues of $x$ and $y$ modulo $|N|$. Take two distinct such pairs $(x_1, y_1)$, $(x_2, y_2)$ with positive coordinates, and set

$$
\frac{x_1 + y_1\sqrt d}{x_2 + y_2\sqrt d} = \frac{(x_1 + y_1\sqrt d)(x_2 - y_2\sqrt d)}{N} = u + v\sqrt d, \qquad u = \frac{x_1x_2 - dy_1y_2}{N}, \quad v = \frac{x_2y_1 - x_1y_2}{N} .
$$

Modulo $|N|$, $x_1x_2 - dy_1y_2 \equiv x_1^2 - dy_1^2 = N \equiv 0$ and $x_2y_1 - x_1y_2 \equiv x_1y_1 - x_1y_1 = 0$, so $u, v \in \Z$. Taking norms, $u^2 - dv^2 = N \cdot N / N^2 = 1$. If $v = 0$ then $u = \pm 1$, and the two positive numbers $x_i + y_i\sqrt d$ would be equal, so the pairs would be equal. Hence $v \ne 0$.

(b) If $\eta = x + y\sqrt d \in U$ and $\eta \gt 1$, then $\bar\eta = \eta^{-1} \in (0, 1)$, so $x = \frac{\eta + \bar\eta}{2} \gt 0$ and $y = \frac{\eta - \bar\eta}{2\sqrt d} \gt 0$. Such $\eta$ exist by (a): replace $(x, y)$ by $(|x|, |y|)$. Among them, the one with the least $x$ (and so the least $y$) is the least element $\varepsilon$ of $U \cap (1, \infty)$. Now let $\eta \in U$ with $\eta \gt 0$, and choose $n$ with $\varepsilon^n \le \eta \lt \varepsilon^{n+1}$. Then $\eta\varepsilon^{-n} \in U \cap [1, \varepsilon)$, so it equals $1$ by minimality, and $\eta = \varepsilon^n$. If $\eta \lt 0$, apply this to $-\eta$.

</Proof>

**Finding $\varepsilon$.** The continued fraction of $\sqrt d$ is periodic, $\sqrt d = [a_0; \overline{a_1, \dots, a_r}]$ with $a_r = 2a_0$ (Lagrange). If $\frac{h_i}{k_i}$ are its convergents, the fundamental solution is $(h_{r-1}, k_{r-1})$ if $r$ is even, and $(h_{2r-1}, k_{2r-1})$ if $r$ is odd. For $d = 13$, $\sqrt{13} = [3; \overline{1, 1, 1, 1, 6}]$ has odd period $5$, and the method gives $649^2 - 13 \cdot 180^2 = 1$. For $d = 61$ the period has length $11$, and the answer is the enormous solution of the introduction. For proofs see Niven, Zuckerman and Montgomery (1991), chapter 7. The third experiment runs the algorithm.

The table is exact. The last lines are **statistical evidence** for a famous open problem. Emil Artin conjectured in 1927 that $2$ (indeed any integer other than $-1$ that is not a perfect square) is a primitive root modulo infinitely many primes, with a density close to $0.3739558$ (Artin's constant, $\prod_p \big(1 - \frac{1}{p(p-1)}\big)$, for $2$). Christopher Hooley proved this in 1967 *assuming* the generalised Riemann hypothesis. Unconditionally, Roger Heath-Brown (1986) showed that at most two prime numbers fail to be primitive roots for infinitely many primes — but no specific integer, not even $2$, has been proved to work.

<Narrator who="margaret">

The first factor of Artin's constant can be seen without a computer. A primitive root is never a square (Euler's criterion gives $g^{(p-1)/2} \equiv -1$), so $2$ can be a primitive root only when $p \equiv \pm 3 \pmod 8$, which by Corollary 7 and Dirichlet's theorem is half of all primes: that is the factor $1 - \frac{1}{2 \cdot 1} = \frac12$ in $\prod_\ell \big(1 - \frac{1}{\ell(\ell - 1)}\big)$. The factor $\frac56$ for $\ell = 3$ removes the primes with $3 \mid p - 1$ for which $2$ is a cube, and so on, down to $0.374$.

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Every modulus has a primitive root."** Modulo $8$ every unit squares to $1$, and modulo $15$ every unit has order dividing $4 \lt 8 = \varphi(15)$. Primitive roots exist exactly for $n = 1, 2, 4, p^k, 2p^k$ with $p$ an odd prime (Theorem 3).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Quadratic reciprocity is an isolated trick."** It has more than two hundred published proofs, through Gauss sums, lattice points, cyclotomic fields, counting arguments and more, and far-reaching generalisations. These include cubic and biquadratic reciprocity (Gauss, Eisenstein, Jacobi), Hilbert's reciprocity law, the Artin reciprocity law of class field theory, and the Langlands programme, which can be read as a vast non-abelian reciprocity law.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A Jacobi symbol $\left(\frac an\right) = 1$ means that $a$ is a square modulo $n$."** The Legendre symbol does detect squares modulo a prime. Its extension to odd composite moduli, the Jacobi symbol $\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) = \prod \left(\frac{a}{p_i}\right)$, does not. For example $\left(\frac{2}{15}\right) = \left(\frac 23\right)\left(\frac 25\right) = (-1)(-1) = 1$, yet $2$ is not a square modulo $15$ (it is not even a square modulo $3$).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Every $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is a sum of two squares."** The congruence condition $n \not\equiv 3 \pmod 4$ is necessary only. $21 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is not a sum of two squares, because $3$ and $7$ divide it to odd powers. The full criterion is about the prime factorisation.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Pell's equation is Pell's."** Euler attributed it to John Pell by mistake (Pell did not solve it). The equation was studied by Brahmagupta (628), solved in general by the *chakravāla* method of Jayadeva and Bhāskara II (11th–12th centuries), and its solvability was first proved by Lagrange (1768).

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Reciprocity from cyclotomic fields">

Let $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/p}$ and form the **Gauss sum** $g = \sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac ap\right)\zeta^a$. A short computation gives $g^2 = \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)p = p^*$, so $\sqrt{p^*} \in \Q(\zeta)$. Now work modulo $q$ in the ring $\Z[\zeta]$. On one side $g^q \equiv \sum \left(\frac ap\right)\zeta^{aq} = \left(\frac qp\right)g$, by the Frobenius property $(x + y)^q \equiv x^q + y^q$. On the other, $g^q = g\,(p^*)^{(q-1)/2} \equiv g\left(\frac{p^*}{q}\right)$, by Euler's criterion. Comparing the two gives reciprocity. In the language of <TopicLink id="math.algebra.fields-galois">Galois theory</TopicLink>, $\left(\frac{p^*}{q}\right)$ records whether the Frobenius element at $q$ fixes $\sqrt{p^*}$, and $\left(\frac qp\right)$ records whether the corresponding element of $\operatorname{Gal}(\Q(\zeta)/\Q) \cong (\Z/p)^\times$ is a square. Reciprocity says these agree, and Artin reciprocity generalises it to all abelian extensions.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Lattices and hyperbolas">

The proof of Theorem 8 is a lattice-point count, and lattice geometry gives other proofs too. By **Minkowski's theorem**, a convex region symmetric about $0$ with area greater than $4\det L$ contains a non-zero point of the lattice $L$. Take $L = \{(a, b) \mid b \equiv xa \pmod p\}$, where $x^2 \equiv -1$, with $\det L = p$, and the disc $a^2 + b^2 \lt 2p$. It contains a point with $0 \lt a^2 + b^2 \lt 2p$ and $p \mid a^2 + b^2$, so $a^2 + b^2 = p$. Pell's equation is geometric too. Its solutions are the lattice points on the hyperbola $x^2 - dy^2 = 1$, and $\varepsilon$ acts on the hyperbola as a hyperbolic "rotation" (a Lorentz boost) preserving the lattice $\Z[\sqrt d]$. The group $U \cong \Z/2 \times \Z$ is the group of such symmetries. This is the first case of Dirichlet's unit theorem in <TopicLink id="math.number.algebraic-number-theory">algebraic number theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Primes in progressions and L-functions">

Are there infinitely many primes $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$? Yes. If $p_1, \dots, p_k$ were all of them, any prime factor of $(2p_1\cdots p_k)^2 + 1$ would be $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ by Corollary 5, and new. **Dirichlet's theorem** (1837) gives infinitely many primes $\equiv a \pmod m$ whenever $\gcd(a, m) = 1$, and each class receives the same share $1/\varphi(m)$. The proof is analytic. It uses the $L$-functions $L(s, \chi) = \sum \chi(n)n^{-s}$ attached to characters $\chi$ of $(\Z/m)^\times$, and the key point is that $L(1, \chi) \ne 0$. For $\chi(n) = \left(\frac{-1}{n}\right)$, the character modulo $4$, Leibniz's series $1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \dots = \frac\pi4$ is $L(1, \chi)$. Its non-vanishing is the first case. See <TopicLink id="math.number.analytic-number-theory">analytic number theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="probability" title="Heuristics, biases and densities">

Many statements in this subject are **densities**: half the odd primes are $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$; $2$ is a quadratic residue for half the primes, namely those $\equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8$; $2$ is a primitive root for a proportion $\approx 0.374$ of primes (conjecturally). Artin's constant comes from a probabilistic heuristic. For each prime $\ell$, the chance that $\ell \mid p - 1$ *and* $2$ is an $\ell$-th power modulo $p$ is "$\frac{1}{\ell(\ell - 1)}$", and treating these events as independent gives $\prod_\ell \big(1 - \frac1{\ell(\ell-1)}\big)$. Heuristics like this are guides, not proofs. They can even mislead: **Chebyshev's bias** is the observation that primes $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$ are ahead of primes $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ in most initial segments. Rubinstein and Sarnak (1994) quantified it, under the generalised Riemann hypothesis and a linear independence hypothesis: the logarithmic density of $x$ for which $3 \bmod 4$ leads is about $0.9959$.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="Cryptography and fast algorithms">

Primitive roots are the setting of **Diffie–Hellman key exchange** (1976). With a public prime $p$ and primitive root $g$, two parties exchange $g^a$ and $g^b$ and share the secret $g^{ab}$. Computing $g^a$ is fast by repeated squaring. Recovering $a$ — the discrete logarithm — is believed to be hard for well-chosen large $p$, a belief supported by decades of cryptanalysis, not by proof (<TopicLink id="math.applied.cryptography">cryptography</TopicLink>). Legendre and Jacobi symbols can be computed in polynomial time with reciprocity, without factoring. Square roots modulo a prime are found efficiently by the Tonelli–Shanks algorithm. Distinguishing squares from non-squares modulo a composite $n = pq$ without knowing $p$ and $q$ seems hard, and the Goldwasser–Micali cryptosystem (1982) is built on that assumption.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **India.** Brahmagupta's *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628) introduced the composition law (*bhāvanā*) for $x^2 - Ny^2 = k$ and used it to generate solutions of Pell's equation. Jayadeva (11th century) and Bhāskara II (*Bījagaṇita*, 1150) gave the cyclic *chakravāla* method, which always succeeds, and solved $x^2 - 61y^2 = 1$.
- **Fermat and Euler.** Fermat stated the two-squares theorem (letter to Mersenne, 1640) and challenged mathematicians with $x^2 - 61y^2 = 1$ (1657); William Brouncker and John Wallis found methods. Euler proved the two-squares theorem (1747–1749), introduced the totient and the term "primitive root", and conjectured quadratic reciprocity. Lagrange proved that Pell's equation always has a non-trivial solution (1768).
- **Legendre and Gauss.** Legendre introduced his symbol (1798) and published an incomplete proof of reciprocity. Gauss gave the first complete proof in the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801), together with the first proof that primitive roots exist modulo every prime, and eventually published eight proofs. Gotthold Eisenstein's lattice-point proof (1844) is the one given here.
- **The nineteenth and twentieth centuries.** August Möbius introduced his function (1832). Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet proved his theorem on primes in progressions (1837), founding analytic number theory. Emil Artin made his conjecture on primitive roots in 1927, and Christopher Hooley proved it conditionally in 1967. Reciprocity grew into class field theory (Hilbert, Takagi, Artin) and then into the Langlands programme.

</Section>
