<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A garden against a wall">

Students at a secondary school in Kisumu are fencing a vegetable garden against the long wall of their classroom block. They have $20$ metres of fencing for the other three sides. If the two sides that stick out from the wall are $x$ metres long, the side parallel to the wall is $20 - 2x$ metres, and the garden's area is

$$
A(x) = x(20 - 2x) = 20x - 2x^2 \quad \text{square metres}.
$$

Two questions come up at once. The agriculture teacher wants exactly $48\ \text{m}^2$: what should $x$ be? And what is the **largest** garden the fencing can enclose?

The first question is an equation, $-2x^2 + 20x = 48$, in which the unknown appears squared: a **quadratic equation**. The second is about the highest point of the graph of $A$, which turns out to be a parabola. By the end of this lesson you will be able to answer both, and to prove that your answers are right.

</Section>

Split the $10x$ into two strips of $5x$ and lay them along two sides of the square $x^2$. The L-shape has area $39$, and it is a large square with a $5 \times 5$ corner missing. Add that corner to both sides:

$$
x^2 + 10x + 25 = 64, \qquad (x + 5)^2 = 64, \qquad x + 5 = 8, \qquad x = 3.
$$

<Narrator who="chloe">

Al-Khwārizmī only looked for positive lengths, so he found $x = 3$. But $(x + 5)^2 = 64$ also allows $x + 5 = -8$, giving $x = -13$. Check it: $169 - 130 = 39$. A picture made of lengths can't show negative numbers, and algebra can.

</Narrator>

Every quadratic equation can be solved this way. Doing it once and for all, with letters instead of numbers, produces the quadratic formula.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

<Definition term="quadratic equation and function">

A **quadratic equation** is an equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ where $a, b, c$ are real numbers and $a \neq 0$. The function $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ is a **quadratic function**, and its graph is called a **parabola**. A solution of the equation is a **root** of $f$ (or a **zero**).

</Definition>

<Definition term="discriminant">

The **discriminant** of $ax^2 + bx + c$ is the number $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="vertex and axis of symmetry">

The **vertex** of a parabola is its lowest point (if $a > 0$) or highest point (if $a < 0$). The **axis of symmetry** is the vertical line through the vertex: the parabola is its own mirror image in this line.

</Definition>

We will use one property of the real numbers again and again.

<Theorem name="Zero-product property">

For real numbers $u$ and $v$: if $uv = 0$, then $u = 0$ or $v = 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose $uv = 0$ and $u \neq 0$. Multiplying both sides by $1/u$ gives $v = \tfrac{1}{u} \cdot 0 = 0$.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="By factorising: the garden">

The garden needs $20x - 2x^2 = 48$. Rearranging and dividing by $2$: $x^2 - 10x + 24 = 0$. We look for two numbers with product $24$ and sum $-10$: $-4$ and $-6$. So

$$
(x - 4)(x - 6) = 0,
$$

and by the zero-product property $x = 4$ or $x = 6$. Both work: a $4 \times 12$ garden or a $6 \times 8$ garden. Factorising is quick when the roots are whole numbers, but most quadratics do not factorise so nicely.

</Example>

<Example title="By completing the square">

Solve $x^2 + 6x - 7 = 0$. Half of $6$ is $3$, and $(x + 3)^2 = x^2 + 6x + 9$, so

$$
x^2 + 6x - 7 = (x + 3)^2 - 16 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x + 3)^2 = 16 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x + 3 = \pm 4.
$$

So $x = 1$ or $x = -7$. Do not forget the $\pm$: both $4^2$ and $(-4)^2$ are $16$.

</Example>

<Example title="By the formula">

Solve $2x^2 - 4x - 3 = 0$. Here $a = 2$, $b = -4$, $c = -3$ and $\Delta = 16 + 24 = 40 > 0$, so there are two real roots:

$$
x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{40}}{4} = 1 \pm \frac{\sqrt{10}}{2} \approx 2.58 \text{ or } -0.58.
$$

</Example>

<Example title="What the discriminant says">

- $x^2 - 4x + 4$: $\Delta = 16 - 16 = 0$. One root, $x = 2$, because $x^2 - 4x + 4 = (x - 2)^2$. The parabola just touches the $x$-axis.
- $x^2 + x + 1$: $\Delta = 1 - 4 = -3 < 0$. No real roots: the parabola stays above the axis. In the complex numbers there are two roots, $x = \tfrac{-1 \pm i\sqrt 3}{2}$.

</Example>

<Example title="A quadratic with given roots">

Find a quadratic with roots $2 + \sqrt 3$ and $2 - \sqrt 3$. Their sum is $4$ and their product is $4 - 3 = 1$. By Vieta's formulas (proved below), $x^2 - 4x + 1$ works. Check with the formula: $x = \tfrac{4 \pm \sqrt{12}}{2} = 2 \pm \sqrt 3$.

</Example>

<Example title="The largest garden">

$A(x) = -2x^2 + 20x$ has $a = -2 < 0$, so its vertex is a highest point, at $x = -\tfrac{b}{2a} = -\tfrac{20}{-4} = 5$. The largest garden is $5$ m by $10$ m, with area $A(5) = 50\ \text{m}^2$. Completing the square shows it directly: $A(x) = 50 - 2(x - 5)^2$, and $2(x - 5)^2 \ge 0$.

</Example>

<Example title="A ball kicked upwards">

At a football training session in Manchester, a player kicks a ball straight up from a height of $1$ m at $14$ m/s. Ignoring air resistance (a modelling assumption), its height after $t$ seconds is about $h(t) = 1 + 14t - 4.9t^2$ metres. The vertex is at $t = \tfrac{14}{9.8} \approx 1.43$ s, and the maximum height is

$$
h_{\max} = c - \frac{b^2}{4a} = 1 - \frac{196}{4 \cdot (-4.9)} = 1 + 10 = 11 \text{ m}.
$$

The ball lands when $h(t) = 0$: $t = \tfrac{14 + \sqrt{196 + 19.6}}{9.8} \approx 2.93$ s. The other root, about $-0.07$ s, is a valid solution of the equation but means nothing for the ball. Always ask which roots make sense in the situation.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Proofs: the formula, the discriminant and the vertex">

<Theorem name="Completing the square">

If $a \neq 0$, then for every $x$:

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a}.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Expand the right-hand side: $a\left(x^2 + \tfrac{b}{a}x + \tfrac{b^2}{4a^2}\right) - \tfrac{b^2}{4a} + \tfrac{4ac}{4a} = ax^2 + bx + \tfrac{b^2}{4a} - \tfrac{b^2}{4a} + c = ax^2 + bx + c$.

</Proof>

Multiplying by $4a$ clears every fraction and gives an identity that holds even when $a = 0$:

$$
4a\,(ax^2 + bx + c) = (2ax + b)^2 - (b^2 - 4ac). \tag{$\ast$}
$$

<Theorem name="Quadratic formula">

Let $a \ne 0$ and $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac \ge 0$. Then $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ if and only if

$$
x = \frac{-b + \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a} \quad \text{or} \quad x = \frac{-b - \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By $(\ast)$, and because $4a \neq 0$, the equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ is equivalent to $(2ax + b)^2 = \Delta$. Since $\Delta \ge 0$, it has the square root $s = \sqrt\Delta \ge 0$, and $(2ax + b)^2 = s^2$ means $(2ax + b - s)(2ax + b + s) = 0$. By the zero-product property, $2ax + b = s$ or $2ax + b = -s$, that is, $x = \tfrac{-b \pm s}{2a}$. Every step is reversible, so these two numbers are roots, and there are no others.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="The discriminant decides">

For $a \neq 0$, the equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ has

- **two different real roots** if $\Delta > 0$;
- **exactly one real root**, $x = -\tfrac{b}{2a}$, if $\Delta = 0$;
- **no real roots** if $\Delta < 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $\Delta > 0$ then $\sqrt\Delta > 0$, and the two roots given by the formula differ by $\tfrac{2\sqrt\Delta}{2a} \ne 0$. If $\Delta = 0$ the formula gives $-\tfrac{b}{2a}$ twice. If $\Delta < 0$, a root $x$ would satisfy $(2ax + b)^2 = \Delta < 0$ by $(\ast)$. But the square of a real number is never negative.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Vieta's formulas">

If $r$ and $s$ are the roots of $ax^2 + bx + c$ (with $r = s$ allowed when $\Delta = 0$), then

$$
r + s = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad rs = \frac{c}{a}.
$$

</Theorem>

<Proof>

*From the formula:* $r + s = \tfrac{-b + \sqrt\Delta - b - \sqrt\Delta}{2a} = -\tfrac{b}{a}$ and $rs = \tfrac{b^2 - \Delta}{4a^2} = \tfrac{4ac}{4a^2} = \tfrac{c}{a}$.

*Without the formula*, for two different roots: subtracting $as^2 + bs + c = 0$ from $ar^2 + br + c = 0$ gives $(r - s)\big(a(r + s) + b\big) = 0$. Since $r - s \neq 0$, $r + s = -b/a$. Then $c = -ar^2 - br = -ar^2 + a(r + s)r = ars$.

</Proof>

Equivalently, $ax^2 + bx + c = a(x - r)(x - s)$. This is the factor theorem of the <TopicLink id="math.algebra.polynomials">polynomials</TopicLink> lesson at work: each root gives a factor.

<Theorem name="Vertex and axis of symmetry">

Let $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a \neq 0$ and put $h = -\tfrac{b}{2a}$. Then:

1. $f(h + t) = f(h - t)$ for every $t$: the line $x = h$ is an axis of symmetry;
2. $f(h) = c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a} = -\tfrac{\Delta}{4a}$;
3. if $a > 0$ then $f(x) \ge f(h)$ for all $x$ (the vertex $(h, f(h))$ is the lowest point); if $a < 0$ then $f(x) \le f(h)$ (it is the highest point).

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By completing the square, $f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + f(h)$ with $f(h) = -\tfrac{\Delta}{4a}$. Then $f(h + t) = at^2 + f(h) = f(h - t)$, which proves 1. For 3, $f(x) - f(h) = a(x - h)^2$ has the sign of $a$, because $(x - h)^2 \ge 0$.

</Proof>

<Note>

There is a pleasant shortcut, popularised by Po-Shen Loh in 2019 (and, as he points out, rooted in very old ideas). Divide by $a$ to get $x^2 + Bx + C = 0$. By Vieta, the roots have average $-B/2$, so write them as $-\tfrac{B}{2} \pm u$. Their product is $\tfrac{B^2}{4} - u^2 = C$, so $u = \sqrt{\tfrac{B^2}{4} - C}$ — the quadratic formula again.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Dividing both sides by $x$.** From $x^2 = 5x$, dividing by $x$ gives $x = 5$ and loses the root $x = 0$. Dividing by $x$ is only allowed when $x \ne 0$. Instead, factorise: $x^2 - 5x = x(x - 5) = 0$, so $x = 0$ or $x = 5$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Misusing the zero-product property.** From $(x - 1)(x - 2) = 6$ it does **not** follow that $x - 1 = 6$ or $x - 2 = 6$. The property only works with $0$ on the right: a product can be $6$ in infinitely many ways. Expand and rearrange first: $x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0$, so $(x - 4)(x + 1) = 0$, and $x = 4$ or $x = -1$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$\Delta < 0$ means there are no solutions at all."** There are no **real** solutions. In the complex numbers there are always two (equal when $\Delta = 0$): $x = \tfrac{-b \pm i\sqrt{-\Delta}}{2a}$. Whether an equation "has solutions" depends on which numbers you allow; see <TopicLink id="math.number.complex-numbers">complex numbers</TopicLink>.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$x^2 = 9$ means $x = 3$."** It means $x = 3$ **or** $x = -3$. The symbol $\sqrt 9$ means only the non-negative root $3$, which is why the formula needs $\pm$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The vertex is at $x = \tfrac{b}{2a}$."** It is at $x = -\tfrac{b}{2a}$, the average of the two roots by Vieta. Check with $y = x^2 - 4x$: its roots are $0$ and $4$, so the vertex is at $x = 2 = -\tfrac{-4}{2}$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Every parabola has the same shape">

A parabola is a **conic section**: the set of points at equal distance from a point (the *focus*) and a line (the *directrix*). The parabola $y = ax^2$ with $a > 0$ has focus $(0, \tfrac{1}{4a})$ and directrix $y = -\tfrac{1}{4a}$. Rays parallel to the axis reflect off it through the focus, which is why satellite dishes and solar cookers are parabolic. Surprisingly, **all parabolas are similar**. Scaling the plane by a factor $k$ sends $y = x^2$ to $y = \tfrac{1}{k}x^2$, and any parabola can be moved onto some $y = ax^2$. A "steep" parabola is just a zoomed-out view of a "flat" one (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.conic-sections">conic sections</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Quadratics in clock arithmetic">

Completing the square only needs division by $2$, so the quadratic formula also works modulo an odd prime $p$, *provided the discriminant has a square root there*. For example, $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real roots, but modulo $5$ it has the roots $2$ and $3$ ($4 + 1 = 5$ and $9 + 1 = 10$). Modulo $7$ it has none, since the squares modulo $7$ are only $0, 1, 2, 4$. Which numbers are squares modulo $p$ is a deep question. A classical theorem, going back to Fermat and Euler, says that $-1$ is a square modulo an odd prime $p$ exactly when $p$ leaves remainder $1$ on division by $4$ (see <TopicLink id="math.number.elementary-number-theory">number theory</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="The vertex is where the slope is zero">

Near its vertex, a parabola is momentarily flat. Calculus makes this precise: the slope of $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ at $x$ is $2ax + b$, which is zero exactly at $x = -\tfrac{b}{2a}$. For quadratics, completing the square finds maxima and minima without calculus. For other functions the idea "look where the slope is zero" is the starting point of optimisation (see <TopicLink id="math.analysis.intro-calculus">introductory calculus</TopicLink>). The garden problem also hides an inequality between means: for positive $u$ and $v$, $uv \le \left(\tfrac{u + v}{2}\right)^2$, because the difference is $\left(\tfrac{u - v}{2}\right)^2 \ge 0$.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="physics" title="Why thrown things trace parabolas">

Galileo Galilei found experimentally, and published in *Two New Sciences* (1638), that a falling body covers distances proportional to the *square* of the time. With constant downward acceleration $g$, the height of a projectile is $h(t) = h_0 + v_0 t - \tfrac12 g t^2$, a quadratic in $t$. A ball thrown at an angle travels horizontally at constant speed while its height follows this quadratic, so its path is a parabola too. This is a model: it ignores air resistance and assumes $g$ is constant, which is excellent for a football and poor for a feather or a rocket (see <TopicLink id="math.applied.modelling-with-functions">modelling with functions</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Mesopotamia.** Old Babylonian clay tablets from around 1800 BCE, such as the tablet known as BM 13901, pose problems like "I added the area and the side of my square: $\tfrac34$", that is, $x^2 + x = \tfrac34$. They solve them by a cut-and-paste procedure equivalent to completing the square.
- **Egypt.** A Middle Kingdom papyrus now in Berlin contains a problem equivalent to finding two squares whose areas add up to $100$ when one side is $\tfrac34$ of the other.
- **India.** **Brahmagupta**'s *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628 CE) gave an explicit rule for solving quadratic equations. **Śrīdhara** (whose dates are uncertain, perhaps the 8th or 9th century) gave the method that multiplies by $4a$ and adds $b^2$, our identity $(\ast)$. It is known from Bhāskara II's quotation of it in the 12th century.
- **Baghdad.** **Al-Khwārizmī** (around 820 CE) classified quadratic equations into six types, since he used only positive numbers, and justified each solution by a geometric picture like the one above.
- **Europe.** Abraham bar Ḥiyya's treatise, translated into Latin in 1145, helped bring these methods to Europe. In his *Ars Magna* (1545), Gerolamo Cardano met $5 \pm \sqrt{-15}$ as "solutions" of dividing $10$ into two parts with product $40$, an early appearance of complex numbers. René Descartes (1637) called such numbers "imaginary".
