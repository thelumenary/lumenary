<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Say it once, do it many times">

At a robot club in Cairo, Yasmin is programming a little floor robot to drive round a square mat. She types:

> forward, turn right, forward, turn right, forward, turn right, forward, turn right

Eight instructions. Her friend Omar laughs. "You said the same thing four times! Why not just say: **repeat 4 times: forward, turn right**?"

That one line does the same job as all eight instructions. And if Yasmin wants the robot to go round the mat ten times, she does not have to type 80 instructions — she just changes 4 into 40.

Later the robot has to cross a pretend road on the mat. "Go when it's safe" is not precise enough for a robot. Yasmin writes: **if the light is green, then go forward; otherwise, wait.** Now the robot can make a **decision**.

Hello! I'm Chloe. Loops and decisions are everywhere — in dances, in games, in recipes, and even in the way you add numbers on paper. Why does the robot come back home after a square? Let's find out.

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Repeating and deciding">

You already know how to follow a program — a list of instructions done one after another (see <TopicLink id="math.comp.following-instructions">Following and giving precise instructions</TopicLink>). Two new ideas make programs much more powerful.

**Repeating.** Many tasks do the same thing again and again: clap, stamp, clap, stamp…; stir, wait, stir, wait…; add the next column, add the next column… A **loop** says the repeated part once, and says how many times to repeat it — or when to stop.

**Deciding.** Some tasks depend on what is happening: *if* it is raining, take an umbrella; *if* the number is even, put it in the left box. A **condition** is a statement that is either true or false, like "the light is green" (see <TopicLink id="math.found.logic-statements">True, false, and, or, not</TopicLink>). An **if–then** rule uses a condition to choose what to do.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="loop">

A **loop** repeats a block of instructions. A **repeat-times loop** repeats it a fixed number of times: "repeat 4 times: forward, turn right". A **repeat-until loop** repeats it until a condition becomes true: "repeat: add 3, until the number is bigger than 20".

</Definition>

<Definition term="condition">

A **condition** is a statement that is either true or false, such as "the number is even" or "the light is green". It is used to decide which instructions to follow, or when to stop a loop.

</Definition>

<Definition term="if–then–otherwise">

An **if–then–otherwise** rule says: **if** the condition is true, **then** do one thing; **otherwise** (if it is false) do another. Rules can be chained: "if …, then …; otherwise, if …, then …; otherwise …". They are checked **in order**, and the first true condition wins.

</Definition>

<Definition term="algorithm">

An **algorithm** is a precise list of steps for doing a task, which can use loops and decisions. Following an algorithm needs no guessing.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Let's try together">

<Example title="Shapes from loops">

- **Square:** repeat 4 times: forward, turn right 90°.
- **Equilateral triangle:** repeat 3 times: forward, turn right **120°**.
- **Regular hexagon:** repeat 6 times: forward, turn right 60°.
- **Five-pointed star:** repeat 5 times: forward, turn right 144°.

In each closed shape the robot ends where it started, facing the same way. Count up its turning: $4 \times 90° = 360°$, $3 \times 120° = 360°$, $6 \times 60° = 360°$ — one whole turn. The star is special: $5 \times 144° = 720°$, two whole turns!

</Example>

<Example title="Repeat until">

"Start at 0. Repeat: add 3, **until** the number is bigger than 20."

$0 \to 3 \to 6 \to 9 \to 12 \to 15 \to 18 \to 21$. Now 21 is bigger than 20, so the loop stops. "Add 3" was done **7** times.

The loop checks the condition each time round. It does not know in advance how many times it will repeat — it just keeps going until the condition is true.

</Example>

<Example title="If–then rules for sorting">

Neema sorts number cards into three boxes with these rules, checked in order:

1. **If** the number is bigger than 50, **then** put it in the red box;
2. **otherwise, if** the number is even, **then** put it in the blue box;
3. **otherwise**, put it in the yellow box.

The card 64 goes in the red box (rule 1 is true, so we stop there). The card 18 goes in blue: rule 1 is false, rule 2 is true. The card 7 goes in yellow: rules 1 and 2 are both false. Every card ends up in exactly one box.

</Example>

<Example title="FizzBuzz: a game with decisions">

Players count in turn, 1, 2, 3, … but with these rules:

- **if** the number is a multiple of 3 **and** of 5, say "FizzBuzz";
- **otherwise, if** it is a multiple of 3, say "Fizz";
- **otherwise, if** it is a multiple of 5, say "Buzz";
- **otherwise**, say the number.

So the game goes: 1, 2, Fizz, 4, Buzz, Fizz, 7, 8, Fizz, Buzz, 11, Fizz, 13, 14, **FizzBuzz**, 16, …

The **order** of the rules matters. If we checked "multiple of 3?" first, then 15 would get "Fizz" — and nobody would ever say "FizzBuzz", because every multiple of 15 is also a multiple of 3. The whole game is a loop ("for each number, …") with decisions inside.

</Example>

<Example title="Column addition is a loop">

Here is the written method for adding two numbers, as an algorithm:

1. Start with a carry of 0.
2. **Repeat** for each column, from the ones column to the left:
   - add the two digits and the carry;
   - **if** the total is 10 or more, **then** write down the total minus 10 and carry 1;
   - **otherwise**, write down the total and carry 0.
3. At the end, **if** the carry is 1, write it down.

For $368 + 275$:

| column | digits + carry | total | write | carry |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ones | $8 + 5 + 0$ | 13 | 3 | 1 |
| tens | $6 + 7 + 1$ | 14 | 4 | 1 |
| hundreds | $3 + 2 + 1$ | 6 | 6 | 0 |

The answer is **643**. A loop (one pass per column) with a decision inside (carry or not)!

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="why" kind="proof" title="Why the loops work">

<Theorem name="The square loop comes home">

"Repeat 4 times: forward 1 square, turn right 90°" brings the robot back to where it started, facing the same way.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Each pass of the loop moves one square and then turns a quarter turn to the right. So the four moves go **up, right, down, left**, in that order. The one step up is cancelled by the one step down, and the one step right by the one step left, so the robot is back where it started. It has turned four quarter turns, which make one whole turn, so it faces the same way as at the start.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="The carry is never more than 1">

When we add two numbers by the column method, the carry into each column is always 0 or 1.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The carry into the ones column is 0. Now suppose the carry into some column is 0 or 1. The two digits in that column are each at most 9, so the column total is at most $9 + 9 + 1 = 19$. A total of at most 19 is less than 20, so it contains at most one ten: the carry into the next column is again 0 or 1. Going from column to column, the carry stays 0 or 1 all the way along.

</Proof>

**Why column addition gives the right answer.** In each column we add the digits in that place: ones with ones, tens with tens, and so on. When a column total is 10 or more, say 13 ones, we write the 3 ones down and move the **ten** to the tens column, where it counts as 1 ten. Nothing is lost and nothing is added: we are just regrouping ten ones as one ten, or ten tens as one hundred. So the digits we write down make exactly the sum.

<Narrator who="chloe">

So the carry isn't a magic trick — it's ten ones being swapped for one ten! You can watch the swap happen with base-ten blocks in the next section.

</Narrator>

<Theorem name="A loop that never stops">

"Start at 9. Repeat: take away 2, until the number is **exactly** 0." This loop never stops.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

The loop starts at 9, which is odd. Taking 2 away from an odd number always gives another odd number (9, 7, 5, 3, 1, −1, −3, …). So the number is **always odd**. But 0 is even, so the number is never 0: the stopping condition can never become true, and the loop goes on for ever.

</Proof>

This is a **bug**. To fix it, change the condition to one that will become true: "until the number is **less than 1**" stops after 5 steps, at $-1$. Or start at an even number: from 10 the loop reaches 0 after 5 steps.

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon only plays with statements that are exactly true or exactly false. Decide, and give a reason.

1. "*Repeat 4 times: forward, turn right* is the same as writing those two instructions out 4 times." — **True.** That is what the loop means: 8 instructions.
2. "*Repeat 3 times: forward, turn right* brings the robot home." — **False.** It walks three sides of the square and stops one square away.
3. "In FizzBuzz, the number 30 is said as 'Fizz'." — **False.** 30 is a multiple of 3 and of 5, and that rule is checked first: "FizzBuzz".
4. "In column addition, the carry can be 2." — **False.** A column total is at most $9 + 9 + 1 = 19$.
5. "*Start at 9, take away 2 until you reach exactly 0* stops after a while." — **False.** The number is always odd, so it is never 0.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"A loop runs for ever, or just once."** A repeat-times loop runs exactly as many times as it says: "repeat 4 times" runs 4 times. A repeat-until loop runs until its condition is true — sometimes 0 times (if the condition is already true at the start), sometimes 7 times, sometimes more.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Writing a stopping condition that can never become true.** "Start at 9, take away 2 until you reach exactly 0" never stops, because the number is always odd. Always ask: *will* the condition become true? Conditions like "until the number is less than 1" are safer than "until it is exactly 0".

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The order of if–then rules doesn't matter."** The rules are checked in order and the first true one wins. Checking "multiple of 3?" before "multiple of 15?" means FizzBuzz is never said.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"To draw a triangle, turn 60°."** The robot turns through the angle *outside* each corner. For an equilateral triangle that is 120°. A 60° turn makes part of a hexagon instead.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="The computer scientist: will it ever stop?">

Margaret's computer had to give up on the loop from 9 — it could not tell by itself that the loop would never stop. Could a cleverer program look at **any** program and decide whether it will stop? In 1936 Alan Turing proved that **no** program can do this correctly for every program. This is called the **halting problem**, and it is one of the most famous results about what computers can and cannot do — see <TopicLink id="math.comp.computability">Computability</TopicLink>. For a particular loop, though, a person can often find a proof, like our odd-number argument.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="The geometer: the turtle turns all the way round">

Add up all the turns the turtle makes in going once round the square, triangle or hexagon, and you get 360° every time: one full turn. Seymour Papert and his colleagues called this the **Total Turtle Trip Theorem**: a turtle that walks round the edge of a shape and comes back facing the same way has turned through a whole number of full turns — exactly one if the path never crosses itself. The five-pointed star crosses itself, and its turtle turns twice round, 720°. This is a first glimpse of ideas in geometry and topology about how much a path winds.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Written methods.** Around 820 CE, in Baghdad, Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī explained how to calculate with the Hindu–Arabic numerals, place value and all. His name, written *Algoritmi* in Latin, gave us the word **algorithm**.
- **Carrying on a counting board.** On an abacus — the Chinese *suanpan*, the Japanese *soroban*, the Russian *schoty* — a carry happens when a rod fills up: you clear it and move one bead on the next rod.
- **Euclid's loop.** About 300 BCE, Euclid's *Elements* described a method for finding the greatest common divisor of two numbers by repeatedly subtracting (or dividing with remainder) until nothing is left — a "repeat until" loop that always stops.
- **A loom that loops.** In 1804 Joseph-Marie Jacquard in France built a loom controlled by a chain of punched cards; joining the chain into a loop repeats a woven pattern.
- **The first loops in a program.** In her notes of 1843 on Charles Babbage's planned Analytical Engine, Ada Lovelace described how a group of operations could be repeated — a "cycle" — many times.
- **Will it stop?** In 1936 Alan Turing in England proved that no algorithm can decide, for every program, whether it will ever stop.
- **Turtles and blocks.** In the late 1960s Seymour Papert, Cynthia Solomon and Wally Feurzeig created the Logo language, in which children write `REPEAT 4 [FORWARD 100 RIGHT 90]` to draw a square. Since 2007, millions of children around the world have used repeat and if–then blocks in Scratch, made at the MIT Media Lab.

</Section>
