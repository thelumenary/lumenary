<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Below zero">

On a January morning in Ulaanbaatar, the thermometer may read $-25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The shore of the Dead Sea lies more than 400 metres **below** sea level, and the city of Baku sits about 28 metres below it. A shop that owes a supplier 300 dirhams has a balance of $-300$.

Whenever a quantity can go *below* a starting point, the counting numbers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ are not enough. We need numbers that point the other way.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="The number line, in both directions">

Picture the counting numbers marching to the right from $0$. The **integers** add their mirror images marching to the left:

$$
\Z = \{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}.
$$

Adding a positive number moves right; adding a negative number moves left. Every integer has an **opposite** on the other side of zero, the same distance away.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions and properties">

<Definition term="additive inverse">

For an integer $a$, its **opposite** or **additive inverse** $-a$ is the integer with $a + (-a) = 0$.

</Definition>

Subtraction is defined as adding the opposite: $a - b = a + (-b)$. So $4 - 7 = 4 + (-7) = -3$.

The integers obey a short list of rules. For all integers $a, b, c$:

| Property | Addition | Multiplication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commutative | $a + b = b + a$ | $ab = ba$ |
| Associative | $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ | $(ab)c = a(bc)$ |
| Identity | $a + 0 = a$ | $a \cdot 1 = a$ |
| Inverse | $a + (-a) = 0$ | — *(see below!)* |

and the two operations are linked by the **distributive law**: $a(b + c) = ab + ac$.

That empty box in the multiplication column is not a mistake. It will lead us somewhere wonderful.

<Note>

With only the counting numbers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ there is no number $n$ with $1 + n = 0$. Inventing the integers is exactly what makes additive inverses exist for *every* number.

</Note>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Adding and subtracting">

- $-3 + 5 = 2$: start at $-3$, move $5$ to the right.
- $2 - 9 = 2 + (-9) = -7$.
- $-4 - (-6) = -4 + 6 = 2$: subtracting a negative is adding its opposite.

</Example>

<Example title="Multiplying">

- $3 \cdot (-2) = (-2) + (-2) + (-2) = -6$.
- $(-3) \cdot 2 = 2 \cdot (-3) = -6$ by commutativity.
- $(-3) \cdot (-2) = 6$ — and the next section explains why it *must* be.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proof" kind="proof" title="Why negative times negative is positive">

Many people learn "a negative times a negative is a positive" as a rule to memorise. In fact it is **forced** by the properties in the table. If we want distributivity to keep working for negative numbers, there is no other choice.

First, two small facts.

<Theorem name="Lemma 1">

For every integer $a$: $\;a \cdot 0 = 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Since $0 = 0 + 0$, distributivity gives $a \cdot 0 = a \cdot (0 + 0) = a \cdot 0 + a \cdot 0$. Adding $-(a \cdot 0)$ to both sides leaves $0 = a \cdot 0$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Lemma 2">

For all integers $a, b$: $\;(-a)\,b = -(ab)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

By distributivity and Lemma 1, $ab + (-a)b = (a + (-a))\,b = 0 \cdot b = 0$. So $(-a)b$ is a number that adds to $ab$ to give $0$ — that is, it is the opposite of $ab$. (An integer has only one opposite: if $x + y = 0$, adding $-x$ to both sides gives $y = -x$.)

</Proof>

<Theorem name="sign rule">

For all integers $a, b$: $\;(-a)(-b) = ab$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Distributivity and Lemma 1 give

$$
(-a)(-b) + (-a)\,b = (-a)\big((-b) + b\big) = (-a) \cdot 0 = 0.
$$

So $(-a)(-b)$ is the opposite of $(-a)b$. By Lemma 2, $(-a)b = -(ab)$, and the opposite of $-(ab)$ is $ab$. Therefore $(-a)(-b) = ab$.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

So the sign rule isn't arbitrary. Once we agree that multiplication distributes over addition — which we need for almost everything — the rule follows. That's the joy of proof: it turns a "because I said so" into a "because it must be".

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="inverses" kind="human" title="The empty box: multiplicative inverses">

Every integer $a$ has an additive partner $-a$ with $a + (-a) = 0$. What about multiplication? Is there an integer $b$ with $a \cdot b = 1$?

For $a = 1$, yes: $b = 1$. For $a = -1$, yes: $b = -1$. But for $a = 2$?

<Theorem name="no half in the integers">

There is no integer $x$ with $2x = 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For any integer $x$, the number $2x$ is even. But $1$ is odd. So $2x \neq 1$.

</Proof>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Two negatives make a positive" — for addition too.** The sign rule is about *multiplication*. For addition, $(-3) + (-4) = -7$: two steps to the left take you further left.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$-a$ is always negative."** $-a$ means *the opposite of $a$*. If $a = -5$, then $-a = 5$, which is positive.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Subtraction is commutative."** $7 - 4 = 3$ but $4 - 7 = -3$. Rewriting subtraction as adding the opposite, $a + (-b)$, shows exactly where the asymmetry comes from.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The sign rule is just a convention."** It is a consequence: as the proof shows, any number system with these properties must have $(-a)(-b) = ab$.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Set-theoretic connection">

The counting numbers form a subset of the integers: $\N \subseteq \Z$. At university you may see a way to *build* the integers out of the counting numbers: an integer is represented by a pair $(m, n)$ of counting numbers, thought of as "$m - n$", where two pairs count as the same integer when $m + n' = m' + n$. This uses an **equivalence relation** — an idea you can preview on the *Remainder families* trail.

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="A glimpse of structure">

"Add $3$" is a transformation of the whole number line: it slides every integer three places right. It can always be **undone**, by "add $-3$". Doing "add $m$" and then "add $n$" is the same as "add $m + n$", and "add $0$" does nothing at all. A collection of reversible transformations that combine like this is called a **group** — and the integers under addition are one of the most important examples. The *Inverses and structure* trail starts here.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- In China, *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* — compiled roughly two thousand years ago — solved problems using positive and negative quantities. In Liu Hui's 3rd-century commentary, counting rods of two colours, red and black, distinguish the two kinds.
- In India, **Brahmagupta**'s *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628 CE) gave rules for calculating with "fortunes" and "debts" — positive and negative numbers — and with zero, including the rule that a debt times a debt is a fortune.
- Mathematicians writing in Arabic and Persian, and later in Europe, used these ideas cautiously. As late as 1545, Gerolamo Cardano called negative solutions "fictitious". Full acceptance of negative numbers as numbers came gradually over the following centuries.

</Section>

<Reflection title="debts and forgiveness">

Brahmagupta called negative numbers *debts*. In the prayer Jesus taught, Christians ask God to "forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" (Matthew 6:12). Adding the opposite cancels a debt, and the account returns to zero — but in real life someone must bear the cost. Christians understand forgiveness as exactly that kind of costly gift. Mathematics, of course, needs none of this to be true; it is offered only as a reflection.

</Reflection>
