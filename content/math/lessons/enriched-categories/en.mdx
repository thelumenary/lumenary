<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Hom-sets with structure">

In many categories the morphisms between two objects form more than a set. Linear maps $V \to W$ form a vector space, and composition is bilinear. Homomorphisms between abelian groups form an abelian group. Continuous maps between nice spaces form a space, and composition is continuous. Functors between two categories, with natural transformations between them, form a category. In each case the extra structure is not decoration: composition **respects** it.

In 1973 F. William Lawvere pushed this idea to an extreme. Take as the "hom-object" from $x$ to $y$ a single number: the distance $d(x, y) \in [0, \infty]$. Composing a route from $x$ to $y$ with a route from $y$ to $z$ gives a route from $x$ to $z$, and the inequality $d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)$ is exactly what composition says. The identity on $x$ is the statement $d(x, x) = 0$. A metric space *is* a category — of a new kind, enriched in the numbers $[0, \infty]$ — and categorical ideas such as functors, the Yoneda lemma and completeness acquire metric meanings.

This lesson develops **enriched category theory**: categories whose hom-objects live in a <TopicLink id="math.cat.monoidal-categories">monoidal category</TopicLink> $\mathcal{V}$.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Replace the set of arrows by an object of arrows">

An ordinary category has, for objects $A, B, C$, a composition function $\mathcal{C}(B, C) \times \mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{C}(A, C)$ and an identity element $\mathrm{id}_A \in \mathcal{C}(A, A)$, i.e. a function $1 \to \mathcal{C}(A, A)$ from a one-point set. Both are phrased using only the cartesian product $\times$ and the unit $1$ of $\cat{Set}$. Replace $(\cat{Set}, \times, 1)$ by any monoidal category $(\mathcal{V}, \otimes, I)$, and the definition still makes sense:

| base $\mathcal{V}$ | a hom-object is | composition $\mathcal{C}(B, C) \otimes \mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{C}(A, C)$ | a $\mathcal{V}$-category is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $(\cat{Set}, \times, 1)$ | a set | a function | a locally small category |
| truth values $(\{0 \le 1\}, \wedge, 1)$ | true or false | $(x \le y) \wedge (y \le z) \Rightarrow (x \le z)$ | a preorder |
| $([0, \infty], \ge, +, 0)$ | a number | $d(x, y) + d(y, z) \ge d(x, z)$ | a Lawvere metric space |
| $(\cat{Ab}, \otimes, \Z)$ | an abelian group | a bilinear map | a preadditive category |
| $(\cat{Vect}_k, \otimes, k)$ | a vector space | a bilinear map | a $k$-linear category |
| $(\cat{Cat}, \times, 1)$ | a category | a functor | a strict 2-category |

Two features are essential. Composition must be a **morphism of $\mathcal{V}$** — a bilinear map, a continuous map, a functor, an inequality — and there may be **no underlying elements**: a number has no "arrows in it", so a Lawvere metric space has no individual morphisms at all, only hom-objects.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Enriched categories, functors and transformations">

Fix a monoidal category $(\mathcal{V}, \otimes, I, \alpha, \lambda, \rho)$.

<Definition term="V-category">

A **$\mathcal{V}$-category** $\mathcal{C}$ consists of a collection of objects; for objects $A, B$ a **hom-object** $\mathcal{C}(A, B) \in \mathcal{V}$; for $A, B, C$ a **composition** morphism $M_{A,B,C} : \mathcal{C}(B, C) \otimes \mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{C}(A, C)$ in $\mathcal{V}$; and for each $A$ an **identity** morphism $j_A : I \to \mathcal{C}(A, A)$ in $\mathcal{V}$, such that composition is associative (the diagram below commutes) and unital: $M_{A,B,B} \circ (j_B \otimes 1) = \lambda$ and $M_{A,A,B} \circ (1 \otimes j_A) = \rho$ as maps $I \otimes \mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{C}(A, B)$ and $\mathcal{C}(A, B) \otimes I \to \mathcal{C}(A, B)$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="V-functor and V-natural transformation">

A **$\mathcal{V}$-functor** $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ assigns to each object $A$ an object $FA$, and to each pair $A, B$ a morphism $F_{A,B} : \mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{D}(FA, FB)$ of $\mathcal{V}$, compatible with composition and identities. For $\mathcal{V}$-functors $F, G : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$, a **$\mathcal{V}$-natural transformation** $\theta : F \Rightarrow G$ is a family of morphisms $\theta_A : I \to \mathcal{D}(FA, GA)$ in $\mathcal{V}$ such that, for all $A, B$, the two composites $\mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{D}(FA, GB)$ — "apply $G$, then precompose with $\theta_A$" and "apply $F$, then postcompose with $\theta_B$" — are equal.

</Definition>

<Definition term="underlying category">

The **underlying category** $\mathcal{C}_0$ of a $\mathcal{V}$-category has the same objects and $\mathcal{C}_0(A, B) = \mathcal{V}(I, \mathcal{C}(A, B))$, with composition $g \circ f = M \circ (g \otimes f) \circ \lambda_I^{-1}$. For $\mathcal{V} = \cat{Set}$, $\cat{Set}(1, X) \cong X$ recovers $\mathcal{C}$; for $\mathcal{V} = \cat{Ab}$ it forgets the group structure on hom-sets; for $\mathcal{V} = [0, \infty]$ there is one arrow $x \to y$ when $d(x, y) = 0$ and none otherwise.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Lawvere metric space">

A **Lawvere metric space** is a set $X$ with distances $d(x, y) \in [0, \infty]$ such that $d(x, x) = 0$ and $d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)$. Unlike a metric, $d$ need not be symmetric, may take the value $\infty$, and may vanish for $x \neq y$.

</Definition>

**Closedness.** $\mathcal{V}$ is **closed** if each $- \otimes A$ has a right adjoint $[A, -]$. For symmetric closed $\mathcal{V}$, $\mathcal{V}$ itself is a $\mathcal{V}$-category with hom-objects $[A, B]$, and (if $\mathcal{V}$ is also complete) $\mathcal{V}$-functors $\mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ between small $\mathcal{V}$-categories form a $\mathcal{V}$-category $[\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D}]$, whose hom-object is the **end** $\int_A \mathcal{D}(FA, GA)$. For $[0, \infty]$, $a + c \ge b \iff c \ge b \mathbin{\dot-} a$, where $b \mathbin{\dot-} a = \max(b - a, 0)$ (with $\infty \mathbin{\dot-} \infty = 0$), so $[a, b] = b \mathbin{\dot-} a$ and ends are suprema.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Examples and non-examples">

<Example title="Metric spaces, and asymmetric ones">

Every metric space, and every pseudo- or extended metric space, is a Lawvere metric space. Asymmetric examples arise whenever the cost of going depends on the direction:

- **heights**: on $\R$, $d(x, y) = \max(y - x, 0)$ — climbing from height $x$ to $y$ costs $y - x$, descending is free;
- **travel times** in a hilly town, along one-way streets, or against a river current; the experiment below computes one;
- **the base itself**: $[0, \infty]$ with $d(a, b) = b \mathbin{\dot-} a$ (truncated subtraction) is a Lawvere metric space — $\mathcal{V}$ enriched over itself;
- **subsets**: for subsets $S, T$ of a Lawvere metric space, $d(S, T) = \sup_{s \in S} \inf_{t \in T} d(s, t)$ (the one-sided Hausdorff distance) is a Lawvere metric; it is $0$ when $S \subseteq T$, and symmetrising with $\max$ gives the usual Hausdorff distance.

A preorder becomes a Lawvere metric space with $d(x, y) = 0$ if $x \le y$ and $\infty$ otherwise.

</Example>

<Example title="Rings and linear categories">

An $\cat{Ab}$-enriched category with one object $*$ is an abelian group $R = \mathcal{C}(*, *)$ with a bilinear associative composition and an identity: a **ring**. With several objects it is a "ring with several objects" (Mitchell): for instance the category of finitely generated free $R$-modules, with hom-groups of matrices. Additive categories (<TopicLink id="math.cat.abelian-categories">abelian categories</TopicLink> in particular) are $\cat{Ab}$-enriched by definition. Over a field $k$, one-object $\cat{Vect}_k$-categories are $k$-algebras, and the path category of a quiver, made $k$-linear, has the path algebra as its total algebra (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.path-algebras">path algebras</TopicLink>).

</Example>

<Example title="Strict 2-categories">

A $\cat{Cat}$-enriched category has, for objects $A, B$, a hom-*category* $\mathcal{C}(A, B)$ whose objects are **1-cells** $f : A \to B$ and whose morphisms are **2-cells** $\sigma : f \Rightarrow g$; composition is a functor $\mathcal{C}(B, C) \times \mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{C}(A, C)$. The prime example is $\cat{Cat}$ itself (categories, functors, natural transformations). Weakening the associativity and unit *equations* to coherent *isomorphisms* gives bicategories (Bénabou 1967), the first step towards <TopicLink id="math.cat.higher-categories">higher categories</TopicLink>.

</Example>

<Example title="Spaces of maps and chain complexes">

Categories enriched in topological spaces (or simplicial sets) have **mapping spaces** with continuous (simplicial) composition: compactly generated spaces with $\mathrm{Map}(X, Y)$, or simplicial sets with $\underline{\mathrm{Hom}}(X, Y)$. Categories enriched in chain complexes are **dg-categories**: chain complexes of modules with the Hom complex, whose differential is $d f = d_Y f - (-1)^{|f|} f d_X$.

</Example>

<Example title="A non-example: structure that composition ignores">

Let $\mathcal{C}$ have abelian groups as objects and **all functions** as morphisms. Each hom-set $\mathcal{C}(A, B) = B^A$ is an abelian group under pointwise addition, and $(f + g) \circ h = f \circ h + g \circ h$. But $h \circ (f + g) \neq h \circ f + h \circ g$ when $h$ is not additive (take $h(x) = x^2$ on $\Z$, as a function $\Z \to \Z$). Composition is not bilinear, hence not a morphism $\mathcal{C}(B, C) \otimes \mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{C}(A, C)$ of $\cat{Ab}$, so this is **not** an $\cat{Ab}$-enriched category, despite having an abelian group of arrows between any two objects.

</Example>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Theorems">

<Theorem name="Proposition 1 (Set-enriched categories)">

Categories enriched in $(\cat{Set}, \times, 1)$ are exactly locally small categories.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

A morphism $1 \to \mathcal{C}(A, A)$ is an element $\mathrm{id}_A$; a function $\mathcal{C}(B, C) \times \mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{C}(A, C)$ is a composition law. Evaluating the unit and associativity diagrams of $\cat{Set}$ on elements $(g, f)$, $(h, g, f)$ gives $\mathrm{id} \circ f = f = f \circ \mathrm{id}$ and $h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$. Conversely these equations make the diagrams commute. (Mathlib: `enrichedCategoryTypeEquivCategory`.)

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Proposition 2 (preorders)">

Categories enriched in truth values $(\{\bot \le \top\}, \wedge, \top)$ are exactly preordered sets.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $x \le y$ for "$\mathcal{C}(x, y) = \top$". A morphism $\top \to \mathcal{C}(x, x)$ exists iff $x \le x$; a morphism $\mathcal{C}(y, z) \wedge \mathcal{C}(x, y) \to \mathcal{C}(x, z)$ exists iff $(x \le y \text{ and } y \le z) \Rightarrow x \le z$. Since the truth-value category has at most one morphism between two objects, the associativity and unit diagrams commute automatically. So the data are a reflexive, transitive relation, and nothing else.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (Lawvere 1973)">

Let $\mathcal{V} = ([0, \infty], \ge, +, 0)$, the poset $[0, \infty]$ ordered by $\ge$ (so there is an arrow $a \to b$ iff $a \ge b$), with $\otimes = +$ and unit $0$. Then:

1. $\mathcal{V}$-categories are exactly Lawvere metric spaces;
2. $\mathcal{V}$-functors $X \to Y$ are exactly the **non-expansive** maps, $d(fx, fx') \le d(x, x')$;
3. a $\mathcal{V}$-natural transformation $f \Rightarrow g$ exists (and is unique) iff $d(fx, gx) = 0$ for all $x$;
4. the $\mathcal{V}$-category of $\mathcal{V}$-functors $X \to Y$ has hom-objects $d(f, g) = \sup_x d(fx, gx)$, a Lawvere metric.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

$\mathcal{V}$ is a monoidal category: $+$ is monotone, so it is a functor, and associativity and unit laws of $+$ give the (identity) associator and unitors; coherence is automatic because $\mathcal{V}$ is a preorder.

(1) An identity $0 \to X(x, x)$ is the inequality $0 \ge d(x, x)$, i.e. $d(x, x) = 0$. A composition $X(y, z) + X(x, y) \to X(x, z)$ is the inequality $d(y, z) + d(x, y) \ge d(x, z)$. Since $\mathcal{V}$ is a preorder, the associativity and unit axioms hold automatically.

(2) $F_{x,x'} : X(x, x') \to Y(fx, fx')$ is the inequality $d(x, x') \ge d(fx, fx')$; the functor axioms are equations between parallel morphisms of a preorder, so they hold.

(3) A component $\theta_x : 0 \to Y(fx, gx)$ is the inequality $d(fx, gx) \le 0$; naturality is again automatic.

(4) For each $x$, $d(fx, hx) \le d(fx, gx) + d(gx, hx) \le \sup d(f, g) + \sup d(g, h)$; take the supremum over $x$. And $d(f, f) = \sup_x 0 = 0$. This supremum is the end $\int_x Y(fx, gx)$, since limits in $\mathcal{V}$ (ordered by $\ge$) are suprema.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (enriched Yoneda embedding for Lawvere metric spaces)">

For every Lawvere metric space $X$ and all $x, y \in X$,

$$
d(x, y) = \sup_{z \in X} \big( d(z, y) \mathbin{\dot-} d(z, x) \big).
$$

Hence $x \mapsto d(-, x)$ is an isometric embedding of $X$ into the Lawvere metric space of functions $X \to [0, \infty]$ with the distance $\sup_z \big(\psi(z) \mathbin{\dot-} \varphi(z)\big)$ from $\varphi$ to $\psi$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

For every $z$, the triangle inequality $d(z, y) \le d(z, x) + d(x, y)$ is equivalent, by the adjunction $a + c \ge b \iff c \ge b \mathbin{\dot-} a$, to $d(z, y) \mathbin{\dot-} d(z, x) \le d(x, y)$. So the supremum is at most $d(x, y)$, and $z = x$ attains it: $d(x, y) \mathbin{\dot-} d(x, x) = d(x, y) \mathbin{\dot-} 0 = d(x, y)$.

</Proof>

This is the Yoneda lemma in miniature: "an object is determined by the arrows into it", with "arrows into $x$" replaced by "distances to $x$". For symmetric metric spaces it is the classical Kuratowski–Fréchet embedding into a space of functions.

<Theorem name="Proposition 5 (one-object Ab-categories are rings)">

$\cat{Ab}$-enriched categories with one object correspond exactly to rings.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

With one object $*$, the data are an abelian group $R = \mathcal{C}(*, *)$, a homomorphism $R \otimes R \to R$ — equivalently a biadditive map $R \times R \to R$, a multiplication satisfying both distributive laws — and a homomorphism $\Z \to R$, determined by the element $1$. Associativity and unit diagrams, evaluated on pure tensors, are associativity of multiplication and $1 \cdot r = r = r \cdot 1$. Conversely a ring gives this data.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Proposition 6 (Cat-enriched categories and the interchange law)">

In a $\cat{Cat}$-enriched category, horizontal and vertical composition of 2-cells satisfy the interchange law $(\tau' \cdot \tau) \ast (\sigma' \cdot \sigma) = (\tau' \ast \sigma') \cdot (\tau \ast \sigma)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Vertical composition $\cdot$ is composition inside the hom-categories, and horizontal composition $\ast$ is the action of the composition functor $M : \mathcal{C}(B, C) \times \mathcal{C}(A, B) \to \mathcal{C}(A, C)$ on morphisms: $\tau \ast \sigma = M(\tau, \sigma)$. In the product category, $(\tau', \sigma') \cdot (\tau, \sigma) = (\tau' \cdot \tau, \sigma' \cdot \sigma)$. Functoriality of $M$ gives $M(\tau' \cdot \tau, \sigma' \cdot \sigma) = M(\tau', \sigma') \cdot M(\tau, \sigma)$, which is the interchange law.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="computation" kind="computation" title="Shortest paths: free enriched categories">

A directed graph freely generates a category, whose morphisms are paths. A directed graph with **lengths** freely generates a Lawvere metric space, whose distance is the length of the **shortest** path: composition in $[0, \infty]$ is $+$, and "the best of all paths" is the infimum, so composing hom-matrices is **min-plus (tropical) matrix multiplication**, and a Lawvere metric is precisely a matrix $D$ with zero diagonal and $D \otimes D = D$. The experiment computes walking times in a small hill town, where uphill paths are slower, checks every axiom on every triple, runs the same algorithm with $(\vee, \wedge)$ to get the free preorder (reachability), and checks that the one-sided Hausdorff distance between sets of places is again a Lawvere metric.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Enrichment means putting some extra structure on each hom-set."** Composition and identities must be morphisms *of $\mathcal{V}$*. Abelian groups with all functions between them have an abelian group of arrows between any two objects, but composition is not bilinear, so this is not an $\cat{Ab}$-category. Likewise a topology on hom-sets is not a $\cat{Top}$-enrichment unless composition is continuous.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Lawvere metric spaces are symmetric."** Nothing in the definition of a $[0, \infty]$-category forces $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$: the hom-object from $x$ to $y$ and the one from $y$ to $x$ are unrelated, just as $\mathcal{C}(A, B)$ and $\mathcal{C}(B, A)$ are in an ordinary category. The uphill distance $d(0, 1) = 1$, $d(1, 0) = 0$ is an example. Lawvere also drops "$d(x, y) = 0 \Rightarrow x = y$" (like a preorder versus a partial order) and allows $d = \infty$ (like a category with empty hom-sets).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The underlying category remembers the enriched category."** $\mathcal{C}_0(A, B) = \mathcal{V}(I, \mathcal{C}(A, B))$ can lose almost everything: for a Lawvere metric space it only records which distances are $0$. Different $\mathcal{V}$-categories can have the same underlying category, and enriched notions (limits, functor categories, Yoneda) must be formed in $\mathcal{V}$, not in $\mathcal{C}_0$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Enrichment needs a concrete $\mathcal{V}$ with underlying sets."** The definition only uses morphisms of $\mathcal{V}$ and its tensor product. In $[0, \infty]$ or truth values there are no elements of hom-objects to speak of — which is why Mathlib's `EnrichedCategory` makes no concreteness assumption.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Viewpoints">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Asymmetric distances and Hausdorff distance">

Asymmetric distances are natural in geometry: Finsler metrics in which the length of a velocity vector $v$ differs from that of $-v$ (Randers metrics, which model travel time with a wind or current — Zermelo's navigation problem), and directed path metrics on graphs. Lawvere's framework treats them uniformly, with symmetry as an optional extra property (a "symmetric" $\mathcal{V}$-category). Constructions on $\mathcal{V}$-categories give familiar geometric objects: the one-sided Hausdorff distance $\sup_{s \in S} \inf_{t \in T} d(s, t)$ between subsets is the hom-object of a power-set construction, and its symmetrisation is the Hausdorff distance used to compare shapes and compact sets (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.point-set-topology">metric spaces</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Completeness is a Yoneda-type property">

Lawvere (1973) showed that **Cauchy completeness** of a metric space is an enriched-categorical property: a symmetric Lawvere metric space is Cauchy complete (every Cauchy sequence converges) if and only if every pair of adjoint $[0, \infty]$-bimodules (distributors) into it is representable by a point — and the Cauchy completion is obtained from the Yoneda embedding of Theorem 4 by adding the "limits" of such bimodules. The same notion of Cauchy completion, applied to $\cat{Ab}$-categories, gives the idempotent-split additive closure of a ring ("Karoubi envelope"): a single categorical idea with an analytic and an algebraic face. The Yoneda isometry itself is the categorical form of the embedding of a metric space into a space of bounded functions with the supremum norm (see <TopicLink id="math.analysis.real-analysis">real analysis</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.analysis.functional-analysis">functional analysis</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Mapping spaces and homotopy theory">

A category enriched in topological spaces or simplicial sets has mapping spaces in place of hom-sets, so homotopies between morphisms are paths in mapping spaces. **Simplicially enriched categories** whose mapping spaces are Kan complexes are one of the standard models of $(\infty, 1)$-categories (Dwyer–Kan localisation; Bergner's model structure, 2007; the homotopy coherent nerve connects them to quasi-categories). Homotopy-coherent algebra — $A_\infty$- and $E_\infty$-structures — is phrased in categories enriched in spaces or chain complexes, and dg-categories (enriched in chain complexes) are the standard enhancement of derived categories (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.infinity-categories">∞-categories</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.geom.homotopy-theory">homotopy theory</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Rings with several objects">

Mitchell's slogan "rings with several objects" (1972) says that $\cat{Ab}$-categories are to rings what categories are to monoids. Many ring-theoretic constructions generalise: modules over an $\cat{Ab}$-category $\mathcal{R}$ are additive functors $\mathcal{R} \to \cat{Ab}$; Morita equivalence becomes equivalence of Cauchy completions; and a $k$-linear category with finitely many objects is the same as a $k$-algebra with a chosen complete set of orthogonal idempotents ($A = \bigoplus_{x,y} \mathcal{R}(x, y)$). Representations of quivers are $k$-linear functors out of the free $k$-linear category on the quiver (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.modules">modules</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.algebra.representation-theory">representation theory</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="logic" title="Generalised truth values">

The title of Lawvere's paper — "Metric spaces, generalized logic, and closed categories" — is meant literally: $[0, \infty]$ is a generalised object of truth values in which "$\top$" is $0$, conjunction is $+$, implication is truncated subtraction ($b \mathbin{\dot-} a$ plays the role of $a \Rightarrow b$, by the same adjunction as $a \wedge c \le b \iff c \le (a \Rightarrow b)$), and a Lawvere metric is a "generalised preorder" in which $d(x, y)$ measures *how far* $x \le y$ is from being true. Replacing $[0, \infty]$ by any **quantale** (a complete lattice with an associative, join-preserving multiplication) gives quantale-enriched categories, used in fuzzy and many-valued logics and in the semantics of programs with quantitative information.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="Tropical algebra and shortest paths">

Over the "tropical" semiring $([0, \infty], \min, +)$, matrix multiplication computes best routes, and the shortest-path distance of a weighted graph is the closure $D = I \oplus W \oplus W^{2} \oplus \cdots$, computable by repeated squaring or by the Floyd–Warshall algorithm in $O(n^3)$ operations. In enriched language, this is the free $[0, \infty]$-category on a weighted graph, and the Boolean semiring $(\{0, 1\}, \vee, \wedge)$ gives transitive closure — the free preorder — by the same algorithm. Path problems over other semirings (widest paths with $(\max, \min)$, reliability with $(\max, \times)$) are free categories enriched in other bases.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Samuel Eilenberg** and **G. Max Kelly** set out the theory of closed categories and enriched ("relative") categories at the 1965 La Jolla conference (published 1966); **Jean Bénabou** introduced related ideas in Paris at the same time, and bicategories in 1967. Strict 2-categories go back to **Charles Ehresmann**'s work on double categories in the early 1960s.
- **F. William Lawvere**'s "Metric spaces, generalized logic, and closed categories" (*Rendiconti del Seminario Matematico e Fisico di Milano*, 1973) recognised metric spaces as enriched categories.
- **Barry Mitchell**'s "Rings with several objects" (1972) developed $\cat{Ab}$-categories as generalised rings. **Kelly**'s *Basic Concepts of Enriched Category Theory* (1982), written in Sydney, became the standard reference.
- Enrichment in chain complexes (dg-categories) appears in Kelly's work of the mid-1960s and became central as an enhancement of triangulated categories through **Alexei Bondal** and **Mikhail Kapranov** (1990); enrichment in simplicial sets underlies **William Dwyer** and **Daniel Kan**'s homotopy theory of categories (1980) and **Julia Bergner**'s model structure (2007).

</Section>
