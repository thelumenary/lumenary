<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Socks, shoes and a number trick">

Every school morning in Chennai, Ravi gets dressed in a hurry. Socks on, then shoes on. One morning he is so sleepy that he puts his shoes on first. Then he tries to pull his socks on over his shoes. It does not work!

In the evening he has to undo it all. Does he take his socks off first? He can't — his shoes are in the way. He takes his **shoes** off first, and **then** his socks.

His friend Ana, visiting from São Paulo, has a trick. "Think of a number. Add 5. Double it. Take away 10. Now halve it. You have got… your number back!" Ravi tries it with 7: $7 + 5 = 12$, doubled is 24, take away 10 is 14, halved is 7. It works! He tries 30, then 100. It works every time. How?

Both puzzles are about **doing one thing after another**: which order we do things in, and how to undo them.

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Chains of actions">

An **action** changes something: "add 5" changes 3 into 8; "turn a quarter turn" changes the direction of an arrow; "put on shoes" changes your feet. When we do one action and **then** another, we make a **chain**:

$$
3 \xrightarrow{\ \text{add } 5\ } 8 \xrightarrow{\ \text{double}\ } 16.
$$

The second action works on the **result** of the first. Now swap the order:

$$
3 \xrightarrow{\ \text{double}\ } 6 \xrightarrow{\ \text{add } 5\ } 11.
$$

Same two actions, different order, different answer!

Pictures like these, with dots for things and arrows for actions, are called **arrow pictures**. Here is a bigger one. The straight arrows mean "add 1" and the curved arrows mean "double".

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Big ideas">

<Definition term="chain">

**Chaining** two actions means doing the first action and then doing the second action to the result. We say "*first*, then *second*": "add 5, then double".

</Definition>

<Definition term="undo">

An action **undoes** another action if doing the other action and then this one puts everything back exactly as it was. "Subtract 5" undoes "add 5"; "halve" undoes "double"; "take off shoes" undoes "put on shoes".

</Definition>

<Definition term="doing nothing">

**Doing nothing** is an action too: it leaves everything exactly as it was. A chain can have the same effect as doing nothing — like "add 5, then subtract 5", or four quarter turns.

</Definition>

<Definition term="arrow picture">

An **arrow picture** shows things as dots and actions (or relationships) as arrows from one dot to another. A **path** follows arrows one after another, always starting where the last arrow ended.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Example title="Undoing a chain">

Ravi starts with the number 3 and does "add 5, then double": $3 \to 8 \to 16$. To get back, he must undo the **last** step first:

- undo "double" by halving: $16 \to 8$;
- then undo "add 5" by subtracting 5: $8 \to 3$. Back at the start!

What if he undoes the steps in the **same** order? Subtract 5 first: $16 \to 11$. Then halve: $11 \to 5\tfrac{1}{2}$. That is not 3. Undoing in the wrong order does not work — just like taking off socks before shoes.

</Example>

<Example title="Four quarter turns">

An arrow on a card points **up**. A quarter turn clockwise makes it point **right**, then **down**, then **left**, and the fourth quarter turn brings it back to **up**. So four quarter turns have the same effect as doing nothing. That also means a quarter turn can be undone by **three more** quarter turns (if you are only allowed to turn clockwise).

</Example>

<Section id="why" kind="proof" title="Why it works">

<Theorem name="Add 5 then double, or double then add 5">

Whatever number you start with, "add 5, then double" always gives exactly 5 more than "double, then add 5". So the two chains never give the same answer.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Picture the start number as a row of counters, and add 5 more counters. Doubling makes **two** copies of everything: two copies of the start number and **two** copies of the 5 — that is 10 extra counters. The other order doubles the start number first and then adds just **5** extra counters. So the first chain always has $10 - 5 = 5$ more.

</Proof>

This also explains Ana's trick. "Add 5, then double" gives double your number plus 10. "Take away 10" removes the 10, leaving double your number. "Halve" undoes the doubling, and you are back where you started.

<Theorem name="The socks-and-shoes rule">

To undo a chain "do $A$, then do $B$", first undo $B$, then undo $A$. In general, a chain of actions is undone by undoing its steps in **reverse order**.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

After doing $A$ and then $B$, the last thing that happened was $B$. Undoing $B$ puts everything back as it was just before $B$ — that is, just after $A$. Now undoing $A$ puts everything back as it was before $A$: the very start.

For a longer chain, use the same idea again and again: undo the last step to get back to just before it, then undo the step before that, and so on, until you reach the start.

</Proof>

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why doesn't the original order work? When Ravi tries to "undo add 5" first, the 5 has already been doubled into 10 — the chain has moved on. You can only undo the step you're standing right after.

</Narrator>

<Theorem name="Four quarter turns">

Four quarter turns, one after another, have the same effect as doing nothing.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

A quarter turn is a quarter of a whole turn. Four quarters make one whole turn, and after a whole turn everything faces exactly the way it did before.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon only plays with statements that are exactly true or exactly false. Decide, and give a reason.

1. "Starting at 3, *add 5, then double* gives 16." — **True.** $3 \to 8 \to 16$.
2. "*Add 5, then double* and *double, then add 5* give the same answer for some starting number." — **False.** The first always gives 5 more.
3. "*Add 3, then add 4* is the same as *add 4, then add 3*." — **True**, for every starting number: both add 7.
4. "To undo *socks, then shoes*, take off the socks first." — **False.** Undo the last step first: shoes off, then socks.
5. "Four quarter turns have the same effect as doing nothing." — **True.** Four quarters make a whole turn.

<Narrator who="gideon">

Watch the little words. Statement 2 is about *some* starting number, and statement 3 is about *every* starting number. Trying a few numbers cannot show that statement 3 holds for every number, or that statement 2 fails for every number: there are infinitely many. A reason can: "add 5, then double" always gives 5 more, and "add 3, then add 4" always adds 7.

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"The order never matters."** Sometimes it doesn't (two additions, hat and gloves), but very often it does: "add 5, then double" and "double, then add 5" never agree, and shoes cannot go on before socks. Always check.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"To undo a chain, undo the steps in the same order."** Undo the **last** step first. To undo "add 5, then double", halve first and then subtract 5. Undoing in the original order gives $5\tfrac{1}{2}$ instead of 3.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If two actions agree once, they are the same."** "Add 1" and "double" both take 1 to 2, but they take 3 to different numbers. To be the same action, they must agree on **every** starting thing.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Doing an action again always undoes it."** Flipping a card twice brings it back, but adding 5 twice adds 10, and two quarter turns make a half turn. Each action needs its own undo.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="The geometer: moves of a square">

A square tile can be put back into its square hole in exactly **8** ways: by doing nothing, by a quarter, half or three-quarter turn, or by flipping it over along one of its 4 lines of symmetry. Doing one of these moves and then another always gives one of the 8 again. Some pairs can be swapped (two turns), others cannot (a quarter turn and a flip, as with the L-shaped tile). Geometers study shapes through exactly these chains of moves — see <TopicLink id="math.geom.symmetry-transformations">Symmetry: slides, turns and flips</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="The algebraist: socks and shoes everywhere">

Algebraists collect actions that can be undone, together with the rule for chaining them, into a structure called a **group**. In every group the socks-and-shoes rule holds: the undo of "$a$, then $b$" is "undo $b$, then undo $a$". The same rule turns up when you solve an equation like $3 \times \square + 1 = 22$ — first subtract 1, then divide by 3 (see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.expressions-and-unknowns">Expressions, unknowns and rules</TopicLink>) — and when you solve a Rubik's Cube backwards.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="Where this leads: arrows that compose">

Arrow pictures are the starting point of a whole branch of mathematics. Three simple facts do a lot of work:

- **Arrows chain**: an arrow from $A$ to $B$ and an arrow from $B$ to $C$ make a path from $A$ to $C$.
- **Doing nothing** is an arrow from each thing to itself, and adding it to a path changes nothing.
- For a chain of three arrows, it does not matter which two you join first.

Pictures where every path between the same two dots gives the same result — like the square for "add 3" and "add 4" — are called **commutative diagrams**. In <TopicLink id="math.cat.chaining-functions">Function machines in sequence</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.cat.composition">Composition</TopicLink> you will write chains as $g \circ f$ and prove these facts, and much later they become the definition of a **category**.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Actions that can be undone.** In the 1830s the young French mathematician Évariste Galois studied ways of rearranging the solutions of equations, and how these rearrangements combine one after another. His ideas grew into the theory of groups.
- **Groups in general.** In 1854 the English mathematician Arthur Cayley described groups as collections of operations with a rule for combining them, one after the other — exactly our chains.
- **A famous puzzle.** In 1974 Ernő Rubik, a lecturer in design in Budapest, in Hungary, invented his cube. Solving it means finding a chain of turns; undoing a chain of turns means doing the reverse turns in reverse order.
- **Arrows.** In 1945 Samuel Eilenberg (born in Poland) and Saunders Mac Lane (from the United States) introduced category theory, a part of mathematics built on arrows that can be chained.

</Section>
