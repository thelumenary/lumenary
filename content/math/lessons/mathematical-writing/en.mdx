<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Who reads a proof?">

In June 1993 Andrew Wiles announced a proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. The manuscript, some two hundred pages long, went to referees, who read it line by line. In September one of them, Nick Katz, asked a question about one step that Wiles could not answer: there was a gap. A year later Wiles, working with his former student Richard Taylor, found a way around it, and the corrected proof was published in 1995. The episode is often told as a story of genius; it is equally a story of **writing and reading** — of a proof set out clearly enough for a careful reader to find the one place where it did not hold.

Every proof has a reader: a referee, a student, a collaborator, your future self, and increasingly a proof assistant. Mathematical writing is the craft of making their job possible. It is not decoration added after the mathematics is done. Choosing what to define, what to name, which hypothesis to state and how to order the steps is part of understanding the mathematics — and writing it down is how mathematicians most often discover that they have not.

This lesson assumes you can already write proofs by the standard methods (see <TopicLink id="math.found.proof-techniques">proof techniques</TopicLink>). It is about writing them **well**.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="The reader's three questions">

A careful reader asks three questions of every sentence:

1. **What does this mean?** Every symbol, word and piece of notation must already have been given a meaning — by a definition, by a stated convention, or by a standard usage that the reader can identify.
2. **Why is this true?** Every claim needs a reason: an earlier step, a hypothesis, a cited result, or a computation the reader can redo.
3. **What is its status?** Is this a hypothesis, a definition, a proved result, a result proved elsewhere, a conjecture, a heuristic, a numerical observation?

Good mathematical writing answers these questions **before** the reader has to ask them. Most of the advice in this lesson is a consequence: define before use, state hypotheses, signpost the structure of proofs, cite precisely, label the status of every claim. The guiding image, from the knowledge graph of this curriculum: *a proof is written for a reader who will check it against the stated hypotheses*.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The vocabulary of mathematical texts">

<Definition term="theorem, lemma, proposition, corollary">

A **theorem** is a principal result of a text; a **proposition** is a result of lesser importance; a **lemma** is an auxiliary result, proved mainly to be used in proving others; a **corollary** is a result that follows quickly from another. All four are claims that the text proves (or cites, with a reference).

</Definition>

<Definition term="conjecture, claim, remark">

A **conjecture** is a precisely stated assertion that is believed but not proved. A **claim** is an intermediate assertion inside a proof, proved on the spot. A **remark** is a comment — context, a generalisation, a warning — that the logic of the text does not depend on.

</Definition>

<Definition term="exposition">

**Exposition** is writing whose purpose is explanation rather than the announcement of new results: textbooks, survey articles, lecture notes, the lessons of this curriculum. Its readers are not checking a new claim but trying to understand known ones, and its standards of honesty are the same: say what is proved, what is cited and what is believed.

</Definition>

**The status of a claim** should never be left to the reader to guess. In this lesson we use four labels: **proved** (a proof is given here), **cited** (a proof exists in an identified reference), **conjectured** (no proof is known), and **evidence** (supported by computation or heuristics, not proved). The Lumenary labels its own content in the same spirit — "formally verified", "computationally tested", "ai-drafted" — and derives the first two from build reports rather than writing them by hand.

**Conventions in definitions.** In a definition, "if" means "if and only if": "an integer is *even* if it is divisible by $2$" defines evenness completely. In theorems, "if" means only "if". Writing "iff" in a theorem, and plain "if" in a definition, is the standard convention; some writers prefer to say "if and only if" everywhere, which is never wrong.

</Section>

<Section id="principles" kind="human" title="Ten principles, with examples">

**1. Define every symbol before you use it — and say what kind of object it is.** Not "$x^2 = 4$ implies $x = 2$", but "if $x$ is a natural number with $x^2 = 4$, then $x = 2$". The type of $x$ is part of the statement: for integers it is false ($x = -2$). "Standard" notation is defined too, at least once: $\N$ contains $0$ in some books and not in others; $\subset$ means "subset" for some authors and "proper subset" for others; $\log$ is base $e$, $10$ or $2$ depending on the field.

**2. State every hypothesis, in the statement.** A theorem should be checkable from its statement alone. "If $a \mid bc$ then $a \mid b$ or $a \mid c$" is false ($6 \mid 4 \cdot 9$); it becomes Euclid's lemma when you add "where $a$ is prime". A hypothesis that appears only in the middle of the proof ("since $f$ is continuous…") is a hypothesis the reader did not know they were granting.

**3. Make the order of quantifiers unmistakable.** "For every $\varepsilon > 0$ and every $x$ there is $\delta > 0$ with …" (continuity) and "for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that for every $x$ …" (uniform continuity) differ only in word order. Prose such as "$|f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon$ for all $x$ when $|x - y| < \delta$ for some $\delta$" is ambiguous. When in doubt, write the quantifiers in front, in order.

**4. Do not start a sentence with a symbol, and separate formulas by words.** "$p$ is odd" after a full stop reads as a continuation; write "The prime $p$ is odd". "Since $n > 2$, $n^2 > 4$" runs two formulas together; write "Since $n > 2$, we have $n^2 > 4$." Logical symbols ($\forall$, $\Rightarrow$, $\therefore$) belong in formulas, not as abbreviations in prose.

**5. Signpost the structure of every proof.** Announce the method and the plan: "We argue by contradiction." "We proceed by induction on $n$." "It suffices to show that …" "There are two cases." Name intermediate claims ("Claim 1") and say when each is finished. A reader who knows where the proof is going can check each step against its purpose.

**6. Give reasons, not reassurances.** "Clearly", "obviously" and "it is easy to see" assert that a step is easy without making it so; if the step is easy, a short reason costs little, and if it is not, the word hides a gap. Keep "clearly" for steps that really are immediate, and even then prefer the reason.

**7. Cite precisely.** "By a theorem of Dirichlet" is not enough; write "by Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions (Apostol, *Introduction to Analytic Number Theory*, 1976, Chapter 7)", or better, give the theorem number in the edition you used. The reader must be able to find the result and check that its hypotheses hold in your situation.

**8. Use one symbol for one thing, and one name for one idea.** Do not let $n$ be a natural number on one page and the dimension on the next; do not call the same object a "map", a "function" and a "transformation" in successive paragraphs, which suggests three different things. Choose suggestive notation ($\varepsilon$ small, $i, j$ indices, $G$ a group) and keep it.

**9. Follow definitions with examples and non-examples.** After defining a *group*, show $\Z$ under addition and say why $\N$ is not one. A non-example that just fails the definition shows which clause matters.

**10. Label the status of every claim.** Theorem or conjecture? Proved here, or cited? Proved for all $n$, or checked numerically for $n \le 10^6$? A theorem that depends on an unproved conjecture is a **conditional** theorem and must say so ("Assuming the generalised Riemann hypothesis, …").

**A short proof is not always a clear one.** Compression removes the signposts. The ideal is the shortest proof in which every step can be checked by the intended reader — which for a textbook is longer than for a research paper, and longer still for a proof assistant.

</Section>

<Section id="visualization" kind="visualization" title="Explore: 'if', 'only if' and the converse">

Much ambiguity in mathematical prose comes from conditionals. "$P$ only if $Q$" means $P \to Q$ ($Q$ is **necessary** for $P$); "$P$ if $Q$" means $Q \to P$ ($Q$ is **sufficient** for $P$). Compare a statement with its converse:

Then try `~q -> ~p` (the contrapositive, equivalent to `p -> q`) and `~p -> ~q` (the inverse, equivalent to the converse). A proof of "$P$ if and only if $Q$" must establish both directions, and good writing labels them: "($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $P$ … ($\Leftarrow$) Conversely, suppose $Q$ …".

<Section id="rewrites" kind="proof" title="Two proofs, before and after">

### Euclid's theorem, and a famous misstatement

Here is a version of Euclid's proof that appears in many places:

> *There are infinitely many primes. For suppose there were finitely many, $p_1, \dots, p_k$. Then $N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$ is prime, since it is not divisible by any $p_i$, contradicting the assumption.*

The conclusion is right but a sentence is wrong. "$N$ is prime" does **not** follow: $N$ is not divisible by any $p_i$, but it might be divisible by primes not in the list — except that, under the assumption, there are none. In the proof by contradiction the claim is harmless; read as a standalone fact ("the product of the first $k$ primes plus one is prime"), it is false:

$$
2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 + 1 = 30031 = 59 \cdot 509.
$$

Many students remember the false standalone version. A careful writer states exactly what is used, and can avoid contradiction altogether:

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (Euclid)">

For every natural number $n$ there is a prime $p > n$. In particular there are infinitely many primes.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Let $N = n! + 1$. Since $N \ge 2$, it has a smallest divisor $p > 1$, and $p$ is prime (any proper divisor of $p$ would be a smaller divisor of $N$). Suppose $p \le n$. Then $p$ divides $n!$, and $p$ divides $N$, so $p$ divides $N - n! = 1$, which is impossible. Therefore $p > n$.

</Proof>

The improvements are worth naming: the statement says exactly what is proved, for every $n$; $N$ and $p$ are defined before use; the one fact used about $p$ (a smallest divisor greater than $1$ is prime) is justified; and nothing false is asserted along the way.

### A divisibility proof

The draft below is correct in outline, but a reader would have to repair it.

> *Theorem. $n^2 - 1$ is divisible by $24$ for $p > 3$ prime. Proof. Clearly $p^2 - 1 = (p-1)(p+1)$. $p$ is odd so both factors are even, and one is divisible by $4$. One of $p - 1, p, p + 1$ is a multiple of $3$, so we are done.*

Problems: the statement uses $n$ where it means $p$; two sentences begin with symbols; "clearly" decorates an identity that needs no comment; "one is divisible by $4$" is stated without reason; the key point — that the multiple of $3$ cannot be $p$ — is omitted; and the final combination of the factors $8$ and $3$ is left to the reader.

<Theorem name="Theorem 2">

If $p > 3$ is prime, then $24$ divides $p^2 - 1$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Write $p^2 - 1 = (p - 1)(p + 1)$.

*Divisibility by $8$.* Since $p > 3$ is prime, $p$ is odd, so $p - 1$ and $p + 1$ are consecutive even numbers. Of two consecutive even numbers, one is divisible by $4$. Hence the product $(p - 1)(p + 1)$ is divisible by $2 \cdot 4 = 8$.

*Divisibility by $3$.* Of the three consecutive integers $p - 1, p, p + 1$, exactly one is divisible by $3$. It is not $p$, because $p$ is a prime greater than $3$. So $3$ divides $p - 1$ or $p + 1$, and hence divides $p^2 - 1$.

*Conclusion.* Since $\gcd(3, 8) = 1$ and both $3$ and $8$ divide $p^2 - 1$, so does $3 \cdot 8 = 24$.

</Proof>

The revision is longer, and better: each paragraph has a stated purpose, every step has a reason, and every hypothesis ("prime", "greater than $3$") is visibly used. A good test of a proof is to ask where each hypothesis is used; a hypothesis that is never used signals either a stronger theorem or a gap.

</Section>

<Section id="latex" kind="human" title="Typesetting with LaTeX">

Almost all mathematics is now typeset with Leslie Lamport's LaTeX, built on Donald Knuth's TeX. A minimal article with theorem environments:

```latex
\documentclass{article}
\usepackage{amsmath, amssymb, amsthm}

\newtheorem{theorem}{Theorem}[section]
\newtheorem{lemma}[theorem]{Lemma}
\theoremstyle{definition}
\newtheorem{definition}[theorem]{Definition}
\theoremstyle{remark}
\newtheorem{remark}[theorem]{Remark}

\newcommand{\N}{\mathbb{N}}               % one definition, used everywhere
\newcommand{\abs}[1]{\left\lvert #1 \right\rvert}

\begin{document}
\section{Primes}
\begin{definition}\label{def:prime}
A natural number $p \ge 2$ is \emph{prime} if its only positive
divisors are $1$ and $p$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Euclid]\label{thm:euclid}
For every $n \in \N$ there is a prime $p > n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $N = n! + 1$ \dots
\end{proof}

By Theorem~\ref{thm:euclid}, \dots
\end{document}
```

Habits that make LaTeX serve the reader:

- **Semantic macros.** Define `\N`, `\abs{x}`, `\norm{v}` once with `\newcommand`. Notation stays consistent, and changing it later is one edit.
- **Labels, not numbers.** Refer with `\label`, `\ref` and `\eqref` (or `\cref` from the `cleveref` package); never type "Theorem 3.2" by hand. Use `~` before references so that they do not break across lines.
- **Displays.** Use `\[ … \]` or `equation` for a single displayed formula and `align` for chains of equalities aligned at `=`; number only the equations you refer to. Punctuate displayed formulas as part of the sentence.
- **Words in formulas.** Write `\text{ for all } x`, `\operatorname{rank}` and `\sin`, not `for all x` or `rank` in italic maths.
- **Bibliography.** Use BibTeX or BibLaTeX with full references (authors, title, journal or publisher, year), so that every citation is identifiable.

LaTeX checks syntax, not sense: a document can compile perfectly and still use an undefined symbol or cite a result whose hypotheses fail. The computation below shows what a mechanical checker can and cannot catch.

<Section id="exposition" kind="human" title="Writing an expository note">

Suppose you are asked to write a three-page note explaining a theorem to fellow students. A structure that works:

1. **What and why.** State the main result early, informally first and then precisely, and say why it matters.
2. **Setting.** Definitions and notation, each with an example. Say which conventions you use ($0 \in \N$ or not).
3. **Results.** Lemmas building to the theorem, each labelled **proved here**, **cited** (with a precise reference) or **conjectured**.
4. **Proofs.** Signposted, with the main idea stated before the details. If a proof is omitted, say so and cite one.
5. **Examples and limits.** An example where the theorem applies; an example showing a hypothesis cannot be dropped.
6. **Further reading.** Where the reader goes next.

A status table at the start or end is a courtesy that formal projects make compulsory. For instance, for a note on the distribution of primes:

| Statement | Status |
| --- | --- |
| There are infinitely many primes (Euclid) | proved here |
| $\pi(x) \sim x / \ln x$ (the prime number theorem) | cited: Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, 1896 |
| Every even $n > 2$ is a sum of two primes (Goldbach) | conjectured; checked by computer far beyond $10^{18}$ |
| $\lvert \pi(x) - \mathrm{li}(x) \rvert = O(\sqrt x \log x)$ | conditional: equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis |

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"A shorter proof is always a clearer one."** Brevity is a virtue only when nothing the reader needs is lost. Omitting a reason, a case or a signpost shortens a proof and lengthens the reading. The right length depends on the reader: a research paper may say "by a standard compactness argument"; a textbook should give the argument.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Standard notation does not need defining."** Notation varies between fields, countries and decades: whether $0 \in \N$, what $\subset$ means, the base of $\log$, whether $(a, b)$ is an interval, a pair or a gcd. Define it once, briefly — "we write $\N = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$" costs one line and prevents misreadings.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The reader can tell what is proved and what is only believed."** Not if the text does not say. A result that silently depends on a conjecture, a numerical observation stated like a theorem, or a cited result presented as if proved here, all mislead. State the status of every claim.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Mathematical writing should hide how the result was found."** A finished proof need not narrate every false start, but explaining the idea — why this auxiliary function, why induct on this quantity — is often what makes the proof checkable and memorable. Polished proofs that conceal the idea are a known complaint (see the analyst's view below).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"If it compiles in LaTeX, or passes a proof assistant, it is well written."** LaTeX checks syntax; a proof assistant checks correctness. Neither checks that the definitions are well chosen, the structure helps the reader, or the important idea is visible.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Many ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="logic" title="Informal proofs and formal derivations">

Logic offers a precise ideal behind informal proof. In **natural deduction** (Gentzen, 1935) a proof is a tree of inferences, and the informal phrases of mathematical writing correspond to its rules: "assume $P$ … hence $Q$; therefore $P \Rightarrow Q$" is implication introduction; "let $x$ be arbitrary" is universal introduction, with the side condition that $x$ not appear free in any open assumption — the formal version of "do not reuse a symbol that already means something". By the completeness theorem every valid first-order argument has such a derivation, and an informal proof is good when a competent reader could, in principle, expand it into one. Under the **Curry–Howard correspondence** proofs are programs, which is why proof assistants such as Lean can check them. See <TopicLink id="math.found.first-order-logic">first-order logic</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.found.proof-theory">proof theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="Literate programming and blueprints">

Donald Knuth, who wrote TeX because he was unhappy with the typesetting of his books, also proposed **literate programming** (1984): a program should be written as an essay for human readers, with the code embedded in the explanation, and the compiler-ready version extracted mechanically. TeX itself was written this way. Blueprints for formalisation projects reverse the arrangement — the informal text is primary and links to the formal code — and the Lumenary's lessons, which display Lean declarations extracted from compiled files, follow the same idea. In all three, the computer checks one layer (the code, the proofs) and the writing must make the other layer (the ideas, the structure) understandable. See <TopicLink id="math.comp.formal-proof-lean">formalising mathematics in Lean</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.comp.formalising-published-mathematics">formalising published mathematics</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="When the picture is part of the proof">

Euclid's very first proposition — constructing an equilateral triangle on a given segment — draws two circles and takes "the point where they cut one another". That the circles meet is read off the diagram; nothing in Euclid's postulates guarantees it. Such gaps were closed only when Moritz Pasch (1882) and David Hilbert (*Foundations of Geometry*, 1899) wrote axioms for betweenness and continuity. The classic fallacy that "every triangle is isosceles" works by a diagram in which a point is drawn inside the triangle that in fact lies outside. Diagrams remain indispensable in geometry, topology and category theory, and modern work (for instance on Euclid's diagrammatic reasoning) shows they can be used rigorously; the writer's duty is to say which facts are taken from the picture and why they hold in general. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.euclidean-geometry">Euclidean geometry</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="topology" title="Proofs and refutations">

Imre Lakatos's *Proofs and Refutations* (1976) is a dialogue about Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ for polyhedra. A classroom proves it, then meets counterexamples: a "picture frame" polyhedron with a tunnel ($V - E + F = 0$), a cube with a cubical cavity ($V - E + F = 4$), star polyhedra. Each forces a choice — bar the "monster", or restrict the theorem, or sharpen the definition of polyhedron — and the theorem that survives is a statement about surfaces of a given topological type: $V - E + F = 2 - 2g$ for a closed orientable surface with $g$ handles. Lakatos's point is a lesson in writing: the precise hypotheses of a theorem are often discovered by trying to prove it and reading the proof critically, and a definition is good when it makes a true theorem stateable. See <TopicLink id="math.geom.surfaces-euler-characteristic">surfaces and Euler characteristic</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Quantifiers in the history of analysis">

Augustin-Louis Cauchy's *Cours d'analyse* (1821) contains a "theorem" that a convergent series of continuous functions has a continuous sum. Niels Henrik Abel pointed out in 1826 that Fourier series such as $\sin x - \frac12 \sin 2x + \frac13 \sin 3x - \cdots$ are counterexamples. The repair, found by Philipp Seidel and George Stokes in 1847 and made standard by Karl Weierstrass, was **uniform convergence** — a single swap in the order of the quantifiers "for every $\varepsilon$", "for every $x$" and "there is $N$". The $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ style of writing analysis exists precisely so that such distinctions are visible on the page. Its descendants remain risky: big-$O$ notation writes $f = O(g)$ for a one-way relation, and "for $n$ sufficiently large" hides a quantifier that must not depend on what comes later. See <TopicLink id="math.analysis.real-analysis">real analysis</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Notation that thinks for you">

Algebra is the field where notation most visibly changed what could be thought. Medieval algebra in Arabic and Latin was **rhetorical** — al-Khwārizmī solved quadratic equations in words, with geometric justifications. Abbreviations appear in the work of al-Qalaṣādī (15th century) and others; François Viète (1591) introduced letters for both unknowns and given quantities, so that a single formula could express a whole family of problems; René Descartes (1637) fixed the convention of $x, y, z$ for unknowns and $a, b, c$ for knowns that we still use. Good notation encodes rules: in Leibniz's $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \frac{du}{dx}$ the chain rule looks like cancellation, and in a commutative diagram an equation between composites is a picture. The cost is that notation which suggests a false rule misleads — a reason to define it carefully. See <TopicLink id="math.algebra.algebraic-structures">algebraic structures</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Definitions, postulates, propositions.** Euclid's *Elements* (c. 300 BCE) fixed a format still recognisable in every textbook: definitions and postulates first, then propositions, each stated, proved and concluded ("which was to be demonstrated", later abbreviated QED).
- **Commentary as proof.** In China, Liu Hui's commentary of 263 CE on the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* supplied justifications for the book's procedures. In India, the concise verse of works such as Āryabhaṭa's *Āryabhaṭīya* (499) was accompanied by prose commentaries with explanations and demonstrations (*upapatti*); the Malayalam *Yuktibhāṣā* of Jyeṣṭhadeva (c. 1530) gives detailed proofs of the infinite series of the Kerala school.
- **From words to symbols.** Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī's algebra (c. 820) was written entirely in words. Symbolic notation developed over centuries — Robert Recorde's equals sign (1557), Viète's letters (1591), Descartes's $x, y, z$ (1637), Leibniz's $dx$ and $\int$ (1675), and Euler's $f(x)$, $\Sigma$ and $i$ in the 18th century.
- **Rigour and style.** The 19th-century arithmetisation of analysis made $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ writing standard. From 1935 the Bourbaki group wrote mathematics in a deliberately uniform, definition–theorem–proof style that shaped textbooks for decades. Paul Halmos's essay "How to write mathematics" (1970) and the 1973 AMS collection with Steenrod, Schiffer and Dieudonné made writing advice part of the profession.
- **Typesetting and sharing.** Donald Knuth created TeX between 1977 and 1978, and Leslie Lamport's LaTeX followed in the early 1980s; the arXiv preprint server (1991) made LaTeX the common language of mathematical publication. Since 2020, blueprints linking informal and formal mathematics have become the working documents of large formalisation projects.

<Section id="reading" kind="reading" title="Further reading">

- Paul R. Halmos, "How to write mathematics", *L'Enseignement Mathématique* (1970) — short, opinionated and still the best start.
- Donald E. Knuth, Tracy Larrabee and Paul M. Roberts, *Mathematical Writing* (1989) — notes from Knuth's Stanford course, with many before-and-after examples.
- Nicholas J. Higham, *Handbook of Writing for the Mathematical Sciences* (2nd edition 1998; 3rd edition 2020) — comprehensive and practical, including LaTeX.
- Norman E. Steenrod, Paul R. Halmos, Menahem M. Schiffer and Jean A. Dieudonné, *How to Write Mathematics* (1973).
- Steven G. Krantz, *A Primer of Mathematical Writing* (1997; 2nd edition 2017).
- Leslie Lamport, *LaTeX: A Document Preparation System* (2nd edition 1994).
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery* (1976).
- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It* (1994; 3rd edition 2019) — for the structure of proofs.
