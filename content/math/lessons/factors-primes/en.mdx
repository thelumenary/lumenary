<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Chairs for the assembly">

Mr Mensah's class in Accra is setting out **24** chairs for an assembly. He wants equal rows with no chair left over. They could make $2$ rows of $12$, or $3$ rows of $8$, or $4$ rows of $6$, or even one long row of $24$.

The next week there are **23** chairs. Now nothing works except one long line of $23$, or $23$ rows of one chair each!

Why can $24$ be arranged in so many ways and $23$ in only one? The answer leads to some of the oldest and deepest ideas in mathematics: **factors**, **multiples** and **prime numbers**.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Rectangles of dots">

Every way of arranging chairs in equal rows is a **rectangle** of dots, just like the arrays in <TopicLink id="math.number.whole-number-arithmetic">whole-number arithmetic</TopicLink>. A rectangle with $4$ rows of $6$ shows that $24 = 4 \times 6$. Try different row lengths for $24$ below, and then try $23$:

<NumberGrid mode="factors" n={24} title="How many rectangles can you make?" />

For $24$ you find the rectangles $1 \times 24$, $2 \times 12$, $3 \times 8$ and $4 \times 6$ (and the same ones turned round). For $23$ the only rectangle is a single line. Numbers bigger than $1$ that, like $23$, can only make a line are the **primes**.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Look at the rectangles for $36$: one of them is a square, $6 \times 6$. Which numbers have a square among their rectangles? Can you guess before you check?

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="The words we need">

<Definition term="factor and multiple">

If $a = b \times k$ for some whole number $k$, we say $b$ is a **factor** of $a$, and $a$ is a **multiple** of $b$. For example, $12 = 3 \times 4$, so $3$ is a factor of $12$ and $12$ is a multiple of $3$.

</Definition>

The two words point in opposite directions. The factor fits exactly into the multiple; the multiple is built from copies of the factor. We also say that $a$ is **divisible by** $b$, or that $b$ **divides** $a$.

<Definition term="prime and composite">

A **prime number** is a whole number greater than $1$ whose only factors are $1$ and itself. A **composite number** is a whole number greater than $1$ that is not prime.

</Definition>

The number $1$ is **neither** prime nor composite. It is not greater than $1$, and it has only one factor.

<Definition term="common factors and common multiples">

A **common factor** of two numbers is a factor of both. The biggest one is their **greatest common factor**. A **common multiple** is a multiple of both, and the smallest one above $0$ is their **least common multiple**.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="The factors of 36, in pairs">

Look for factors in pairs, starting from $1$:

$$
36 = 1 \times 36 = 2 \times 18 = 3 \times 12 = 4 \times 9 = 6 \times 6.
$$

$5$ is not a factor, because $36 = 7 \times 5 + 1$. After $6 \times 6$ the pairs only repeat the other way round ($9 \times 4$, $12 \times 3$, …), so we can stop. The factors of $36$ are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$: nine of them. The number is odd because $6$ pairs up with itself.

</Example>

<Example title="Common factors and common multiples">

- Factors of $24$: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24$. Factors of $36$: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$. The common factors are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$, and the greatest common factor is $12$.
- Multiples of $4$: $4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, \dots$ Multiples of $6$: $6, 12, 18, 24, \dots$ The common multiples are $12, 24, 36, \dots$, and the least common multiple is $12$, *not* $4 \times 6 = 24$.

</Example>

<Example title="Prime or not?">

- $2$ is prime. It is the only even prime, because every other even number has the factor $2$.
- $9$ is odd but **not** prime: $9 = 3 \times 3$.
- $51 = 3 \times 17$ and $91 = 7 \times 13$ are composite. They fool many people!
- $97$ is prime. To be sure, we only need to try the primes $2, 3, 5, 7$ (see the proofs below for why we can stop there).

</Example>

<Section id="why" kind="proof" title="Why it all works">

### Finding factors: when to stop

Factors come in pairs $b \times k$. As the first number of the pair grows, the second shrinks. Once the first number passes the second, every pair we find is an old pair turned round. So we can stop when the two numbers meet, in the middle, at the side of a square. For $36$ that is $6 \times 6$. For $24$ we stop after $4 \times 6$, because $5$ is not a factor and $6 \times 4$ is old.

### The smallest factor is always prime

<Theorem name="smallest factor">

For any whole number $n$ greater than $1$, the smallest factor of $n$ that is bigger than $1$ is a prime.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Call this smallest factor $p$. If $p$ were not prime, it would have a factor $q$ with $1 < q < p$. But a factor of a factor of $n$ is also a factor of $n$: if $n = p \times k$ and $p = q \times j$, then $n = q \times (j \times k)$. So $q$ would be a factor of $n$ smaller than $p$, which is impossible. So $p$ is prime.

</Proof>

### Every number is built from primes

<Theorem name="factor trees">

Every whole number greater than $1$ is either a prime or a product of primes.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Grow a factor tree. If the number is prime, stop. If not, split it into two factors, each smaller than the number itself, and do the same with each factor. Every split makes the numbers smaller, and whole numbers cannot get smaller for ever. So the tree must stop growing, and it can only stop at primes. The number is the product of those primes.

</Proof>

There is more: **the primes at the bottom of the tree are always the same**, however you grow it (only their order may change). This is called the **fundamental theorem of arithmetic**. It is true, but it is harder to prove than it looks. You will meet the proof later, in <TopicLink id="math.number.divisibility-gcd">divisibility and unique factorisation</TopicLink>.

<ForGrownUps title="why uniqueness needs a proof">

Imagine a world where only **even** numbers exist. Call an even number an *even-prime* if it cannot be written as a product of two even numbers. Then $2$, $6$, $10$ and $30$ are all even-primes ($6 = 2 \times 3$, but $3$ does not exist in this world). And

$$
60 = 6 \times 10 = 2 \times 30
$$

has **two different** factorisations into even-primes! So uniqueness is a special property of the whole numbers, not something that comes for free. The same thing happens in more serious number systems: among the numbers $a + b\sqrt{-5}$ (with $a$ and $b$ integers), $6 = 2 \times 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$, and none of these four factors can be split further. This puzzle helped start the branch of mathematics called <TopicLink id="math.number.algebraic-number-theory">algebraic number theory</TopicLink>.

</ForGrownUps>

### The divisibility tests

They all rest on one fact: **if a number divides two numbers, it divides their sum**. If $a = d \times j$ and $b = d \times k$, then $a + b = d \times (j + k)$ by the distributive law. In the same way, it divides their difference.

**Tests for $2$, $5$ and $10$: look at the last digit.** Any number is "some tens plus its last digit". For example, $3475 = 347 \times 10 + 5$. The tens part, $347 \times 10$, is always a multiple of $10$, and so also of $2$ and of $5$. So whether the whole number is divisible by $2$, $5$ or $10$ depends only on the last digit. $3475$ ends in $5$, so it is divisible by $5$ but not by $2$ or $10$.

**Tests for $9$ and $3$: add the digits.** The trick is that $10 = 9 + 1$, $100 = 99 + 1$, $1000 = 999 + 1$, and so on. So

$$
572 = 5 \times 100 + 7 \times 10 + 2 = \underbrace{5 \times 99 + 7 \times 9}_{\text{a multiple of } 9} + (5 + 7 + 2).
$$

A number is therefore "a multiple of $9$, plus its digit sum". So the number and its digit sum leave the **same remainder** when divided by $9$, and the number is divisible by $9$ exactly when its digit sum is. Since $9 = 3 \times 3$, a multiple of $9$ is also a multiple of $3$, and the same test works for $3$. For $572$, the digit sum is $14$, which is not divisible by $3$ or $9$, so neither is $572$.

<Narrator who="chloe">

The digit-sum test is not magic: it works *because* we count in tens and $10$ is one more than $9$. It does **not** work for $7$. Margaret found a counterexample below!

</Narrator>

### Why the sieve can stop early

To find the primes up to $100$, we only need to cross out the multiples of $2$, $3$, $5$ and $7$. Any composite number up to $100$ is a product $a \times b$ of two numbers bigger than $1$. They cannot both be bigger than $10$, because then $a \times b$ would be bigger than $10 \times 10 = 100$. So one of them is at most $10$, and its smallest prime factor ($2$, $3$, $5$ or $7$) has already crossed the number out.

### The primes never run out

<Theorem name="Euclid's theorem">

There are infinitely many primes: however far you go, there is always a bigger prime.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose someone hands you a list of primes, say $2, 3, 5$. Multiply them all and add $1$: $2 \times 3 \times 5 + 1 = 31$. This new number leaves remainder $1$ when divided by each prime on the list, so none of them is a factor. But the new number does have a prime factor (its smallest factor bigger than $1$). That prime is **not on the list**. So no list of primes can ever be complete.

</Proof>

The new number need not be prime itself: $2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 \times 11 \times 13 + 1 = 30031 = 59 \times 509$. But its prime factors, $59$ and $509$, are new.

</Section>

<Section id="formal" kind="formal" title="Gideon's precise statements">

<Narrator who="gideon">

"Prime" needs a precise definition, or people argue about $1$. Mine: a whole number greater than $1$ whose only factors are $1$ and itself. Now, true or false?

</Narrator>

1. "$3$ is a factor of $12$." — **True:** $12 = 3 \times 4$. But "$12$ is a factor of $3$" is **false**.
2. "$1$ is a prime number." — **False.** The definition needs a number greater than $1$.
3. "Every odd number greater than $1$ is prime." — **False.** $9 = 3 \times 3$. One counterexample is enough.
4. "$91$ is prime." — **False.** $91 = 7 \times 13$.
5. "There is a prime bigger than one million." — **True.** Euclid's proof works for any list of primes.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"1 is a prime number."** A prime must be greater than $1$ and have exactly two factors, $1$ and itself. The number $1$ has only one factor. Leaving $1$ out also keeps factor trees unique: otherwise $6 = 2 \times 3 = 1 \times 2 \times 3 = 1 \times 1 \times 2 \times 3 = \cdots$

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"All odd numbers are prime."** $9 = 3 \times 3$, $15 = 3 \times 5$ and $21 = 3 \times 7$ are odd and composite. Being odd only tells you that $2$ is not a factor.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"2 can't be prime, because it is even."** $2$ has exactly two factors, $1$ and $2$, so it is prime. It is the *only* even prime.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Mixing up factors and multiples.** The factors of $12$ are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$: they fit *into* $12$. The multiples of $12$ are $12, 24, 36, \dots$: they are *built from* $12$. A number has only a few factors but infinitely many multiples.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The digit-sum test works for every number."** It works for $3$ and $9$ because $10 = 9 + 1$. For $7$ it fails: $14$ is divisible by $7$, but its digit sum $5$ is not.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="Primes are the atoms of arithmetic">

A number theorist thinks of primes as **atoms**: every whole number is built from them by multiplying, in exactly one way. Many questions about primes are easy to ask and very hard to answer. For example: is every even number greater than $2$ the sum of two primes ($4 = 2 + 2$, $10 = 3 + 7$, $100 = 3 + 97$)? This is **Goldbach's conjecture** (1742). It has been checked by computer for enormous numbers, but **nobody has proved it**. It is still an open problem. See <TopicLink id="math.number.elementary-number-theory">number theory</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Factors are rectangles">

A geometer sees the factor pairs of $n$ as the rectangles with area $n$ and whole-number sides. A prime is a number of squares that can only be arranged as a single strip. A perfect square ($36$) is one whose rectangles include a square. Common factors also have a picture: the squares of side $12$ are the biggest squares that tile a $24$ by $36$ rectangle exactly. Euclid's method for finding them is in <TopicLink id="math.number.divisibility-gcd">Euclid's algorithm</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="computation" title="Multiplying is easy, factoring is hard">

A computer can multiply two numbers with hundreds of digits in a blink. But going backwards, finding the prime factors of a huge number, seems to be extremely hard: nobody knows a fast method for ordinary computers. Much of the secret-keeping on the internet, such as the RSA system, depends on this. It is a widely held **belief, not a theorem**, that no fast method exists. See <TopicLink id="math.applied.secret-codes">secret codes</TopicLink> and <TopicLink id="math.applied.cryptography">cryptography</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Factors and multiples as sets">

The factors of $24$ form a set, $F_{24} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24\}$, and so do the factors of $36$. The common factors are exactly the **intersection** (see <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">sets</TopicLink>):

$$
F_{24} \cap F_{36} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}.
$$

In a Venn diagram, the overlap holds the common factors, and the greatest common factor is the biggest number there. The multiples of $4$ and the multiples of $6$ are infinite sets. Their intersection is the set of multiples of $12$. The set of all primes is infinite too, by Euclid's theorem.

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **A puzzle from Africa.** The Ishango bone, found in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo and roughly $20{,}000$ years old, carries groups of notches. One column has groups of $11$, $13$, $17$ and $19$, the primes between $10$ and $20$. Some people have suggested it records primes, but most researchers think this is a coincidence. Nobody knows what the notches meant.
- **Greece.** Euclid's *Elements* (around 300 BCE) defines prime numbers (Book VII). Book IX contains his proof that there are more primes than any given list. Eratosthenes of Cyrene, a scholar at Alexandria in the 3rd century BCE, is credited with the sieve. The method is described by Nicomachus of Gerasa around 100 CE.
- **China.** The *Sunzi Suanjing* (written between the 3rd and 5th centuries CE) asks for a number that leaves remainder $2$ when divided by $3$, remainder $3$ when divided by $5$ and remainder $2$ when divided by $7$. Puzzles like this one, about remainders and multiples, grew into the **Chinese remainder theorem**.
- **Germany.** Carl Friedrich Gauss gave the first complete, careful proof that prime factorisations are unique in his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801).
- **Today.** Volunteers all over the world search for record-breaking primes with their computers. In October 2024 the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search found a prime with more than $41$ million digits, the largest known at that time.
