<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="Patterns that fit onto themselves">

Look at a butterfly: its left wing is the mirror image of its right wing. Look at a snowflake: turn it a little and it looks exactly the same. People all over the world make patterns like this on purpose. In Tamil Nadu in South India, families draw **kolam** patterns of loops and dots at their doorsteps each morning. In Ghana, **Adinkra** symbols are stamped onto cloth. In the Alhambra palace in Granada, Spain, walls are covered with tiles whose patterns repeat in every direction.

What do all these patterns have in common? They can be **moved** — folded over, turned or slid — and still fit exactly onto themselves. That is what mathematicians call **symmetry**.

<Narrator who="chloe">

A square fits onto itself in lots of ways. A rectangle in fewer. A long wiggly line maybe in none at all. Can we count the ways — and understand *why* there are that many?

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Folding and turning tests">

**The folding test.** Draw a shape on paper and cut it out. If you can fold it so that the two halves match exactly, the fold line is a **line of symmetry**. A mirror placed on that line shows the whole shape.

**The turning test.** Lay a piece of tracing paper over the shape, trace it, and hold the tracing down at the centre with the point of a pencil. Turn the tracing slowly, all the way round. Count how many times the tracing fits exactly over the shape — including the very end, when you have turned all the way back to the start.

A square passes the folding test along 4 different lines, and in the turning test it fits 4 times in one full turn. A rectangle that is not a square passes the folding test for only 2 lines. (Try folding a sheet of paper along its diagonal: the corners do not match!)

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Words we need">

<Definition term="line of symmetry">

A **line of symmetry** (or mirror line) divides a shape into two halves that are mirror images of each other: flipping the shape over the line leaves it looking exactly the same.

</Definition>

<Definition term="rotational symmetry">

A shape has **rotational symmetry of order $n$** if exactly $n$ turns within one full turn (counting the full turn itself) leave it looking the same. A shape that only fits after a full turn has order 1 — we say it has no rotational symmetry.

</Definition>

There are three ways to move a shape without changing its size or shape:

<Definition term="slide, turn and flip">

- A **slide** (or **translation**) moves every point the same distance in the same direction — for example, 3 squares right and 1 square up.
- A **turn** (or **rotation**) turns every point about a fixed point, the **centre of turn**, through the same angle — for example, a quarter turn anticlockwise about the point P.
- A **flip** (or **reflection**) reflects every point in a **mirror line**: each point goes to the same distance on the other side of the line.

</Definition>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Counting symmetries">

<Example title="Shapes">

| Shape | Lines of symmetry | Order of rotational symmetry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| square | 4 | 4 |
| rectangle (not a square) | 2 | 2 |
| equilateral triangle | 3 | 3 |
| isosceles triangle (not equilateral) | 1 | 1 |
| parallelogram (not a rectangle or rhombus) | 0 | 2 |
| kite (not a rhombus) | 1 | 1 |
| regular hexagon | 6 | 6 |
| circle | infinitely many | any turn at all fits |

</Example>

<Example title="A surprise: the parallelogram">

A parallelogram has **no** line of symmetry — fold it any way you like, the halves never match. But turn it through half a turn about its centre and it fits exactly onto itself. So a shape can have rotational symmetry without line symmetry.

</Example>

<Example title="Letters">

In simple block capitals, **A** has one (vertical) line of symmetry, **B** has one (horizontal) line, and **H** has two lines and rotational symmetry of order 2. **N**, **S** and **Z** have no line of symmetry but do have rotational symmetry of order 2: turn the page upside down and they look the same. (Letters in fancy fonts may break these rules!)

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Why the rules work">

<Theorem name="Moves keep lengths and angles">

Slides, turns and flips do not change lengths or angles. A shape and its image are exactly the same size and shape.

</Theorem>

Think of the shape drawn on tracing paper. A slide pushes the tracing along; a turn spins it about a pencil point; a flip turns the tracing over onto its other side. The paper is never stretched, squashed or torn, so every length and every angle stays the same. (This is a physical picture, not a proof. In the Lean section below, the computer proves it for every pair of points on squared paper.)

<Theorem name="Flipping twice">

Flipping twice in the same mirror line puts every point back where it started.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

A point that is some distance $d$ on one side of the mirror goes to distance $d$ on the other side. Flipping again sends it distance $d$ back to the first side — exactly where it began. Points on the mirror line do not move at all.

</Proof>

So **every move can be undone**: a slide is undone by the opposite slide (3 right and 1 up is undone by 3 left and 1 down); a turn is undone by turning back through the same angle about the same centre; and a flip is undone by the same flip.

<Theorem name="Two flips in parallel lines make a slide">

Flipping in one mirror line and then in a second, parallel mirror line slides every point at right angles to the mirrors, by **twice** the distance between them.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Measure positions across the mirrors with a number line: the first mirror at 0 and the second at $g$. A point at position $x$ goes to $-x$ in the first mirror. Now it is at distance $g - (-x) = g + x$ from the second mirror, so the second flip sends it to $g + (g + x) = x + 2g$. Every point has moved $2g$: a slide by twice the gap.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Two flips in crossing lines make a turn">

Flipping in one mirror line and then in a second mirror line that crosses it is a **turn about the crossing point**, through **twice** the angle between the mirrors.

</Theorem>

Two examples: mirrors that cross at a right angle ($90^\circ$) give a half turn ($180^\circ$); mirrors that cross at $45^\circ$ give a quarter turn ($90^\circ$). Here is why, as a sketch. The crossing point lies on both mirrors, so it never moves, and each flip keeps every point at the same distance from it: every point stays on its own circle around the crossing point. To see how far round its circle a point goes, measure angles from the first mirror. A point at angle $a$ goes to angle $-a$ in the first mirror, and then, if the second mirror is at angle $m$, to angle $2m + a$. Every point has moved round through the same angle $2m$: that is a turn.

<Theorem name="Symmetries of a regular polygon">

A regular polygon with $n$ sides has exactly $n$ lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order $n$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Number the corners $1, 2, \dots, n$ going round. A symmetry moves corners to corners and keeps neighbours next to each other (it does not change lengths).

- Corner 1 can go to any of the $n$ corners.
- Corner 2, its neighbour, must go to a neighbour of wherever corner 1 went: there are only 2 choices, one on each side.
- Once we know where corners 1 and 2 go, every other corner is fixed too: corner 3 is the other neighbour of corner 2, and so on round the polygon.

So there are at most $n \times 2 = 2n$ symmetries. For a regular polygon all of them really happen. In $n$ of them the corners keep going round the same way: these are the $n$ turns (through $\frac{360^\circ}{n}$, twice that, …, up to the full turn), so the order of rotational symmetry is $n$. In the other $n$ the order of the corners is reversed: these are flips, one for each of $n$ lines of symmetry.

</Proof>

For example, the equilateral triangle has $2 \times 3 = 6$ symmetries: 3 turns ($120^\circ$, $240^\circ$, $360^\circ$) and 3 flips. A rectangle that is not a square is *not* regular — its sides are not all equal — and it has only 4 symmetries: 2 turns and 2 flips. Its diagonals are not lines of symmetry.

</Section>

<Section id="order" kind="human" title="One move after another: does order matter?">

Doing one move and then another is again a move. Two slides make a slide; two turns about the same centre make a turn; and, as we just saw, two flips make a slide or a turn.

But the **order** can matter. Cut out a paper letter **F**. First give it a quarter turn anticlockwise and then flip it in a vertical mirror line. Start again: first flip it, then turn it. The two results face different ways! Doing the same two moves in a different order can give a different result.

<Narrator who="chloe">

With slides the order never matters: 3 right then 1 up is the same as 1 up then 3 right. With turns and flips it can. I wonder which pairs of moves can be swapped and which cannot…

</Narrator>

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon only plays with statements that are exactly true or exactly false. Decide, and give a reason.

1. "The diagonals of every rectangle are lines of symmetry." — **False.** Only for squares. Fold a sheet of paper along a diagonal and the corners do not match.
2. "Flipping a shape twice in the same mirror line puts it back where it started." — **True.**
3. "A flip in a vertical line and a half turn always have the same effect." — **False.** A flipped letter F faces the other way; a half-turned F is upside down.
4. "Two flips in parallel mirror lines 3 squares apart make a slide of 6 squares." — **True.** Twice the gap.

<Narrator who="gideon">

For statement 3, one counterexample — the letter F — is enough to show "always" is false. Be careful: for the letter H, the flip and the half turn give the same *picture*. But they are still different moves, because they send the corner squares of the H to different places.

</Narrator>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**"The diagonals of a rectangle are lines of symmetry."** Only when the rectangle is a square. Fold a sheet of A4 paper along a diagonal: the two halves do not match. A rectangle has just 2 lines of symmetry, through the middles of opposite sides.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A flip and a half turn always do the same thing."** They are different moves. A flipped letter F faces the other way; a half-turned F is upside down. For a very symmetric shape such as H, the two *pictures* happen to agree, but the points still go to different places.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A parallelogram has two lines of symmetry, like a rectangle."** A parallelogram that is not a rectangle or a rhombus has no line of symmetry at all — but it does have rotational symmetry of order 2.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A shape with no rotational symmetry has order 0."** Every shape fits onto itself after a full turn, so the smallest order is 1.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Symmetries form a group">

The symmetries of a shape can be combined (do one, then another) and undone, and "doing nothing" is always a symmetry. A collection of moves with these properties is called a **group**. The square has a group of 8 symmetries: 4 turns and 4 flips. Combining two of them always gives one of the 8 — two flips give a turn, a turn and a flip give a flip — and the order of combining can matter. Groups are one of the central ideas of modern mathematics: see <TopicLink id="math.algebra.groups">groups</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Geometry is what moves cannot change">

Slides, turns and flips keep every length and every angle, so they never change the facts geometry cares about. In 1872 the mathematician Felix Klein suggested a new way to think about geometry: choose a family of moves, and study whatever those moves cannot change. You will meet this idea in <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">rigid motions, congruence and similarity</TopicLink> and, much later, in <TopicLink id="math.geom.transformation-geometry">transformation geometry and the Erlangen programme</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="categories" kind="categories" title="One move after another">

Doing one move after another, and undoing a move, are the same ideas you meet in <TopicLink id="math.cat.chaining-actions">one thing after another</TopicLink>: actions that can be chained, where chaining is associative and "do nothing" changes nothing. Mathematicians who study such chains in general — functions, moves, processes — use the language of *category theory*. Young learners need only the moves.

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- Symmetric patterns appear in the art of almost every culture: woven cloth in the Andes, Adinkra stamps and kente weaving in Ghana, kolam and rangoli in India, lattice windows in China, and the tiled walls of the **Alhambra** in Granada, built under the Nasrid dynasty in the 13th and 14th centuries.
- In the 19th century mathematicians began to study symmetry itself. In **1872** the German mathematician **Felix Klein** described geometry as the study of what stays the same under a group of moves.
- In **1891** the Russian crystallographer **Evgraf Fedorov** proved that repeating wallpaper patterns can have exactly **17** different kinds of symmetry. The same result was found again in 1924 by George Pólya and Paul Niggli, and the Dutch artist M. C. Escher studied Pólya's paper while making his famous tessellations.

</Section>
