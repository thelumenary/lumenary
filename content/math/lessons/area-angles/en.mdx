<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A fence, a floor and a turn">

Kofi lives on a farm in Ghana. He has **24 metres** of fence to make a rectangular pen for his goats. He could make it long and thin, or short and wide. Does it matter? The fence is the same length either way — but will the goats get the same amount of grass?

In a school in Lima, Ana's class is covering their classroom floor, 8 metres long and 6 metres wide, with square mats, each one metre long and one metre wide. How many mats do they need?

And when you ride a bicycle round a corner, you **turn**. Turn all the way round and you are facing where you started. How can we measure *how much* you turned?

These are three different questions: **how far around** (perimeter), **how much surface** (area), and **how much turn** (angle). This lesson is about all three — and about not mixing them up.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Covering, walking round, turning">

**Area** is about *covering*. Lay down square tiles, all the same size, with no gaps and no overlaps. The number of tiles you need is the area.

**Perimeter** is about *walking round the edge*. Walk all the way round a shape and measure how far you went.

Here is a rectangle drawn as rows of unit squares. Change the width and the height. Count the squares, then press "Trace the perimeter" to walk round it.

Four rows of six squares is $4 \times 6 = 24$ squares. But walking round, I go $6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 20$ metres. The numbers are different because they answer different questions: one counts squares, the other measures length.

</Narrator>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Words we need">

<Definition term="area">

The **area** of a shape is the amount of surface it covers. We measure it by counting **unit squares**: squares 1 unit long and 1 unit wide. A square 1 centimetre on each side has an area of **1 square centimetre**, written $1\text{ cm}^2$. A square 1 metre on each side has area $1\text{ m}^2$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="perimeter">

The **perimeter** of a shape is the total length of its boundary — the distance all the way round. It is a length, so it is measured in units of length such as centimetres (cm) or metres (m).

</Definition>

<Definition term="angle">

An **angle** is the amount of **turn** between two rays (arms) that start at the same point, the **vertex**. We measure angles in **degrees**: one full turn is $360^\circ$.

</Definition>

So a **half turn** is $180^\circ$ (a **straight angle**) and a **quarter turn** is $90^\circ$ (a **right angle**, like the corner of a book). An angle smaller than a right angle is **acute**; an angle between a right angle and a straight angle is **obtuse**; an angle bigger than a straight angle is **reflex**.

<Definition term="volume">

The **volume** of a solid is the amount of space it takes up. We measure it by counting **unit cubes**, for example cubes 1 cm on each edge, each of volume $1\text{ cm}^3$.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="A rectangle">

A rectangle is 7 cm long and 3 cm wide.

- **Area:** 3 rows of 7 unit squares: $3 \times 7 = 21\text{ cm}^2$.
- **Perimeter:** $7 + 3 + 7 + 3 = 20\text{ cm}$, which is also $2 \times (7 + 3)$.

</Example>

<Example title="Ana's classroom">

The floor is 8 m long and 6 m wide. The mats make 6 rows of 8, so the class needs $6 \times 8 = 48$ mats: the area of the floor is $48\text{ m}^2$. A ribbon round the edge of the floor would be $8 + 6 + 8 + 6 = 28$ m long.

</Example>

<Example title="An L-shaped room">

A room is shaped like an L: a 5 m by 4 m rectangle with a 2 m by 2 m square missing from one corner.

- **Area by subtracting:** $5 \times 4 - 2 \times 2 = 20 - 4 = 16\text{ m}^2$.
- **Area by splitting:** cut the L into a 5 m by 2 m strip and a 3 m by 2 m strip: $10 + 6 = 16\text{ m}^2$. Two different methods, the same answer — a good check.
- **Perimeter:** walk round the six walls: $5 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 18\text{ m}$. A wall you cannot see on the plan still has a length: work it out from the others.

</Example>

<Example title="Units matter">

A side is a length, so it is in cm. An area counts squares, so it is in $\text{cm}^2$. Writing "the area is 21 cm" is like saying "I have 21" without saying 21 *what*. Always say which unit you are counting.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="different" kind="human" title="Same area, different perimeter — and the other way round">

Back to Kofi and his 24 metres of fence. Here are some rectangles with whole-metre sides he could make:

| Pen | Perimeter | Area |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 m by 11 m | 24 m | $11\text{ m}^2$ |
| 2 m by 10 m | 24 m | $20\text{ m}^2$ |
| 4 m by 8 m | 24 m | $32\text{ m}^2$ |
| 6 m by 6 m | 24 m | $36\text{ m}^2$ |

Same fence, very different amounts of grass! The long thin pen gives the goats less than a third of the square pen. **Shapes with the same perimeter can have different areas.**

It works the other way too. All these rectangles have area $24\text{ m}^2$: 1 by 24 (perimeter 50 m), 2 by 12 (28 m), 3 by 8 (22 m), 4 by 6 (20 m). **Shapes with the same area can have different perimeters.**

Try it yourself: make three different shapes with an area of 12 squares, record each one, and compare their perimeters.

<AreaGrid mode="draw" modes={["draw"]} subtitle="Make three different shapes with an area of 12 squares. Record each one. Which has the longest perimeter? The shortest?" />

<Narrator who="chloe">

Why does the square pen hold the most? Look at the 4 m by 8 m pen. Make each long side 2 m shorter and each short side 2 m longer: the same fence now makes a 6 m by 6 m pen. The pen loses a strip at one end, 4 m by 2 m, which is $8\text{ m}^2$. But it gains a strip along one side, 6 m by 2 m, which is $12\text{ m}^2$. It gains more than it loses! Moving fence from the long sides to the short sides keeps helping until the pen is a square.

</Narrator>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Why the rules work">

<Theorem name="Area of a rectangle">

A rectangle that is $w$ units wide and $h$ units high has area $w \times h$ square units.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Cover the rectangle with unit squares. Each row holds $w$ squares, and there are $h$ rows (one for each unit of height). So there are $w + w + \dots + w$ ($h$ times) $= h \times w$ squares. Because $h \times w = w \times h$, it does not matter whether we count by rows or by columns.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Angles of a triangle">

The three angles of any triangle drawn on a flat surface add up to $180^\circ$.

</Theorem>

First an experiment you can do with paper and scissors: cut out a triangle — any triangle — and tear off its three corners. Put the corners together, points touching. They fit together into a **straight line**: a half turn, $180^\circ$. Try it with a long thin triangle and a fat one. It always works. But trying triangles is not a proof: here is why it *must* work.

<Proof>

Call the angles of the triangle $a$, $b$ and $c$, as in the picture. Through the top corner, draw the line **parallel** to the base: it never meets the base, however far it goes.

- Along the parallel line, the three angles $d$, $c$ and $e$ fit together into a straight line, so $d + c + e = 180^\circ$.
- The angle $d$ and the angle $a$ form a "Z" with the two parallel lines. Such **alternate angles** are equal: $d = a$. In the same way, $e = b$.

So $a + b + c = d + e + c = 180^\circ$.

</Proof>

<Note>

The fact that alternate angles at parallel lines are equal is one of the basic properties of a flat surface. On a curved surface, such as a globe, it fails — and so does the $180^\circ$ rule (see the viewpoints below).

</Note>

The $180^\circ$ rule has some quick consequences:

- **A triangle cannot have two right angles.** $90^\circ + 90^\circ = 180^\circ$ already, which would leave $0^\circ$ for the third angle.
- **A triangle has at most one obtuse angle**, for the same reason.
- **If all three angles are equal, each is $60^\circ$**, because $180 \div 3 = 60$.
- **The angles of a quadrilateral add up to $360^\circ$.** Cut it along a diagonal into two triangles: $180^\circ + 180^\circ = 360^\circ$.

<Theorem name="Vertically opposite angles">

Where two straight lines cross, the angles opposite each other are equal.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Call the angles going round the crossing point $a$, $b$, $c$. The angles $a$ and $b$ together make a straight line, so $a + b = 180^\circ$. The angles $b$ and $c$ also make a straight line, so $b + c = 180^\circ$. Both $a$ and $c$ are "$180^\circ$ take away $b$", so $a = c$.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="sorting-shapes" kind="visualization" title="Sorting triangles and quadrilaterals">

We can sort triangles by their **sides**:

- **equilateral:** all three sides equal;
- **isosceles:** at least two sides equal;
- **scalene:** no two sides equal;

and by their **angles**:

- **acute:** all three angles are acute;
- **right:** one angle is a right angle;
- **obtuse:** one angle is obtuse.

Quadrilaterals have names too: **square**, **rectangle**, **rhombus**, **parallelogram**, **trapezium** (called a *trapezoid* in the USA) and **kite**. Every square is also a rectangle (four right angles) and also a rhombus (four equal sides).

<Theorem name="Volume of a box">

A box $\ell$ units long, $w$ units wide and $h$ units high has volume $\ell \times w \times h$ cubic units.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Each layer is a rectangle of $\ell \times w$ cubes (by the area rule), and there are $h$ layers, so there are $\ell \times w \times h$ cubes.

</Proof>

</Section>

<Section id="gideon-game" kind="formal" title="Gideon's true-or-false game">

Gideon only plays with statements that are exactly true or exactly false. Decide, and give a reason.

1. "A rectangle 3 cm by 4 cm and a rectangle 2 cm by 6 cm have the same area." — **True.** Both are $12\text{ cm}^2$.
2. "…so they also have the same perimeter." — **False.** 14 cm and 16 cm. One counterexample is enough to break "same area means same perimeter".
3. "Some triangle has two right angles." — **False.** Its angles would already add up to $180^\circ$ before the third one.
4. "An angle with longer arms is bigger." — **False.** The size of an angle is the amount of turn, not the length of the arms.

<Narrator who="gideon">

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Things that trip people up">

<Misconception>

**Mixing up area and perimeter.** Area counts the squares *inside* (square units); perimeter measures the distance *around* (units of length). A 3 by 4 rectangle has area $12\text{ cm}^2$ but perimeter 14 cm.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Shapes with the same perimeter have the same area."** Kofi's pens all use 24 m of fence, but their areas range from $11\text{ m}^2$ to $36\text{ m}^2$. And shapes with the same area can have different perimeters.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"An angle gets bigger when its arms are drawn longer."** An angle measures turn. Longer arms reach further, but the turn between them is the same.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Bigger triangles have bigger angle sums."** Every triangle on a flat surface has angle sum $180^\circ$, whether it fits on a stamp or covers a football field.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Writing the wrong units.** Area is in square units ($\text{cm}^2$, $\text{m}^2$), volume in cubic units ($\text{cm}^3$), and perimeter in plain units of length (cm, m).

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="When angles add up to more than 180°">

The $180^\circ$ rule is a fact about **flat** surfaces. On a globe, draw a triangle from the North Pole down to the equator, a quarter of the way round the equator, and back up to the pole. All three angles are right angles: the sum is $270^\circ$! On a sphere, every triangle has an angle sum bigger than $180^\circ$, and the bigger the triangle, the bigger the sum. Geometers measure how curved a surface is by exactly this extra angle — see <TopicLink id="math.geom.non-euclidean-geometry">non-Euclidean geometry</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="The area of curved shapes">

How can we find the area of a circle, when no square tiles fit it exactly? Cover it with smaller and smaller squares: the squares completely inside give an under-estimate, the squares touching it give an over-estimate, and as the squares shrink the two estimates squeeze together. Making this squeezing precise is part of what analysts do. Much later, <TopicLink id="math.analysis.measure-theory">measure theory</TopicLink> asks which sets of points have an area at all — and finds, surprisingly, that if area must behave well even for infinitely many pieces, some very strange sets cannot be given any area.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="sets" kind="sets" title="Area, pieces and sets">

When we split the L-shaped room into two strips, we used a rule about pieces: if a shape is made of two pieces that **do not overlap** (they may share an edge), its area is the area of one piece plus the area of the other. This is just like counting the members of two collections with nothing in common, from the lesson on <TopicLink id="math.found.sets">sets</TopicLink>. If the pieces *do* overlap, we must subtract the overlap once, just as $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|$.

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- In ancient **Egypt**, the *Rhind Mathematical Papyrus* (copied by the scribe Ahmes around 1550 BCE) contains problems about the areas of fields and the volumes of granaries. Measuring land mattered: the yearly Nile floods washed away field boundaries.
- In **China**, the first chapter of *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, compiled roughly two thousand years ago, is called *Rectangular Fields* and teaches how to find the areas of fields of many shapes.
- In **India**, the *Śulba-sūtras* (written between about 800 and 200 BCE) give rules for building fire altars of exact shapes and areas, including how to make a square with the same area as a given rectangle.
- Dividing a full turn into **360 degrees** is usually traced back to astronomers in ancient **Mesopotamia**, who counted in sixties; Greek astronomers such as Hipparchus later used it, and it spread around the world.
- **Euclid**'s *Elements* (Alexandria, about 300 BCE), Book I, Proposition 32, proves that the angles of a triangle add up to two right angles, using a line parallel to one side — the same idea as our proof.
