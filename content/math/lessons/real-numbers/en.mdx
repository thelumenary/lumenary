<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="A diagonal that is not a fraction">

Draw a square whose sides are 1 unit long. By the Pythagorean theorem its diagonal $d$ satisfies $d^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2$, so $d = \sqrt 2$. How long is that, exactly?

People have asked this for a very long time. A clay tablet from Old Babylonian Mesopotamia (about 1800–1600 BCE, now in the Yale collection as YBC 7289) shows a square with its diagonals and the number $1;24,51,10$ in base sixty — that is, $1 + \tfrac{24}{60} + \tfrac{51}{60^2} + \tfrac{10}{60^3} \approx 1.41421296$, correct to about six decimal places. The Indian *Śulba-sūtras*, manuals for building fire altars, give $1 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac{1}{3 \cdot 4} - \tfrac{1}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 34} = \tfrac{577}{408} \approx 1.4142157$.

These are superb approximations. But is there a fraction that is **exactly** $\sqrt 2$? In this lesson we prove that there is not — and then ask what kind of number $\sqrt 2$ must be, if not a fraction.

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Crowded, and still full of holes">

The fractions (rational numbers) are packed tightly on the number line. Between any two of them, $a < b$, lies their average $\tfrac{a + b}{2}$; between $a$ and that average lies another; and so on forever. There is no "next" fraction after $1$, and every tiny interval, however short, contains infinitely many fractions. You met this **density** in <TopicLink id="math.number.rational-numbers">Rational numbers</TopicLink>.

So it is natural to guess that the fractions cover the whole line. They do not. The point at distance $\sqrt 2$ from $0$ is surrounded by fractions — $\tfrac75$, $\tfrac{17}{12}$, $\tfrac{41}{29}$, $\tfrac{577}{408}$, each closer than the last — but none of them lands on it. The fractions are like an infinitely fine sieve: dense, yet full of holes.

<Narrator who="chloe">

Here is a strange thing. If the fractions miss $\sqrt 2$, then the set of fractions whose square is less than $2$ has no "edge" inside the fractions: it goes right up to the hole and stops. Can a set of fractions be bounded but have no best bound? Keep that question in mind — it is the heart of the lesson.

</Narrator>

The **real numbers** $\R$ are what we get when we fill in all the holes. Every point of the line is a real number; the rational ones are special, and the others are called **irrational**.

</Section>

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Definitions">

Informally, a **real number** is a point on the number line, or equivalently an infinite decimal such as $3.14159\ldots$ or $-0.5000\ldots$. (A rigorous construction of $\R$ from $\Q$ is the subject of <TopicLink id="math.number.number-system-constructions">Constructing ℤ, ℚ and ℝ</TopicLink>; here we use the real numbers and their arithmetic and order as familiar, plus one extra property, *completeness*, stated below.)

<Definition term="rational and irrational numbers">

A real number $x$ is **rational** if $x = \tfrac{p}{q}$ for some integers $p, q$ with $q \ne 0$. The set of rational numbers is $\Q$. A real number that is not rational is **irrational**.

</Definition>

<Definition term="absolute value and distance">

The **absolute value** of a real number $x$ is

$$
|x| = \begin{cases} x & \text{if } x \ge 0, \\ -x & \text{if } x < 0. \end{cases}
$$

It is the distance from $x$ to $0$, and $|x - y|$ is the **distance** between $x$ and $y$. Always $|x| \ge 0$, $|-x| = |x|$, $|xy| = |x|\,|y|$ and $-|x| \le x \le |x|$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="intervals">

For real numbers $a < b$:

| Notation | Meaning | Endpoints included? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $(a, b)$ | all $x$ with $a < x < b$ | neither (open interval) |
| $[a, b]$ | all $x$ with $a \le x \le b$ | both (closed interval) |
| $[a, b)$ | all $x$ with $a \le x < b$ | only $a$ |
| $(a, \infty)$ | all $x$ with $x > a$ | — ($\infty$ is not a number) |

An inequality about distance is a statement about an interval: $|x - c| < r$ means $c - r < x < c + r$, i.e. $x \in (c - r, c + r)$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="upper bound and least upper bound">

Let $S$ be a set of real numbers. A number $b$ is an **upper bound** of $S$ if $s \le b$ for every $s \in S$; then $S$ is **bounded above**. A **least upper bound** (or **supremum**, $\sup S$) is an upper bound $b$ such that no smaller number is an upper bound: if $c < b$, some element of $S$ is bigger than $c$.

</Definition>

The supremum need not belong to the set: $\sup\,[0, 1) = 1$, although $1 \notin [0, 1)$. A set has at most one least upper bound (if $b$ and $b'$ were both least, then $b \le b'$ and $b' \le b$).

<Theorem name="completeness of ℝ (informal statement)">

Every non-empty set of real numbers that is bounded above has a least upper bound **in $\R$**.

</Theorem>

In this lesson completeness is taken as a basic property of $\R$ — the property that says "there are no holes". (It *can* be proved, once $\R$ has been built from $\Q$, for example by Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences; Lean's library does exactly that.) The rational numbers satisfy all the usual rules of arithmetic and order, but **not** completeness — we will prove this below.

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Decimals and ordering">

To compare two decimals, compare digit by digit from the left: $0.3289\ldots < 0.33$, and $\sqrt 2 = 1.41421\ldots < 1.4143$. One subtlety: some numbers have **two** decimal expansions. $0.4999\ldots$ and $0.5000\ldots$ are the same real number, just as $0.999\ldots = 1$; this happens exactly for decimals that end in an infinite string of $9$s. (Why $0.999\ldots = 1$ is proved in <TopicLink id="math.analysis.sequences-series">Sequences and series</TopicLink>: a decimal *means* a sum of an infinite series.)

</Example>

<Example title="A repeating decimal is a fraction">

Let $x = 0.272727\ldots$. Then $100x = 27.272727\ldots$, and subtracting,

$$
99x = 27, \qquad x = \frac{27}{99} = \frac{3}{11}.
$$

The same trick (multiply by $10^k$, where $k$ is the length of the repeating block, and subtract) turns every eventually repeating decimal into a fraction. Conversely, long division of $p$ by $q$ can only produce the remainders $0, 1, \dots, q - 1$; as soon as a remainder recurs, the digits repeat. So **a decimal is rational exactly when it terminates or eventually repeats**, and the repeating block of $\tfrac{p}{q}$ has at most $q - 1$ digits. For instance $\tfrac17 = 0.\overline{142857}$ uses all $6$ possible non-zero remainders.

</Example>

<Example title="Absolute value inequalities">

$|x - 3| \le 2$ means "the distance from $x$ to $3$ is at most $2$", i.e. $1 \le x \le 5$, the interval $[1, 5]$. And $|2x + 1| > 5$ means $2x + 1 > 5$ or $2x + 1 < -5$, so $x > 2$ or $x < -3$: the union $(-\infty, -3) \cup (2, \infty)$.

</Example>

<Example title="Suprema, attained and not">

- $S = \{1 - \tfrac1n : n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\} = \{0, \tfrac12, \tfrac23, \tfrac34, \dots\}$. Every element is less than $1$, so $1$ is an upper bound. If $c < 1$, choose $n$ with $\tfrac1n < 1 - c$; then $1 - \tfrac1n > c$, so $c$ is not an upper bound. Hence $\sup S = 1$, and $1 \notin S$.
- $T = [0, 1]$ has $\sup T = 1 \in T$: here the supremum is the **maximum**.
- $\N = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ is not bounded above, so it has no supremum in $\R$ at all. Completeness only promises suprema for sets that are bounded above.

</Example>

<Example title="Rational or irrational?">

- $\sqrt{49} = 7$ and $\sqrt{\tfrac{9}{4}} = \tfrac32$ are rational.
- $\sqrt 2$, $\sqrt 3$, $\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt 3$ are irrational (proved below).
- $3 + \sqrt 2$ is irrational: a rational plus an irrational is irrational.
- $\sqrt 2 \cdot \sqrt 8 = \sqrt{16} = 4$ is rational, although both factors are irrational.
- $\pi$ is irrational (Lambert, 1761) — so $\tfrac{22}{7} = 3.142857\ldots$ and $3.14$ are approximations, not values.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Proofs">

We start with a fact about parity.

<Theorem name="Lemma 1 (squares and parity)">

For an integer $p$: if $p^2$ is even, then $p$ is even.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

We prove the contrapositive: if $p$ is odd, then $p^2$ is odd. Write $p = 2k + 1$. Then $p^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$, which is odd.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 1 (√2 is irrational)">

There are no natural numbers $p, q$ with $q \ne 0$ and $p^2 = 2q^2$. Consequently $\sqrt 2$ is irrational.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose, for a contradiction, that there were such pairs. By the well-ordering principle, choose one, $(p, q)$, in which $q \ge 1$ is as **small as possible**.

Since $p^2 = 2q^2$ is even, $p$ is even by Lemma 1; write $p = 2k$. Then $4k^2 = 2q^2$, so $q^2 = 2k^2$. Thus $(q, k)$ is another solution of the same equation. Moreover $k \ge 1$ (otherwise $q^2 = 0$), and $k < q$, because $k^2 < 2k^2 = q^2$. So $(q, k)$ is a solution with a smaller second number than $q$ — contradicting the choice of $(p, q)$. Hence no solution exists.

Finally, if $\sqrt 2 = \tfrac{a}{b}$ with integers $a, b$, $b \ne 0$, then $a^2 = 2b^2$, and $(|a|, |b|)$ would be a solution in natural numbers with $|b| \ne 0$. So $\sqrt 2$ is not rational.

</Proof>

This is an argument by **infinite descent**: any solution would produce a smaller one, and the natural numbers cannot go down forever. (The version you may have seen — "write $\sqrt 2 = \tfrac pq$ in lowest terms; then both $p$ and $q$ are even, a contradiction" — is the same argument: "lowest terms" is where the well-ordering hides.)

What about other square roots? The same descent works for every non-square, with a neat trick that needs no factorisation into primes.

<Theorem name="Theorem 2 (√n for non-squares)">

Let $n$ be a positive integer that is not a perfect square. Then $\sqrt n$ is irrational.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Since $n$ is not a square, there is an integer $m$ with $m < \sqrt n < m + 1$ (take $m$ the largest integer with $m^2 < n$). Suppose $\sqrt n = \tfrac pq$ with $p, q$ positive integers, and choose such a representation with $q$ as small as possible. Put

$$
q' = p - mq, \qquad p' = nq - mp.
$$

These are integers. Since $p = q\sqrt n$, we have $q' = q(\sqrt n - m)$, and $0 < \sqrt n - m < 1$ gives $0 < q' < q$. And

$$
\frac{p'}{q'} = \frac{nq - mp}{p - mq} = \frac{q\sqrt n\,(\sqrt n - m)}{q(\sqrt n - m)} = \sqrt n,
$$

using $n q = \sqrt n \cdot \sqrt n \, q = \sqrt n \, p$ and $mp = m q \sqrt n$. Since $p' = q'\sqrt n > 0$, this writes $\sqrt n$ as a fraction of positive integers with the smaller denominator $q' < q$ — a contradiction.

</Proof>

Where does the proof use that $n$ is not a square? In the strict inequalities $m < \sqrt n < m + 1$, which give $0 < q' < q$. If $n = k^2$ is a square, then $\sqrt n = k$ is a whole number, no integer $m$ satisfies them, and indeed $\sqrt n = \tfrac k1$ already has the smallest possible denominator.

<Theorem name="Theorem 3 (rational + irrational)">

If $q$ is rational and $x$ is irrational, then $q + x$ is irrational.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose $q + x = r$ were rational. Then $x = r - q$ is a difference of two rationals, which is rational. That contradicts the irrationality of $x$.

</Proof>

No such rule holds for two irrationals: $\sqrt 2 + (-\sqrt 2) = 0$ and $\sqrt 2 \cdot \sqrt 2 = 2$ are rational. (Sometimes the answer is irrational, e.g. $\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3$ — try squaring it — but it has to be checked case by case.)

<Theorem name="Theorem 4 (triangle inequality)">

For all real numbers $x, y$: $\;|x + y| \le |x| + |y|$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

From $-|x| \le x \le |x|$ and $-|y| \le y \le |y|$, adding gives

$$
-\big(|x| + |y|\big) \le x + y \le |x| + |y|.
$$

A number $t$ with $-c \le t \le c$ satisfies $|t| \le c$ (whichever of $t$ and $-t$ is $|t|$, it is at most $c$). With $t = x + y$ and $c = |x| + |y|$ this is the claim.

</Proof>

In terms of distance, the inequality reads $|a - c| \le |a - b| + |b - c|$ (put $x = a - b$, $y = b - c$): going from $a$ to $c$ via $b$ is never shorter than going directly. That is where the name comes from.

Now the question Chloe asked. Let

$$
S = \{q \in \Q : q^2 < 2\}.
$$

$S$ is non-empty ($0 \in S$) and bounded above by $2$ (if $q > 2$ then $q^2 > 4$). The heart of the matter is one clever fraction.

<Theorem name="Lemma 2 (pushing towards √2)">

For a rational $p > 0$ put $p' = \dfrac{2p + 2}{p + 2}$. Then

$$
p' - p = \frac{2 - p^2}{p + 2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad p'^2 - 2 = \frac{2(p^2 - 2)}{(p + 2)^2}.
$$

So if $p^2 < 2$, then $p' > p$ and still $p'^2 < 2$; if $p^2 > 2$, then $p' < p$ and still $p'^2 > 2$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Both identities are algebra: $p' - p = \tfrac{2p + 2 - p^2 - 2p}{p + 2} = \tfrac{2 - p^2}{p + 2}$, and

$$
p'^2 - 2 = \frac{(2p + 2)^2 - 2(p + 2)^2}{(p + 2)^2} = \frac{4p^2 + 8p + 4 - 2p^2 - 8p - 8}{(p + 2)^2} = \frac{2p^2 - 4}{(p + 2)^2}.
$$

Since $p + 2 > 0$, the signs of $p' - p$ and $p'^2 - 2$ are those of $2 - p^2$ and $p^2 - 2$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="Theorem 5 (ℚ is not complete)">

The set $S = \{q \in \Q : q^2 < 2\}$ has no least upper bound in $\Q$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Suppose $b \in \Q$ were a least upper bound of $S$. Since $1 \in S$, $b \ge 1 > 0$. By Theorem 1, $b^2 \ne 2$. Let $b' = \tfrac{2b + 2}{b + 2}$, a rational number.

- If $b^2 < 2$: by Lemma 2, $b' > b$ and $b'^2 < 2$, so $b' \in S$ is bigger than the upper bound $b$. Impossible.
- If $b^2 > 2$: by Lemma 2, $b' < b$ and $b'^2 > 2$. Then $b'$ is an upper bound of $S$: if some $s \in S$ had $s > b'$, then (as $b' > 0$) $s^2 > b'^2 > 2$, contradicting $s^2 < 2$. So $b'$ is an upper bound smaller than the *least* upper bound $b$. Impossible.

Both cases fail, so no rational least upper bound exists.

</Proof>

In $\R$, by completeness, $S$ does have a least upper bound $s$, and $s^2 = 2$: the argument of Theorem 5 still rules out $s^2 > 2$, and it rules out $s^2 < 2$ once we add that between $s$ and $s' = \tfrac{2s + 2}{s + 2}$ there is a rational number, which would then lie in $S$ above $s$. This is how completeness *creates* $\sqrt 2$: the hole in $\Q$ is filled by a real number. Lean confirms below that the supremum is exactly $\sqrt 2$.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"$\pi = \tfrac{22}{7}$" or "$\pi = 3.14$".** These are approximations. $\tfrac{22}{7} = 3.142857\ldots$ agrees with $\pi = 3.141592\ldots$ only to two decimal places, and Zu Chongzhi's $\tfrac{355}{113}$ to six. Since $\pi$ is irrational, no fraction and no terminating decimal equals it.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A long decimal that never seems to repeat could still be a fraction."** If you know the decimal *never* repeats, it is not a fraction: every fraction's decimal terminates or repeats with period at most $q - 1$. What a finite list of digits cannot tell you is whether it *never* repeats — that needs a proof about the number.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The product (or sum) of two irrational numbers is irrational."** $\sqrt 2 \cdot \sqrt 2 = 2$, $\sqrt 3 \cdot \sqrt{12} = 6$, and $\sqrt 2 + (2 - \sqrt 2) = 2$. Only "rational $+$ irrational" and "non-zero rational $\times$ irrational" are guaranteed irrational.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"$0.999\ldots$ is just below $1$."** The decimal $0.999\ldots$ is the limit of $0.9, 0.99, 0.999, \dots$, whose distances from $1$ are $\tfrac{1}{10}, \tfrac{1}{100}, \dots$ — eventually below any positive number. A limit is a single number, and here it is exactly $1$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Irrational numbers are rare exceptions."** It is the other way round. The rationals can be listed in one infinite sequence, but the reals cannot (Cantor's diagonal argument, on the *How many infinities?* trail). In that precise sense almost every real number is irrational.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Incommensurable lengths">

For Greek geometers the discovery was about lengths, not numbers. Two segments are **commensurable** if both are whole-number multiples of a common unit. Theorem 1 says the side and diagonal of a square are **incommensurable**: no unit, however small, measures both exactly. Euclid's *Elements* (Book X) builds a whole classification of incommensurable lengths, and Eudoxus' theory of ratios (Book V) compares them by asking which fractions lie below and which above — an idea that Dedekind turned into a construction of $\R$ in 1872.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="number-theory" title="The best fractions for √2">

The near misses $p^2 - 2q^2 = \pm 1$ in the experiment — $\tfrac11, \tfrac32, \tfrac75, \tfrac{17}{12}, \tfrac{41}{29}, \dots$ — are solutions of a **Pell equation**. Each comes from the previous one by $(p, q) \mapsto (p + 2q, p + q)$, and they are the convergents of the continued fraction $\sqrt 2 = 1 + \cfrac{1}{2 + \cfrac{1}{2 + \cdots}}$. Dividing $p^2 - 2q^2 = \pm 1$ by $q^2$ shows $\big|\tfrac pq - \sqrt 2\big| = \tfrac{1}{q^2\,(p/q + \sqrt 2)} < \tfrac{1}{2q^2}$: irrational numbers can be approximated remarkably well, and how well is a deep question of number theory (Diophantine approximation).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="analysis" title="Why calculus needs completeness">

Over $\Q$, the function $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ is negative at $1$ and positive at $2$, yet it is never $0$ at a rational point: the **intermediate value theorem fails** in $\Q$. Completeness is exactly what repairs it, and with it the existence of limits of increasing bounded sequences, of maxima of continuous functions on $[a, b]$, and of integrals. Every theorem of <TopicLink id="math.analysis.real-analysis">real analysis</TopicLink> leans on it.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="sets" title="Countably many fractions, uncountably many reals">

The rationals can be listed as a sequence (go through $\tfrac pq$ by increasing $|p| + q$), so $\Q$ is **countable**. Cantor proved in 1874 that $\R$ is not: any list of reals misses one. So irrational numbers are not the exception but the rule. See <TopicLink id="math.found.countability">Countability and Cantor's diagonal argument</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

</Section>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- **Mesopotamia and India.** The Old Babylonian tablet YBC 7289 (about 1800–1600 BCE) records $\sqrt 2 \approx 1;24,51,10$ in base sixty. The *Baudhāyana Śulba-sūtra* (first millennium BCE) gives the approximation $\tfrac{577}{408}$, used for constructing altars of prescribed areas. Neither source asks whether an exact fraction exists.
- **Greece.** The discovery that side and diagonal are incommensurable was made among the Pythagoreans, probably in the 5th century BCE; the story that Hippasus was drowned for revealing it is a later legend. Plato's *Theaetetus* reports that Theodorus of Cyrene proved the irrationality of $\sqrt 3, \sqrt 5, \dots, \sqrt{17}$. Aristotle alludes to the classical parity proof for $\sqrt 2$, and Euclid's *Elements* (about 300 BCE) sets out Eudoxus' theory of proportion and, in Book X, a theory of incommensurable magnitudes.
- **China, India and the Islamic world.** Zu Chongzhi (5th century CE) showed that $\pi$ lies between $3.1415926$ and $3.1415927$ and proposed the approximation $\tfrac{355}{113}$; his bounds were not improved for nearly a thousand years. Āryabhaṭa (499 CE) gave $\pi \approx \tfrac{62832}{20000} = 3.1416$ and called it "approximate". In the 11th century Omar Khayyam, commenting on Euclid, argued that ratios of incommensurable magnitudes could be treated as numbers.
- **Europe.** Simon Stevin popularised decimal fractions (*De Thiende*, 1585). Johann Heinrich Lambert proved $\pi$ irrational in 1761. In 1872 Richard Dedekind (cuts) and Georg Cantor (Cauchy sequences), building on ideas of Charles Méray and Karl Weierstrass, published rigorous constructions of $\R$ from $\Q$, and in 1874 Cantor proved that $\R$ is uncountable.
