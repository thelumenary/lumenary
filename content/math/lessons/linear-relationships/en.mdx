<Section id="motivation" kind="motivation" title="When do the thermometers agree?">

Most of the world measures temperature in degrees Celsius; a few countries, including the United States, use degrees Fahrenheit. Water freezes at $0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, which is $32\,^\circ\mathrm{F}$, and boils (at sea level) at $100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, which is $212\,^\circ\mathrm{F}$. A warm afternoon in Accra at $25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is $77\,^\circ\mathrm{F}$.

| Celsius $C$ | $0$ | $25$ | $100$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fahrenheit $F$ | $32$ | $77$ | $212$ |

Here is a puzzle: **is there a temperature at which both thermometers show the same number?** At $0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the Fahrenheit number is bigger by $32$; at $100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, bigger by $112$. The gap grows as it gets warmer — so perhaps it shrinks as it gets colder, and vanishes somewhere below zero?

More than 1,500 years ago, a Chinese textbook, the *Sunzi Suanjing*, posed a famous puzzle of the same kind: pheasants and rabbits share a cage; there are $35$ heads and $94$ feet. How many of each? Both puzzles ask for two unknown numbers satisfying two conditions at once. By the end of this lesson you will solve them in three different ways — and know exactly when such a puzzle has one answer, none, or infinitely many.

</Section>

<Section id="intuition" kind="intuition" title="Constant rate of change">

A taxi in Lima charges a fixed $5$ soles plus $2$ soles per kilometre.

| distance $x$ (km) | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| fare $y$ (soles) | $5$ | $7$ | $9$ | $11$ | $13$ |

Each extra kilometre adds the same amount, $2$ soles. The fare changes at a **constant rate** of $2$ soles per kilometre, and the formula is $y = 2x + 5$. Plot the table and the points line up perfectly: a constant rate of change draws a **straight line**. The rate, $2$, tells us how steep the line is; the starting value, $5$, tells us where it crosses the vertical axis.

<Section id="definitions" kind="definitions" title="Slope, linear functions and systems">

<Definition term="slope">

The **slope** of the line through two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ with $x_1 \neq x_2$ is

$$
m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}.
$$

It is the change in $y$ per unit change in $x$: a **rate of change**. A vertical line ($x_1 = x_2$) has no slope — its run is $0$, and we cannot divide by $0$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="linear function">

A **linear function** has the form $f(x) = mx + b$ for fixed numbers $m$ and $b$ (see <TopicLink id="math.found.functions">functions</TopicLink>). Its graph, drawn in the <TopicLink id="math.geom.coordinate-plane">coordinate plane</TopicLink>, is the line $y = mx + b$, with slope $m$ and **$y$-intercept** $b$ (the height where it crosses the $y$-axis, at $x = 0$).

</Definition>

<Definition term="proportional relationship">

A relationship is **proportional** if $y = kx$ for a constant $k$, the **constant of proportionality** (see <TopicLink id="math.number.ratios-proportions">ratios and proportional reasoning</TopicLink>). Proportional relationships are the linear functions with $b = 0$: their graphs pass through the origin.

</Definition>

<Definition term="system of equations">

A **system** of equations is a collection of equations in the same unknowns. A **solution** of the system is a choice of values that satisfies **every** equation at once. Equations solved together are also called **simultaneous equations**.

</Definition>

</Section>

<Section id="examples" kind="examples" title="Worked examples">

<Example title="Slopes from two points">

- Through $(2, 1)$ and $(6, 9)$: $m = \dfrac{9 - 1}{6 - 2} = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2$. The line rises $2$ for every $1$ to the right.
- Through $(-1, 4)$ and $(3, -4)$: $m = \dfrac{-4 - 4}{3 - (-1)} = \dfrac{-8}{4} = -2$. A negative slope: the line falls.
- Through $(1, 3)$ and $(5, 3)$: $m = \dfrac{0}{4} = 0$. A horizontal line: $y$ does not change.
- Through $(3, 1)$ and $(3, 6)$: the run is $0$. This is the vertical line $x = 3$; its slope is **undefined**.

You may take the points in either order, as long as you subtract in the same order on top and bottom: $\dfrac{1 - 9}{2 - 6} = \dfrac{-8}{-4} = 2$ as well.

</Example>

<Example title="A line from its slope and one point">

Find the line with slope $3$ through $(2, 5)$. It has the form $y = 3x + b$, and the point must satisfy it: $5 = 3 \cdot 2 + b$, so $b = -1$. The line is $y = 3x - 1$.

In general, the line with slope $m$ through $(x_1, y_1)$ is $y = mx + (y_1 - m x_1)$, which can also be written in **point–slope form**: $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$.

</Example>

<Example title="A line through two points: Celsius to Fahrenheit">

The points $(0, 32)$ and $(100, 212)$ give the slope $m = \dfrac{212 - 32}{100 - 0} = \dfrac{180}{100} = \dfrac{9}{5}$, and the intercept is $32$. So

$$
F = \tfrac{9}{5}\,C + 32.
$$

Check with the third point: $\tfrac{9}{5} \cdot 25 + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77$. ✓ The slope says that each degree Celsius is $1.8$ degrees Fahrenheit.

</Example>

<Example title="Recognising linear relationships in tables">

In a table whose $x$-values go up in equal steps, a relationship is linear exactly when the $y$-values go up (or down) by equal steps too.

- Rice at $60$ rupees per kilogram: $1 \mapsto 60$, $2 \mapsto 120$, $5 \mapsto 300$. Linear **and proportional**: $y = 60x$.
- The taxi: $0 \mapsto 5$, $1 \mapsto 7$, $2 \mapsto 9$. Linear but **not** proportional: doubling the distance does not double the fare ($2$ km costs $9$ soles, not $2 \cdot 7 = 14$).
- The area of a square: $1 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 4$, $3 \mapsto 9$, $4 \mapsto 16$. The steps are $3, 5, 7$: **not linear**.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="proofs" kind="proof" title="Why a line has one slope">

The examples suggest two facts: on a line, the slope does not depend on which two points we pick; and a constant rate of change always gives a formula $y = mx + b$. Here are the proofs.

<Theorem name="the slope of y = mx + b is m">

For any two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ on the line $y = mx + b$ with $x_1 \neq x_2$, the slope between them is $m$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Since both points are on the line, $y_1 = m x_1 + b$ and $y_2 = m x_2 + b$. So the rise is

$$
y_2 - y_1 = (m x_2 + b) - (m x_1 + b) = m x_2 - m x_1 = m(x_2 - x_1),
$$

and dividing by the run $x_2 - x_1 \neq 0$ gives exactly $m$.

</Proof>

<Theorem name="constant rate of change means linear">

Suppose a function $f$ has a constant rate of change $m$: for all $x_1$ and $x_2$, $\;f(x_2) - f(x_1) = m(x_2 - x_1)$. Then $f(x) = mx + b$ for every $x$, where $b = f(0)$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Take $x_1 = 0$ and $x_2 = x$: $f(x) - f(0) = m(x - 0) = mx$, so $f(x) = mx + f(0)$.

</Proof>

The picture gives the geometric reason: slope triangles drawn on one line are **similar** (their angles match because the line has one direction), and similar triangles have proportional sides (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.rigid-motions-similarity">similarity</TopicLink>). The algebraic proof above needs no picture at all.

<Theorem name="proportional means through the origin">

The linear relationship $y = mx + b$ is proportional — that is, $mx + b = kx$ for all $x$, for some constant $k$ — if and only if $b = 0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

If $mx + b = kx$ for every $x$, put $x = 0$: $b = 0$. Conversely, if $b = 0$ then $y = mx$, which is proportional with $k = m$.

</Proof>

<Section id="systems" kind="examples" title="Simultaneous equations: three methods">

Each linear equation in two unknowns $x$ and $y$, such as $x + y = 35$, has infinitely many solutions — all the points of a line. A **system** of two such equations asks for the points on **both** lines: where they meet.

<Example title="Graphing: pheasants and rabbits">

Let $x$ be the number of pheasants and $y$ the number of rabbits. Counting heads and feet (two feet per pheasant, four per rabbit):

$$
x + y = 35, \qquad 2x + 4y = 94.
$$

Solving each for $y$: $y = 35 - x$ and $y = 23.5 - 0.5x$. The lines meet at $(23, 12)$.

<FunctionPlot
  fns={["35 - x", "23.5 - 0.5*x"]}
  labels={["heads: x + y = 35", "feet: 2x + 4y = 94"]}
  points={[[23, 12, "(23, 12)"]]}
  x={[0, 40]}
  y={[0, 40]}
  xLabel="pheasants"
  yLabel="rabbits"
  editable={false}
  title="Two conditions, one meeting point"
/>

A graph shows the answer at a glance, but reading it is only as accurate as the drawing — a meeting point at $(23.4, 11.7)$ would look much the same. Algebra gives exact answers.

</Example>

<Example title="Elimination: pheasants and rabbits again">

Multiply the first equation by $2$, giving $2x + 2y = 70$, and subtract it from the second:

$$
(2x + 4y) - (2x + 2y) = 94 - 70, \quad\text{so}\quad 2y = 24 \quad\text{and}\quad y = 12.
$$

Then $x = 35 - 12 = 23$. **Check both equations:** $23 + 12 = 35$ heads, and $2 \cdot 23 + 4 \cdot 12 = 46 + 48 = 94$ feet. ✓ Elimination works because, if both equations hold, any combination of them holds too.

</Example>

<Example title="Substitution: when do the thermometers agree?">

We want $F = \tfrac{9}{5}C + 32$ and $F = C$ at the same time. Substitute the second equation into the first:

$$
C = \tfrac{9}{5}C + 32 \;\Longrightarrow\; -\tfrac{4}{5}C = 32 \;\Longrightarrow\; C = -40.
$$

So $-40\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = -40\,^\circ\mathrm{F}$ — a temperature reached in the coldest winters of places such as Yakutsk in Siberia.

</Example>

<Example title="No solution, and infinitely many">

- $x + y = 3$ and $2x + 2y = 10$. Doubling the first gives $2x + 2y = 6$, which contradicts $2x + 2y = 10$. **No solution**: as $y = 3 - x$ and $y = 5 - x$, the graphs are parallel lines.
- $x + 2y = 4$ and $2x + 4y = 8$. The second equation is just the first one doubled: both describe the **same** line. **Infinitely many solutions**: for every number $t$, the point $(4 - 2t, t)$ works.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="trichotomy" kind="proof" title="One, none, or infinitely many">

<Theorem name="where two lines meet">

Consider the lines $y = m_1 x + b_1$ and $y = m_2 x + b_2$.

1. If $m_1 \neq m_2$, they meet in exactly one point, at $x = \dfrac{b_2 - b_1}{m_1 - m_2}$.
2. If $m_1 = m_2$ and $b_1 \neq b_2$, they never meet: they are **parallel**.
3. If $m_1 = m_2$ and $b_1 = b_2$, they are the same line.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

A meeting point needs $m_1 x + b_1 = m_2 x + b_2$, that is, $(m_1 - m_2)x = b_2 - b_1$. This is a linear equation in $x$ of the form $ax = b$, and the lesson on <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-equations">linear equations</TopicLink> showed it has exactly one solution if $m_1 - m_2 \neq 0$, none if $m_1 = m_2$ and $b_2 - b_1 \neq 0$, and every $x$ as a solution if both are zero. When there is exactly one $x$, there is exactly one meeting point $(x, m_1 x + b_1)$.

</Proof>

Vertical lines $x = c$ are not of the form $y = mx + b$, but they are easy: two different vertical lines are parallel, and a vertical line meets every non-vertical line exactly once. The next theorem covers all lines at once, vertical or not, and in fact all linear equations.

<Theorem name="two solutions give infinitely many">

If two different points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ both satisfy a linear equation $ax + by = e$, then for every number $t$ so does the point

$$
P_t = \big((1 - t)x_1 + t x_2,\; (1 - t)y_1 + t y_2\big),
$$

and different values of $t$ give different points.

</Theorem>

<Proof>

Substitute $P_t$ and regroup:

$$
a\big((1 - t)x_1 + t x_2\big) + b\big((1 - t)y_1 + t y_2\big) = (1 - t)(a x_1 + b y_1) + t(a x_2 + b y_2) = (1 - t)e + te = e.
$$

If $P_s = P_t$ with $s \neq t$, then subtracting coordinates gives $(s - t)(x_2 - x_1) = 0$ and $(s - t)(y_2 - y_1) = 0$, so $x_1 = x_2$ and $y_1 = y_2$, contradicting that the points are different.

</Proof>

The points $P_t$ make up the line through the two points ($P_0$ is the first point, $P_1$ the second, $P_{1/2}$ the midpoint). Now apply the theorem to every equation of a system at once: if a system of linear equations — with any number of equations — has two different solutions, every $P_t$ solves all of them. So:

**A system of linear equations has exactly one solution, no solution, or infinitely many.** Never exactly two, or three.

<Note>

**Going further: the general formula.** For the system $ax + by = e$, $cx + dy = f$, multiply the first equation by $d$, the second by $b$, and subtract: the $y$-terms cancel and $(ad - bc)\,x = ed - bf$. Eliminating $x$ in the same way gives $(ad - bc)\,y = af - ec$. So if the number $ad - bc$ is not zero, the system has exactly one solution,

$$
x = \frac{ed - bf}{ad - bc}, \qquad y = \frac{af - ec}{ad - bc}.
$$

The number $ad - bc$ is called the **determinant** of the system; when both equations describe genuine lines, it is zero exactly when the two lines are parallel or the same. You will meet it again in <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">linear algebra</TopicLink>.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**Slope as "run over rise".** Slope is $\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$: change in $y$ divided by change in $x$. Upside down, the line through $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 3)$ would get slope $\tfrac{1}{3}$ — but it climbs $3$ for every $1$ across, so its slope is $3$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Every straight-line relationship is proportional."** Only lines through the origin are. The taxi fare $y = 2x + 5$ is linear, but $2$ km costs $9$ soles while $4$ km costs $13$, not $18$. Doubling the input doubles the output only when $b = 0$.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A vertical line has slope zero."** A *horizontal* line has slope $0$: its rise is $0$. A vertical line has run $0$, and division by $0$ is undefined, so a vertical line has **no** slope. (Its equation is $x = c$, which is not of the form $y = mx + b$.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Mixing the order of subtraction.** $\dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_1 - x_2}$ gives the wrong sign. Either order of the points is fine, but use the **same** order on top and bottom.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A system of two equations always has exactly one solution."** Parallel lines give none, and two equations describing the same line give infinitely many. What can never happen is exactly two solutions.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Section id="viewpoints" kind="viewpoints" title="Other ways of seeing">

<Viewpoint lens="geometry" title="Similar triangles and parallel lines">

Slope is a geometric invariant: every slope triangle on a line has the same shape, so the ratio rise ÷ run is the same. Parallel lines are lines with the same slope, and the formula $y = mx + (y_1 - m x_1)$ shows that through any point there is **exactly one** line parallel to a given non-vertical line. That is the coordinate version of the parallel postulate of Euclid's geometry — a statement that, astonishingly, fails in the non-Euclidean geometries of the sphere and the hyperbolic plane (see <TopicLink id="math.geom.non-euclidean-geometry">non-Euclidean geometry</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="algebra" title="Elimination becomes linear algebra">

Elimination works because we may add multiples of equations to each other without changing the solutions. With many equations and many unknowns, doing this systematically is **Gaussian elimination**, and the coefficients arranged in a rectangle form a **matrix**. The number $ad - bc$ is the determinant of the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$; geometrically, it measures how the map $(x, y) \mapsto (ax + by,\, cx + dy)$ scales areas, which is why it is zero exactly when the system fails to have a unique solution. This is the beginning of <TopicLink id="math.algebra.linear-algebra">linear algebra</TopicLink>.

</Viewpoint>

<Viewpoint lens="categories" title="Linear functions compose">

Doing $x \mapsto m_2 x + b_2$ and then $x \mapsto m_1 x + b_1$ gives $x \mapsto m_1 m_2\, x + (m_1 b_2 + b_1)$ — another linear function, whose slope is the **product** of the slopes. Converting Celsius to Fahrenheit and Fahrenheit to Kelvin, for instance, composes into one linear conversion. Every linear function with $m \neq 0$ can be undone: the inverse of $x \mapsto mx + b$ is $x \mapsto \tfrac{x - b}{m}$. Linear functions with non-zero slope, under composition, are a first example of a structure that mathematicians call a group (see <TopicLink id="math.cat.chaining-functions">chaining functions</TopicLink>).

</Viewpoint>

<Section id="history" kind="history" title="History and global context">

- Chapter 8 of the Chinese *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, *Fangcheng* ("rectangular arrays"), solves systems of several linear equations in several unknowns. The coefficients were laid out as columns of counting rods on a board and combined column by column — the method we now call Gaussian elimination, more than 1,500 years before Gauss. The same chapter states rules for calculating with negative numbers, which elimination needs.
- The *Sunzi Suanjing* ("Master Sun's Mathematical Manual", China, between the 3rd and 5th centuries CE) contains the pheasants-and-rabbits problem. In Japan it later became *tsurukamezan*, the "crane and turtle calculation", still a favourite in schools.
- In the 1630s, **Pierre de Fermat** and **René Descartes** independently connected algebra with geometry through coordinates. Fermat showed that every equation of the first degree in two unknowns describes a straight line — which is why such equations are called *linear*.
- Determinants appeared in the work of **Seki Takakazu** in Japan (1683) and, independently, **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** in Germany (1693). **Gabriel Cramer** published the general formula for solving systems with determinants in 1750.
